Mudd Club

Nightclub / venue at 77 White Street, Tribeca, New York City. Open roughly October 1978 to 1983. No dedicated Discogs artist or label ID, but documented in Discogs release notes and tracklists across multiple recordings. The Mudd Club label on Discogs (label ID 422320) is referenced in Diego Cortez’s profile.

Overview

The Mudd Club was the primary social and performance hub for New York’s downtown art, punk, No Wave, and new wave milieu from its opening in late 1978 until it closed in 1983. Positioned deliberately in Tribeca — then an industrial, unfashionable neighborhood south of SoHo — it operated as a direct counter to the uptown velvet-rope culture of Studio 54 and Xenon. Entry was democratic by comparison; the people at the door were interested in weirdness and creativity rather than celebrity. The result was a convergence point for artists, musicians, filmmakers, visual artists, and writers who became the defining figures of the early-1980s downtown scene.

The name references Dr. Samuel Mudd, the physician convicted (controversially) of conspiracy in the Lincoln assassination — an oblique historical provocation consistent with the club’s anti-establishment posture.

Founding and Key Figures

Steve Mass

Co-founded the Mudd Club in 1978 alongside Diego Cortez and Anya Phillips. Discogs lists a “Steve Mass” with a 2001 active year (Discogs artist ID: 7325905) but this entry does not appear connected to the club’s founder; no substantive Discogs credits attach to him. His role is documented primarily in historical accounts of the scene rather than in release credits.

Web sources describe Mass as a filmmaker and entrepreneur. To secure the 77 White Street loft (owned by artist Ross Bleckner), he described the planned venue as an art-bar cabaret in the mold of Mickey Ruskin’s One University Place (itself modeled on Ruskin’s Max’s Kansas City). The Schirn Magazin piece describes him as a “publicist” at the time of founding; the NYU finding aid describes the Mudd Club as showcasing “intersections of popular and avant-garde performance culture, gender and sexuality, celebrity culture, music, visual art, fashion, film, and nightlife.” Mass was attentive to the economic realities of the downtown scene: he recognized that working artists had little money but constituted the scene itself, and admitted them to the club for free. After the Mudd Club closed in 1983, he pursued nonprofit work, teaching, writing, and photography in New York. In 2001 he opened Mudd Club Berlin at Grosse Hamburger Strasse 17, operating it as an intimate venue for touring bands; he went on to own and operate multiple nightclubs and restaurants in Berlin. He drafted an unpublished memoir (manuscript held in his papers). His archive — the Stephen Mass Papers (inclusive dates 1940–2019; bulk dates 1978–2009) — is held at NYU’s Fales Library and Special Collections (MSS 592), and includes planning notebooks, financial and legal documents, promotional materials, press coverage, photography, and memoir drafts. The papers also contain interview transcripts and correspondence with historian Tim Lawrence. [Sources: Wikipedia/Mudd Club (as of 2026-06-09); NYU Fales Finding Aid MSS 592 (findingaids.library.nyu.edu/fales/mss_592/); Schirn Magazin (schirn.de/en/magazine/context/new_york_downtown_and_mudd_club/); 80WSE Gallery exhibition page (80wse.org/exhibitions/mudd-club-1978-1983)]

Diego Cortez

Real name James Allan Curtis (September 30, 1946 – June 21, 2021). Discogs artist ID: 177968. Co-founded the Mudd Club with Mass and Anya Phillips in 1978. Cortez was an art curator, filmmaker, and scene organizer who had studied at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (MFA 1973) under Stan Brakhage and Nam June Paik, and had worked as a studio assistant to Dennis Oppenheim and Vito Acconci after moving to New York.

At the Mudd Club, Cortez served as the primary curatorial intelligence — programming performances, art installations, and events. He was also the person who advised Brian Eno to attend the “Bands at Artists Space” festival in spring 1978 and document what he saw, a recommendation that led directly to No New York. In 1981, he curated New York/New Wave at P.S. 1 Contemporary Art Center in Long Island City, a landmark group exhibition featuring Andy Warhol, David Byrne, William S. Burroughs, Robert Mapplethorpe, Nan Goldin, Keith Haring, Fab 5 Freddy, Futura 2000, Ann Magnuson, and Jean-Michel Basquiat — the show that catapulted Basquiat to mainstream art-world recognition. He declined an offer to manage Madonna in 1982.

Cortez directed music videos for Talking Heads and Blondie, and in 1978 served as Brian Eno’s production advisor on the No New York compilation. His archive — the No Wave Collection (1972–1981) and Mudd Club Collection (c. 1977–1979) — is held at Cornell University. He died of kidney failure in Burlington, North Carolina, aged 74.

His own Discogs release credits center on his early-1980s musical work and curatorial outputs:

  • American Narrative/Story Art: 1967–1977 (1978, US) — curatorial/compilation credit
  • You & You (1978, US)
  • That’s When Your Heartaches Begin (from the Grutzi Elvis soundtrack, 1979, UK/France) — a release connected to his film work, featuring the theme and other pieces

Anya Phillips

See full page: Anya Phillips. Born February 1955, Taiwan — died June 19, 1981, Valhalla, New York, aged 26. Discogs artist ID: 259198. Phillips co-conceived the Mudd Club — she originally envisioned it as an elegant space she called the Molotov Cocktail Lounge — and was intended to manage it in its early period. According to Wikipedia and scene accounts, her day-to-day operational involvement ended after a dispute with Mass; the exact nature and timing of that dispute is not pinned to a primary source. She was simultaneously the manager and romantic partner of James Chance (The Contortions, James White & The Blacks). She died of brain cancer roughly two and a half years after the club opened; her loss removed one of its primary creative architects. [Source: Wikipedia/Anya Phillips (as of 2026-06-09); faroutmagazine.co.uk/anya-phillips-forgotten-hero-of-new-yorks-no-wave-movement/]

Programming and Scene

The Mudd Club hosted performances across a deliberately wide range — No Wave, punk, new wave, funk, dance music, avant-garde, and performance art. Its programming philosophy, shaped heavily by Cortez in the early years, treated music and visual art as continuous. The club occupied multiple floors: the basement was a dance floor, the main floor held the stage, and upper floors were used for gallery-style art exhibitions and theme nights curated by figures including Jean-Michel Basquiat, Kenny Scharf, and Keith Haring.

The club’s programming extended beyond music. It was described as the first nightclub in New York to function simultaneously as a gallery. Keith Haring curated a late-night gallery on the fourth floor, mounting an exhibition of approximately 100 artists — mostly on the fringes of the establishment art world — followed by shows focused on photography and graffiti art. Mass reportedly paid Haring to frequent the club, making him an embedded presence. Themed parties (blond nights, monster parties, 1960s revivals) were a programming staple. The club also hosted film screenings and fashion shows — including runway shows for designers Anna Sui and Jasper Conran. Literary figures Allen Ginsberg and William S. Burroughs appeared at events. The Schirn Magazin summary characterizes the club’s cultural ethos as “poor chic”: it welcomed local artists who had been priced out of SoHo galleries during New York’s fiscal crisis period, treating their presence as the value proposition rather than celebrity headlining. Andy Warhol’s oft-quoted observation about the club: “At the Mudd Club mistakes could pass as experiments.” [Sources: Wikipedia/Mudd Club (as of 2026-06-09); schirn.de/en/magazine/context/new_york_downtown_and_mudd_club/]

Regular Performers

  • James White & The Blacks / The Contortions — James Chance’s groups were the club’s most emblematic acts; their confrontational funk-punk was central to the venue’s identity
  • Lydia Lunch — Teenage Jesus And The Jerks and her subsequent projects performed regularly; she was among the most visible No Wave figures at the club
  • Klaus Nomi — the German countertenor (real name Klaus Sperber; Discogs artist ID: 73282) made the Mudd Club a central venue for his early New York career. Wikipedia’s Nomi article places his Mudd Club performances across the late 1970s as part of a circuit including Club 57 and the Pyramid Club. One specific documented moment: Nomi and Joey Arias were at the Mudd Club when David Bowie introduced himself to them, leading to their appearance as Bowie’s backing performers on Saturday Night Live on December 15, 1979 — one of the most-cited events in Nomi’s career. A setlist.fm entry documents a Mudd Club performance on April 28, 1980. No dedicated live recording of his Mudd Club sets has been found in Discogs. [Sources: Wikipedia/Klaus Nomi (as of 2026-06-09); setlist.fm]
  • Jean-Michel Basquiat’s band Gray (2) — Basquiat’s noise-art band Gray performed at the Mudd Club regularly; after a performance there in the summer of 1981, Basquiat departed the band to focus on painting. [Source: Wikipedia/Gray_(band) (as of 2026-06-09)]
  • The B-52s — described in the Schirn Magazin account as the club’s “unofficial house band.” [Source: schirn.de/en/magazine/context/new_york_downtown_and_mudd_club/]
  • Judas Priest — performed at the Mudd Club on March 11, 1979 during their Killing Machine tour. The recording circulated among fans for decades and was officially released as part of the 50 Heavy Metal Years of Music box set (October 2021); individual tracks were also released to streaming services. The show illustrates the club’s programming breadth — a British heavy metal act performing in a venue otherwise associated with No Wave and art-punk. [Sources: setlist.fm; wolfgangs.com; Legacy Recordings press release]
  • Kid Creole and the Coconuts (Kid Creole And The Coconuts, Discogs ID: 32620) — the ZE Records act led by August Darnell performed at the club; their connection to the downtown scene ran through ZE’s roster and the shared social network
  • Elliott Sharp (Discogs artist ID: 63308) — his 1981 live cassette Moving Info (Zoar Records, ZCS 3; Discogs ID: 3666486) is documented in release notes as “Recorded live at Mudd Club and Hurrah in 1981,” with a band featuring Bill Laswell (bass), Percy Jones (bass), Phillip Wilson (drums), Art Baron (trombone), and Olu Dara (trumpet) — a document of the club’s overlap with the downtown jazz-improv scene

Notable Attendees

Jean-Michel Basquiat (described by Schirn Magazin as a “regular and virtual resident”), Keith Haring (paid by Mass to frequent the club), Andy Warhol, Debbie Harry, David Bowie, William S. Burroughs, Ann Magnuson, Robert Mapplethorpe, Nan Goldin, David Byrne, Jim Jarmusch, Madonna, and Lou Reed are among the figures consistently cited as Mudd Club regulars in scene accounts. [Sources: Wikipedia/Mudd Club (as of 2026-06-09); schirn.de/en/magazine/context/new_york_downtown_and_mudd_club/]

ZE Records Overlap

The Mudd Club served as a social node for ZE Records artists. The label’s roster — James White & The Blacks, Kid Creole And The Coconuts, Was (Not Was), Cristina, Suicide — reflected the same downtown synthesis of punk, funk, disco, and art practice that the club embodied. Michael Zilkha and Michel Esteban’s label grew partly out of connections formed in and around the club.

Other Mudd Club-Related Discogs Appearances

  • Frank Zappa, You Are What You Is (Barking Pumpkin / Epic, 1981; Discogs ID: 1564942) — contains a track titled “Mudd Club” (side D, track 2); this is a studio composition referencing the venue rather than a live recording there
  • Frank Zappa, Zappa ‘80 Mudd Club (Zappa Records, 2023; master ID: 3038627; Discogs ID: 26596082) — official posthumous live release documenting a Zappa performance at the Mudd Club on May 8, 1980. The show was sandwiched between much larger arena dates (Cincinnati, Philadelphia, Nassau Coliseum). The venue had a capacity of approximately 240; the 15-song set ran roughly one hour. The release is part of a 3CD set (Zappa ‘80 Mudd Club/Munich) that also documents a Munich Olympiahalle show from July 3, 1980; a standalone Mudd Club 2LP was also issued. Band: Frank Zappa (lead guitar, vocals), Ike Willis (vocals, guitar), Ray White (vocals, guitar), Arthur Barrow (bass), Tommy Mars (keyboards), David Logeman (drums) — the only Zappa touring lineup to feature Logeman, who was temporarily substituting for the regular drummer. The setlist drew heavily from the recently released 1979 albums and included early versions of material that appeared on You Are What You Is (1981), including the song “Mudd Club.” Released March 3, 2023. Closure

The Mudd Club closed in 1983, roughly five years after opening. No single reason is documented in Discogs sources. Its closure coincided with the broader shift in the downtown scene — the gentrification of Tribeca, the mainstreaming of several scene figures (Basquiat’s commercial gallery trajectory, Blondie’s mainstream success, the transition from No Wave’s confrontational posture to post-punk and dance music), and the wider cultural drift toward the mid-1980s. The club’s co-founder Anya Phillips had died in June 1981; Diego Cortez had moved toward gallery curation. Danceteria (see Danceteria) had by then emerged as a parallel and partially successor venue.

Legacy

The Mudd Club’s historical significance rests on its compression of scenes: in five years it hosted performers who defined No Wave (The Contortions, Teenage Jesus And The Jerks, Lydia Lunch), bridged that scene to broader new wave and post-punk (Klaus Nomi, Kid Creole And The Coconuts), and welcomed visual artists who crossed into cultural history (Basquiat, Haring, Warhol). Frank Zappa’s 2023 archival release Zappa ‘80 Mudd Club demonstrates the venue’s reach beyond the No Wave core — Zappa, a West Coast avant-rock figure with no ideological connection to the downtown scene, regarded it as a significant enough stop to record there.

Diego Cortez’s dual archive at Cornell (the No Wave Collection and the Mudd Club Collection) represents the most substantial institutional preservation of primary materials from the club’s operational period.
