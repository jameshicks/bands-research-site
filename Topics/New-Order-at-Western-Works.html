New Order at Western Works

On 7 September 1980 — two days after their third gig as New Order and less than four months after the death of Ian Curtis — the three surviving members of Joy Division travelled from Manchester to Sheffield and spent a day recording demos at Western Works, the private studio owned and operated by Cabaret Voltaire. The session produced six tracks, including embryonic versions of songs that would appear on New Order’s debut album Movement (1981) and their first single “Ceremony.” It is the only known occasion New Order used Western Works; no commercially released New Order record carries a Western Works studio credit. The recordings circulated as heavily bootlegged cassettes from the early 1980s onward before appearing, in clean form, as part of the Movement Definitive Edition box set in April 2019.

Background: The Sheffield–Manchester Axis

Shared bills and Factory Records

Cabaret Voltaire and Joy Division were natural counterparts in the post-punk underground: both rejected the music industry mainstream, both worked in self-built or makeshift studio situations, and both circulated through the same network of small clubs and independent labels from 1978 onwards. The relationship predated Western Works by several years.

The formal public link was Factory Records. In January 1979, Factory released A Factory Sample (FAC 2), a double 7” EP drawn from acts who had performed at Tony Wilson and Alan Erasmus’s Russell Club nights in Manchester. It featured Joy Division, The Durutti Column, comedian John Dowie, and — as the Sheffield contingent — Cabaret Voltaire, who contributed “Baader Meinhof” and “Sex in Secret.” CV had appeared regularly at the Factory nights and were prominent enough to earn a spot on the label’s inaugural vinyl release, even though they would not sign to Factory as a full label act — Rough Trade had already secured them by offering a four-track Revox tape machine as an advance. (Stephen Mallinder later commented that if Tony Wilson had been able to help out at that point it might have been different.) [source: Cabaret Voltaire Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabaret_Voltaire_(band)]

Live connections

Joy Division and Cabaret Voltaire shared multiple concert bills during 1978–1979. Confirmed dates include:

  • 20 October 1978: The Factory I (Russell Club), Manchester — Joy Division, Cabaret Voltaire, The Tiller Boys
  • 16 February 1979: Eric’s, Liverpool — Joy Division and Cabaret Voltaire
  • 8 September 1979: Futurama Festival, Queens Hall, Leeds — both bands on the same bill, alongside PiL and OMD

Mallinder subsequently described their relationship as a reciprocal touring arrangement: when two bands shared a booking, the one with the longer drive played first so they could leave earlier. [source: search result aggregating interview quotes]

The planned Joy Division session

Several sources indicate that a Joy Division recording session at Western Works had been arranged before Ian Curtis’s death. The Cabs’ own space was an obvious practical attraction: Martin Hannett, Joy Division’s regular producer, ran tightly controlled and often expensive sessions at commercial studios; Western Works offered access to a self-run environment without an external producer’s interference. The precise terms or intended scope of the planned session are unconfirmed — no primary source (memoir, interview) has been located that specifies what Joy Division intended to record there. Curtis died on 18 May 1980, the night before Joy Division were due to begin their first North American tour. The Western Works session that had been floated never happened. [sources: multiple fansite/blog summaries drawing on circulating documentation; see thepowerofindependenttrucking.blogspot.com, 2009]

Why Western Works after Curtis’s death

When the three survivors — Bernard Sumner, Peter Hook, and Stephen Morris — decided to continue as a new band, the Cabaret Voltaire invitation was still open. Taking up that offer served several purposes: it placed them away from the Manchester spotlight during an extremely raw transitional period; it put them in a self-contained environment without a producer’s agenda; and it maintained the existing relationship between the two groups. There is no documented evidence that Factory Records, Tony Wilson, or Rob Gretton facilitated the booking — it appears to have been a direct arrangement between the bands. The session took place on 7 September 1980, two days after New Order’s third post-Curtis performance. [sources: multiple; primary consolidation at thefatangelsings.com/2021/05/19/new-order-western-works/]

The third gig: Scamps, Blackpool, 5 September 1980

New Order’s third ever performance — two days before the Western Works session — took place at Scamps, Blackpool, on 5 September 1980. The show was booked by Section 25, who headlined; New Order were the supporting act and were billed on the poster as “New Orders” (a misspelling). The audience recording was taped by Larry Cassidy of Section 25. The setlist ran approximately 32 minutes: “Dreams Never End,” “Truth,” “Mesh,” “Homage,” “Sister Ray” (Velvet Underground cover), “Cries and Whispers,” and “Ceremony.” At this point the band was still a three-piece; Gillian Gilbert had not yet joined. [sources: archive.org recording entry https://archive.org/details/NewOrder_Live_at_Scamps_Blackpool_England_September_5_1980_AUD; Manchester Digital Music Archive poster https://www.mdmarchive.co.uk/artefact/4025/New-Order-Poster-1980]

What the session was not

This was a private demo session, not a commercial production. No Factory Records catalogue number was assigned. No Discogs release entry carries a Western Works recording credit on any New Order release — the Western Works trawl on the Discogs database (conducted 2026-06-05) returned zero matches across release notes, release_company entries, and shared artist credits between New Order (ID 3909) and any CV member (IDs 2238, 24050, 76866). The six tracks were known only through bootleg cassettes for nearly three decades. A 1/4” reel of tape containing the session surfaced on eBay in 2009, described in its listing as something other than what it was; collectors recognised it and it circulated in significantly better quality from that point. [source: dothebrokenrecords.wordpress.com/2009/05/13/new-order-western-works-demos-7-sept-1980/]

Official release: Movement Definitive Edition (2019)

The Western Works session was officially released for the first time as part of the Movement Definitive Edition box set, released 5 April 2019 on Warner/Rhino. The bonus disc includes both the Western Works demos and a separate set of Cargo Studios demos (recorded in Rochdale, January 1981).

Sonic Impact

No producer: freedom and rawness

The most consistent observation across all secondary sources discussing these recordings is the absence of Martin Hannett. Joy Division’s sound had been profoundly shaped by Hannett’s production — his dense, reverberant, often alienating sonic architecture. At Western Works, with only Watson engineering and no producer imposing a framework, the three musicians were entirely self-directed. The results are audibly raw: the production is sparse, the arrangements provisional, and the vocal performances exploratory. Different members trying different lead vocal roles on different songs documents the band’s uncertainty about what kind of group they were becoming.

The NewOrderTracks blog notes that “Truth” incorporates early drum machine programming and “departs significantly from Joy Division’s sound,” while “Are You Ready For This?” with its bossanova Dr Rhythm programming is “totally like Cabaret Voltaire.” This is plausible given the context: with CV equipment around them and CV’s Chris Watson at the desk, the sonic palette was necessarily inflected by Sheffield’s industrial-electronic environment rather than Hannett’s Manchester aesthetic.

The Sheffield-Manchester difference

Bernard Sumner stated in a September 2012 interview with Max Dax for Telekom Electronic Beats: “Once I had understood that you could make music without guitars, I felt like there were no longer any borders.” The full passage contextualises it alongside Kraftwerk, the Human League, and OMD as formative influences on his shift toward electronics: “By the time I wrote ‘Blue Monday’, I had already done some gigs with Cabaret Voltaire as well.” [source: Max Dax interviews Bernard Sumner, Telekom Electronic Beats, 2 September 2012, https://www.electronicbeats.net/max-dax-interviews-bernard-sumner] The Western Works session precedes New Order’s full electronic turn by approximately two years (their synthesiser-led approach crystallises on “Everything’s Gone Green” in late 1981 and fully on Power, Corruption & Lies in 1983). There is no documented claim, from any band member, that the Western Works session was a direct catalyst for the later electronic shift. What is documentable is that the session placed them in the physical environment — the studio of the Sheffield band who had done most to demonstrate that electronics could replace guitars — at precisely the moment when they were forming their musical identity.

The ambient context of Western Works is relevant here: the studio was housed in a derelict Victorian cutlery factory, surrounded by Sheffield’s collapsing heavy-industrial infrastructure. Richard H. Kirk described it as “like our own version of Warhol’s Factory.” [source: DJ Mag interview, https://djmag.com/node/4152] The Noise Heat Power tribute essay (2014) characterises CV’s records from Western Works as seeming “to both condense and disperse the escaping sonic effluvia of steel production” — the sense that the building’s industrial context was an active part of the sound. Whether this atmosphere consciously registered with New Order, or whether it contributed unconsciously to the drum-machine experiments evident on “Truth” and “Are You Ready For This?”, is unverifiable from existing sources.

The demos in context

The Western Works demos are embryonic, not transformative. “Dreams Never End” and “Truth” survive largely intact into Movement; “Ceremony” — the most emotionally charged track — was already fully formed. The session’s value is primarily historical: it documents three men finding out what they could do without their front man and without their producer, in a space lent to them by friends, three months into an unprecedented crisis. As the Wilfully Obscure blog characterised it: “New Order in their most sullen and undiluted form… mourning through music.” [source: wilfullyobscure.blogspot.com/2018/04/new-order-western-works-studio-demos.html]

Web sources (added 2026-08-12): Planned Joy Division session — primary-source audit

The question

Whether the Western Works arrangement was a formal booking (dates confirmed, studio reserved) made while Ian Curtis was alive, or only an informal discussion that had not been formalised before his death on 18 May 1980.

What online sources show

The originating secondary claim. The most specific statement appears in The Power of Independent Trucking blog (April 2009): “At some point, JD was going to work with the Cabs in the Cabs’ own Western Works Studio in Sheffield, but this opportunity had not yet come to pass at the time of Ian’s death. Suddenly with no lead singer and a wide-open new beginning, the survivors (now known as New Order) took the Cabs up on their offer and decamped to Western Works on 7 September 1980.” [source: https://thepowerofindependenttrucking.blogspot.com/2009/04/homage-new-order-western-works-980.html] The blog cites no primary source for the pre-Curtis arrangement; no interview, memoir page, or contemporary document is named. This is an unsourced fan-blog claim.

Hook’s Substance (2016). The only snippet accessible via Google Books reads: “went into Western Works, Cabaret Voltaire’s studios in Sheffield, and recorded and wrote a couple of tracks with them, one featuring Rob on vocals.” This refers to the September 1980 New Order session — it says nothing about a pre-Curtis Joy Division booking. No online excerpt, review, or interview citing Substance contains a passage about a Joy Division session at Western Works being formally booked before Curtis’s death. [source: https://books.google.com/books?id=WR7ABQAAQBAJ]

Sumner’s Chapter and Verse (2014). No online excerpt, Google Books snippet, or review of Chapter and Verse contains any passage about Western Works at all. The book’s coverage of the post-Curtis transitional period is described in reviews as focusing on Curtis’s death and the decision to continue, not on specific early sessions. [source: https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/24464160-chapter-and-verse; https://www.loudersound.com/reviews/bernard-sumner-chapter-and-verse-new-order-joy-division-and-me]

Cabaret Voltaire member interviews. Three Mallinder sources were checked: the Quietus interview (“No Lectures from Western Works,” 2014 — 403 error); the Red Bull Music Academy lecture (returned only CSS); and The Ransom Note interview. None discusses a pre-Curtis Joy Division session at Western Works. The only online Mallinder statement about Joy Division and Western Works is the boilerplate: “Their Sheffield studio, Western Works, gave the remaining members of Joy Division a place to develop their first songs as New Order.” No Richard H. Kirk interview available online addresses the subject. No Martin Hannett interview found online mentions Western Works.

Contemporaneous press. The NME, 29 November 1980, describes the session simply as: “recently they all did some exploratory work with New Order at Western Works.” No suggestion of a pre-existing booking for Joy Division; the framing treats the arrangement as belonging to New Order, not as the fulfilment of a Joy Division plan. [source: https://www.brainwashed.com/common/htdocs/publications/cv-nme_19801129.php?site=cv]

The Joy Division / Cabaret Voltaire pre-Curtis connection that does exist. Joy Division and Cabaret Voltaire had confirmed a joint North American tour for May–June 1980, organised by Rough Trade. Ian Curtis mentioned it in a BBC Blackburn interview: “I think we’re going with Cabaret Voltaire.” Curtis died on 18 May 1980, the night before the tour was due to begin. This tour connection is the documented, primary-source-backed pre-Curtis arrangement between the two bands — not a studio session. There is a risk that the fan-blog claim about a “planned Western Works session” is a confusion or extrapolation from this tour arrangement. [source: Ian Curtis Wikipedia article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Curtis]

Conclusion

No primary source — no page from Hook’s Substance, no page from Sumner’s Chapter and Verse, no Cabaret Voltaire member interview, no contemporaneous press account — has been located online that describes a Joy Division session at Western Works as formally booked (dates set, studio reserved) before Curtis’s death. The claim that “JD was going to work with the Cabs at Western Works” is present only in unsourced fan-blog writing (2009). The physical memoirs remain the only likely location for a definitive primary-source answer. The Research Queue item above should be addressed by reading Substance (chapters covering late 1980) and Chapter and Verse (chapter covering post-Curtis transition) in physical or ebook form.
