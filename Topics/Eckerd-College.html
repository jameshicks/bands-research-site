Oct 10, 20269 min read

Eckerd College

Private liberal arts college. St. Petersburg, Florida (Boca Ciega Bay waterfront campus). Founded 1958 (as Florida Presbyterian College); renamed Eckerd College 1972.

An unlikely feeder institution for the New York No Wave scene: at least five figures central to the movement attended Eckerd in the early-to-mid 1970s, including members of two of the four bands on No New York (1978). The connection runs through the college’s arts and theater programs and the countercultural atmosphere of a small residential campus in the early 1970s — and through specific friendships that translated directly into band formation.

Institutional History

Founded as Florida Presbyterian College in 1958 under joint sponsorship of the United Presbyterian Church and the Presbyterian Church U.S.A.; opened 1960 on a 170-acre waterfront site on Boca Ciega Bay donated by the city of St. Petersburg (with temporary early quarters at a former WWII maritime base at Bayboro Harbor). Small, residential, liberal arts-focused.

In 1971, drugstore magnate Jack Eckerd donated $12.5 million — the institution’s name was changed to Eckerd College in 1972. The visual arts program had been active from the early 1960s; a theater building (Bininger Theatre) and music center (Roberts Music Center) were on campus by the early 1970s.

The No Wave Pipeline

The 1974 Cohort

Three figures relocated from Eckerd to New York together in 1974 — the year of the college’s renaming:

Lindsay graduated Eckerd in 1974. Cunningham studied theater there; he is originally from New Jersey. Burg grew up in Ohio and met both at college. All three moved to New York together that year — Lindsay going on to form DNA with Tim Wright and Ikue Mori, while Cunningham and Burg (alongside Sumner Crane and Nancy Arlen) formed what became Mars.

The band that became Mars was originally called China — giving Connie Burg her stage name “China Burg.” It was renamed Mars in 1977 when a commercial act claimed the name.

The Later Cohort

Bobby Swope (aka Bobby Berkowitz Swope) and his sister Liz Swope also attended Eckerd — a later cohort, date unconfirmed. Bobby is described in a contemporary review as “a migrant from Florida’s Eckerd College.” Both became members of Beirut Slump (1978–1979), Lydia Lunch’s first band, alongside Jim Sclavunos and Vivienne Dick.

Campus Atmosphere (1970–1974): What Drew Them Toward Experimental Work

The clearest primary-source account comes from Mark Cunningham himself, in the Mars chronology published at fromthearchives.com. He describes Eckerd as “a freak school in St. Petersburg, Florida that was probably like a lot of other arty type schools of the time.” On his first day he met Arto Lindsay — “this strange kinda nerdy looking kid from Virginia who had grown up in Brazil” — and they immediately roomed together for all four years. The two “started jamming” with other “music and word freaks” on campus. Cunningham describes the formative aesthetic clearly: “We had no technique and no rules, worshipped the beats and Miles, Warhol and the Velvets.” (fromthearchives.com/cm/chronology.html, accessed 2026-10-09)

In a separate interview (Perfect Sound Forever, exact date unknown), Lindsay says only: “I came back to college in 1970 and then I moved to New York four years later” — and in the same piece mentions that at the art school on 8th Street in New York (after Eckerd) he encountered Morton Feldman. He does not discuss Eckerd faculty or programs in detail in any interview found. (furious.com/perfect/artolindsay.html, accessed 2026-10-09)

The BOMB Magazine interview with Lindsay confirms he roomed with Cunningham in college and was close to the future Mars members from that time, but provides no institutional detail. (bombmagazine.org/articles/arto-lindsay/, accessed 2026-10-09)

What the sources do and do not support

Supported by primary sources:

  • The college attracted students already inclined toward countercultural and experimental interests; Cunningham’s “freak school” characterization suggests a self-selecting bohemian population rather than a formal avant-garde curriculum
  • The Beat writers, Miles Davis, Warhol, and the Velvet Underground were the shared touchstones — not formal academic training or specific faculty
  • Friendships formed in dormitory life (Cunningham and Lindsay roomed together for four years) were the operative mechanism, not coursework
  • The physical arts infrastructure was growing: studio space moved to pre-fabricated huts near Bininger Theatre and Roberts Music Center in 1971; the Ransom Arts Center was under construction from 1972; the Frances Cady Elliott Gallery opened 1973 (eckerd.edu/visualarts/history/, accessed 2026-10-09)

Not supported / not found in web sources:

  • No specific faculty member at Eckerd has been identified in any interview or secondary source as a formative influence on the No Wave cohort
  • No visiting artist or artist-in-residence program at Eckerd during this period appears in any source checked
  • No formal avant-garde or experimental music curriculum has been documented for this era
  • The influences named (Beats, Miles, Warhol, Velvets) are all external to the college — absorbed from records, books, and general cultural exposure, not from Eckerd programming

Tentative interpretation (from sources, not speculation): Cunningham’s framing — “probably like a lot of other arty type schools of the time” — suggests the college’s role was permissive rather than directive: a small, residential, liberal-arts environment with loosely countercultural norms that allowed like-minded students to find each other and self-organize. The experimental impulse arrived with the students (Lindsay already had Brazilian tropicália and bossa nova in his formation; Cunningham came drawn to free jazz), not from institutional programming.

The Connection in Context

The Eckerd pipeline accounts for members of two of the four bands on No New York (DNA and Mars). By contrast, the other two No New York bands — The Contortions (James Chance, from Milwaukee) and Teenage Jesus & The Jerks (Lydia Lunch, from Rochester) — had no Eckerd connection.

What Eckerd offered these figures was less a formal music education than a residential arts community outside New York: a place to find collaborators and develop unconventional sensibilities before arriving in the city. Cunningham studied theater; Lindsay’s musical formation was Brazilian (missionary parents) rather than institutional. The college appears to have functioned as a gathering point rather than a training ground.

The Eckerd-to-No Wave arc also connects to the Lust-Unlust Music world through the Swopes — placing Eckerd alumni across at least three of the No Wave documentary records: No New York, the Beirut Slump 7” on Migraine, and by extension the Lust/Unlust catalog.

The structural pattern: Eckerd alumni did not form a scene with each other in Florida — they converged in NYC in small groups (the 1974 trio; then Stevenson; then the Swopes) and attached to separate but overlapping No Wave orbits. The college was a point of departure, not of artistic formation.

Mark Pauline / SRL — Web Research Note

Mark Pauline (b. 1953, Sarasota, FL) graduated Eckerd College in 1977 with a degree in visual arts — three years after the Lindsay/Cunningham/Burg cohort departed (1974). No campus-era overlap with the No Wave figures is possible on this timeline. Pauline discovered London’s punk scene during a study-abroad period while at Eckerd, then founded Survival Research Laboratories in San Francisco in 1978. SRL’s documented scene connections run through SF/industrial figures (Monte Cazazza, Graeme Revell), not New York No Wave. No interview, biography, or secondary source found links Pauline to any Eckerd-to-No Wave network. The Eckerd connection appears coincidental: two separate avant-garde currents drawing independently from the same small college at different times.
