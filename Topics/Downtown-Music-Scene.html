Downtown Music Scene

Location: Lower Manhattan — Greenwich Village, East Village, SoHo, TriBeCa
Period: c.1960–present (peak concentration 1975–1990)
Related Topics: No Wave, minimalism, post-minimalism, loft jazz

Overview

“Downtown music” names a cultural geography as much as a style: the experimental music, performance art, and avant-garde work that emerged from the lofts and art spaces of lower Manhattan, defined primarily by what it rejected — the institutional conventions of uptown classical music (Lincoln Center, Juilliard, Carnegie Hall) and the commercially defined conventions of pop. The scene was not a single movement but an intersection of several overlapping traditions: minimalism and its aftermath, free improvisation, text-sound and electronic opera, performance art, No Wave punk, and the dance-music underground. What held them together was geography, density, cheap rents, and the willingness of participants to cross genre lines freely.

The term “downtown” entered critical usage in the early 1970s and became standard shorthand by the 1980s. It has never been tidy — La Monte Young and Glenn Branca share a label but not much else — but the scene produced several figures who are now recognized as among the most important American composers and artists of the 20th century, most of them recognized only belatedly.

Roots: La Monte Young and the Minimalist Origin (1960–1970)

The most direct genealogical source is La Monte Young (b. 1935, ID 32203). Young arrived in New York in the early 1960s and immediately began curating concerts at Yoko Ono’s loft at 112 Chambers Street — the moment conventionally cited as the opening of “downtown” as a cultural space. He had met Terry Riley (b. 1935) at Berkeley and collaborated with him; both became the first generation of American minimalists, alongside Steve Reich (b. 1936, ID 22946) and Philip Glass (b. 1937).

Young’s work in New York centered on the Theatre of Eternal Music — an ensemble that at various points included John Cale, Tony Conrad, Angus MacLise, and Marian Zazeela. Young’s approach — sustained tones, microtonal tuning, extreme duration — was the origin point for drone music. He was also connected to Fluxus (performed in their concerts) and published An Anthology (1963) with Jackson Mac Low, collecting printed works by radical artists of the time.

Steve Reich’s formative work: phase-shifting (tapes of “It’s Gonna Rain,” 1965; Violin Phase, 1967; Piano Phase, 1967), then movement into more elaborate ensemble writing (Music for 18 Musicians, 1976). Trained at Juilliard then Mills College (Berio, Milhaud). His mother was Broadway singer/lyricist June Carroll.

Philip Glass studied at Juilliard and with Nadia Boulanger in Paris; collaborated with Ravi Shankar (Indian rhythmic structures informed his early work). Einstein on the Beach (1976, with Robert Wilson) was the movement’s landmark theatrical work.

By the mid-1970s Glass and Reich had moved toward larger concert presentations and European touring — the “uptown” absorption of minimalism. La Monte Young remained a more intractable figure. The downtown scene that emerged around 1975–1980 was partly defined against the minimalists’ institutional success.

The Experimental Opera / Text-Sound Strand

Running parallel to but distinct from Glass-and-Reich minimalism: the tradition centered on Robert Ashley (1930–2014, ID 32210). Ashley studied at the University of Michigan (where he worked in speech research laboratories, psychoacoustics and cultural speech patterns) and the Manhattan School of Music. He co-founded the ONCE Group and the Sonic Arts Union — both Ann Arbor/New York collectives committed to electronic music and the integration of speech, text, and music.

Ashley’s central project was Perfect Lives — a seven-part opera for television composed and recorded 1977–1983, which he called “an opera for television” and which The Kitchen commissioned. The “score” — called “music beds” in TV production terms — was largely created by Peter Gordon (who “spent hundreds of hours in a 24-track analog studio with Ashley discovering new sounds and processes”) and pianist Blue Gene Tyranny (born Robert Nathan Sheff, 1945–2020, ID 294084). Tyranny was the pianist on the majority of Ashley’s recordings and a central figure in the Ann Arbor / Lovely Music axis. Both were distributed through Lovely Music — the label run by Ashley’s collaborator circle.

Jill Kroesen (ID 294085) — no-wave musician, composer, and performance/video artist, who had studied at Mills College — appears throughout this strand: on LOLO records, on Ashley’s Perfect Lives performances, and on the Sleeping Bag Records 24→24 Music (Dinosaur L, 1981). She is one of the recurring figures connecting the Ashley/text-sound world to the No Wave and downtown dance communities.

The Kitchen

The Kitchen (267642 in Discogs — minimal catalog presence; primarily a venue/institution) was the central institutional space of the downtown scene. Located initially at 59 Wooster Street (SoHo), later at 512 West 19th Street. Founded 1971 by video artists Steina and Woody Vasulka as a space for video, music, and performance.

Rhys Chatham served as Music Director 1971–1980 — a decade-long institutional role through which he shaped the programming that made The Kitchen the hub of the avant-garde: minimalists, no-wavers, performance artists, all in the same space. This position is what allowed Chatham to bridge minimalism (he studied with La Monte Young and Morton Subotnick) with the electric guitar ensemble work that would follow.

Key commissions from The Kitchen: Robert Ashley’s Perfect Lives; premieres of work by Philip Glass, Meredith Monk, Laurie Anderson, and many others. The institution is still active.

The Guitar Ensemble Strand: Chatham and Branca

Two composers brought minimalist processes to massed electric guitars — connecting the experimental tradition to punk rock’s physical energy:

Rhys Chatham (b. 1952, NYC): Guitar Trio (1977) — three electric guitars, electric bass, drums; first performed at Max’s Kansas City with Glenn Branca, Nina Canal, David Rosenblum, Wharton Tiers. Also Tone Death (1977), a 35-minute piece for saxophone, two guitars, and drums, first performed at Franklin Furnace with Peter Gordon on saxophone. Later: An Angel Moves Too Fast to See (1989) for 100 guitars; Crimson Grail (2005) for 400 guitars.

Glenn Branca (1948–2018, ID 55972): arrived in New York 1976; formed Theoretical Girls (no-wave group) before moving to guitar symphonies. The Ascension (99 Records, 1981) — the key transitional record between no-wave and the guitar ensemble work. Founded Neutral Records c.1982 with Josh Baer. Both Thurston Moore and Lee Ranaldo of Sonic Youth played in Branca’s guitar ensemble before forming Sonic Youth — the direct genealogical line from the scene to 1980s indie rock.

Meredith Monk

Meredith Monk (b. November 20, 1942, NYC, ID 245341) — composer, performer, director; voice as primary instrument and compositional medium. Her work integrates extended vocal technique, movement, film, and installation across what she calls “interdisciplinary performance.” Rooted in Judson Dance Theater and the Judson Church circle of the 1960s. Recorded primarily for ECM New Series from the 1980s onward (Dolmen Music, 1981; Turtle Dreams, etc.) — a rare example of a downtown figure institutionalized within the European new-music world. Julius Eastman appeared on Dolmen Music (ECM, 1981) — voice and percussion — connecting the two strands.

Julius Eastman

Julius Eastman (1940–1990, ID 343937) — “Afro-American new music composer, pianist and vocalist.” Member of the Creative Associates at SUNY Buffalo and the S.E.M. Ensemble. One of the most significant and most overlooked American composers of the 20th century during his lifetime. Died May 28, 1990 in Buffalo — homeless, of AIDS-related illness, age 49. His scores were lost when his possessions were evicted from his apartment.

Eastman occupied an unusual position: trained within the academic new music world (SUNY Buffalo, working with Morton Feldman and the Creative Associates) but emphatically personal and political in his work. His compositions — including Femenine (c.1974), Gay Guerrilla, Evil Nigger, Crazy Nigger — used deliberately provocative titles as artistic statements. He performed and recorded widely: voiced Peter Maxwell Davies’s Eight Songs For A Mad King (Unicorn, 1971); appeared on Morton Feldman’s Rothko Chapel (Columbia, 1976); played keyboards and vocals on Dinosaur L’s 24→24 Music (Sleeping Bag, 1981); conducted Arthur Russell’s Tower Of Meaning (Chatham Square, 1983); sang on Peter Gordon’s Leningrad Xpress (New Tone, 1990) — likely among his final studio recordings.

Posthumous reassessment began with Unjust Malaise (New World Records, 2005); accelerated through New Amsterdam Records’ ongoing Julius Eastman series (Femenine Vol. 1, 2021; Joy Boy Vol. 2, 2022) and Frozen Reeds / Sub Rosa reissues.

No Wave

The most visceral branch of the downtown scene: deliberately anti-musical, anti-commercial punk that rejected both New Wave’s pop ambitions and the formalism of the experimental tradition. c.1976–1983. See No Wave for full coverage.

The canonical document: No New York (Antilles AN 7067, 1978) — produced by Brian Eno, cover designed by Eno and Steve Keister. Four bands:

Peter Gordon and the Dance Music Intersection

Peter Gordon and the Love of Life Orchestra represent the precise intersection where post-minimalism, No Wave, and dance music met. LOLO was founded April 1977 — before No New York, contemporary with the first Chatham guitar works — and its founding lineup (Arthur Russell, Peter Zummo, Jill Kroesen, Blue Gene Tyranny, David Van Tieghem, Scott Johnson, Ernie Brooks, Ed Friedman, Kathy Acker) is a roll call of the entire scene.

Gordon’s key insight — that disco’s groove functions like a minimalist process (sustain the foundation, vary freely on top) — produced records that crossed these worlds without belonging fully to any of them. David Byrne and Arto Lindsay played guitar on the same LOLO EP (Extended Niceties, 1980); Bill Laswell played bass on Casino (1982); Arthur Russell appeared on Innocent (CBS, 1986).

Arthur Russell is the other central downtown-to-dancefloor figure: see his own file for the full coverage. His work ranged from orchestral minimalism (Tower Of Meaning) to disco production (Kiss Me Again, 1979; Loose Joints) to country-inflected singer-songwriter material.

The Bang on a Can Generation (1987–)

The Bang on a Can festival — founded 1987 in New York by composers David Lang, Julia Wolfe, and Michael Gordon (no relation to Peter) — marks the next phase: “totalism,” a post-minimalist direction emphasizing rhythmic complexity and rock-influenced beat momentum. Bang on a Can consciously positioned itself as the downtown heir, explicitly anti-academic. It has since expanded into an annual festival (Bang on a Can All-Stars), a record label (Cantaloupe Music), and a summer institute. Further from the vault’s current coverage but the institutional continuation of the tradition.
