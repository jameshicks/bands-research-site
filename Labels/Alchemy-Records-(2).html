Overview

Alchemy Records is an Osaka-based Japanese label founded in 1984 and operated by noise musician Jojo Hiroshige. It began as a noise imprint but expanded over time to encompass psychedelic and progressive rock, building a catalogue that peaked in the 1990s with more than half its 106 selected releases concentrated in that decade. The label has spun off several subsidiaries — including Alchemy Records USA, Alchemy Video, and Good Alchemy — and its roster centres on key figures in the Japanese underground noise scene, among them Hijokaidan, Toshiji Mikawa, Masahiko Ohno, and Tetsuya Kotani.

Notes (web)

On the expansion from noise into psychedelic and progressive rock

No source found that documents a formal A&R strategy or deliberate policy shift. The available evidence points consistently toward Hiroshige’s own broad musical interests and personal networks as the driving force.

Hiroshige’s pre-noise background. The note.com archived interview (Onojima, “Archive of Past Manuscripts Vol. 20 — JOJO Hiroshige Interview: Talking About the Earliest Days of Hijokaidan”, note.com) establishes that before Hijokaidan became a noise group, Hiroshige was operating in a “Velvet Underground-type rock band” mode, and that early sessions were “close to normal rock band performances.” He noted he was initially disappointed that the group didn’t play songs or have rock influences like Hawkwind — indicating a deep personal affinity for 60s/70s psychedelic and heavy rock well before Alchemy was founded. A search-result snippet from the same piece quotes Hiroshige: “I wasn’t doing things like ‘the incarnation of noise’ from the beginning; I started from the place where I loved music just like everyone else.” (Source: snippet via Google, note.com article as above.)

Personal taste: Yoshiko Sai. The Wikipedia article on Yoshiko Sai (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshiko_Sai, as of 2026-10-30) records that Hiroshige became a devoted fan of Yoshiko Sai — a Japanese folk-rock and enka singer — during his high school years in the 1970s, describing her as “the artist I love the most and who has had the greatest influence on me.” He wrote liner notes for her 1999 CD reissues, co-produced her return to activity, and released their 2001 improvised collaboration Crimson Voyage (ARCD-136) on Alchemy. Alchemy also co-sponsored a 2003 retrospective exhibition of her artwork. This demonstrates that Hiroshige used his label as an outlet for music he personally loved, well outside the noise genre.

Personal networks, not A&R scouting. The two most-documented cases of psych/prog artists arriving on Alchemy are Marble Sheep and Angel’in Heavy Syrup, and both point to personal connection rather than deliberate genre diversification:

  • Marble Sheep: guitarist Ken Matsutani told It’s Psychedelic Baby Magazine (interview, June 2019, psychedelicbabymag.com): “JoJo Hiroshige is founder and manager of Alchemy Records. They were still a big independent label in Japan. We were friends with JoJo and he helped us to organize some shows and offered us to make a recording.” Their debut on Alchemy appeared in 1990.

  • Angel’in Heavy Syrup: according to the Wikipedia article on Angel’in Heavy Syrup (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel%27in_Heavy_Syrup, as of 2026-10-30, snippet only — full article did not detail the signing), founding members Mineko Itakura and Mine Nakao first encountered Hiroshige and Alchemy Records at a live-house gig in Osaka in 1989 where Hanadensha played. They subsequently signed to Alchemy and released their debut album in 1991. The snippet notes that Hiroshige and Alchemy “made it a principle to lead the Japanese noise music scene,” yet signed Angel’in Heavy Syrup anyway — whose aesthetic was “gracefully meditative psychedelic progressive rock influenced by Krautrock and 1970s psychedelic progressive rock.”

The “West Psychedelia” compilation as an early marker. The catalogue shows that the first departure from pure noise came as early as 1988 with West Psychedelia (ARLP-017) and its follow-up West Psychedelia 2 (ARLP-024). Both appeared while noise releases continued uninterrupted, suggesting the broadening was additive rather than a replacement of the noise identity. No source was found that explicitly discusses who curated these compilations or what prompted them.

Summary assessment. Across all sources consulted, no statement from Hiroshige or any label history describes a deliberate A&R strategy to move into psychedelic/progressive rock. The pattern that emerges is: Hiroshige came to noise from a background that already included strong personal affinities for 60s/70s psychedelic and heavy rock; he used Alchemy from the outset as a vehicle for music he personally valued; and psych/prog artists arrived largely through pre-existing friendships within the Osaka underground network. The expansion appears organic — an expression of Hiroshige’s own tastes and social circle — rather than a calculated genre repositioning.
