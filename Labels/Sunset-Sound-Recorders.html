Overview

Sunset Sound Recorders is one of the defining recording studios of American popular music from the mid-1960s through the late 1970s. Founded around 1960 and opened to the public in 1962 by Tutti Camarata — a trumpeter and arranger who had worked for Walt Disney Productions — the studio at 6650 Sunset Boulevard in Hollywood became the place where a remarkable cross-section of West Coast rock, folk, jazz, and pop was committed to tape.

In its earliest years the facility functioned almost exclusively as a private recording and mastering shop for Walt Disney Productions, cutting records for the Disneyland and Buena Vista Records labels. After 1962 the doors opened to outside clients, and the studio quickly became a nexus of the emerging Hollywood rock scene.

The most concentrated period of activity documented in Discogs credits runs from 1966 — when The Beach Boys, Love, The Doors, Captain Beefheart, and Frank Zappa all used the studio — through the early 1980s, when acts like Van Halen and The Doobie Brothers were still tracking there. In January 1982, the Camarata family purchased the nearby The Sound Factory, at which point the enterprise was rebranded as Sunset Sound. Discogs convention is that releases made or mastered at the facility after 1982 should carry the Sunset Sound credit, not Sunset Sound Recorders.

Also credited on releases as: Sunset Sound, Sunset Sound Studios, Sunset Sound Recorders (Hollywood), Sunset Sound Recorders (North Hollywood, California), Sunset Sound Recorders (Los Angeles).

Early Disney Phase (1960–1965)

Before the studio opened to the wider music industry, it was essentially an in-house production facility for Walt Disney’s record divisions. Brian Ross-Myring was the primary engineer on dozens of Disneyland and Buena Vista Records releases throughout the 1960s and into the 1970s — film soundtracks, children’s story albums, educational records. The Mary Poppins original cast soundtrack (1964, 64 pressings documented) is the largest single release from this period.

Louis Armstrong recorded Disney Songs The Satchmo Way (1968) at Sunset Sound, which became one of the wider-distributing Disney-adjacent titles from the era (26 pressings across multiple territories).

The Elektra / Doors Era (1966–1971)

From 1966, Sunset Sound became the preferred tracking room for Elektra Records’ West Coast signings. Bruce Botnick was the house engineer for this work, and the relationship produced some of the most critically significant recordings associated with the studio.

  • Love — debut album (1966), engineered by Botnick, produced by Jac Holzman and Mark Abramson
  • The Doors — first two albums recorded here (1967); also Paul A. Rothchild producing
  • Clear Light (2) — self-titled debut (1967); Botnick engineering
  • Captain Beefheart — Strictly Personal (1968), Trout Mask Replica (1969; produced by Frank Zappa)
  • Frank Zappa — partial work on Uncle Meat (1969) overdubs; Hot Rats (1969) partial

The pattern of this period was Elektra A&R, Rothchild or Holzman producing, and Botnick engineering — a consistent production chain that runs through nearly all the major releases.

The Singer-Songwriter / Warner Constellation (1970–1975)

As the 1970s opened, Sunset Sound attracted the Asylum/Warner/Reprise-affiliated singer-songwriter community centered on LA. Key sessions:

  • James Taylor — Sweet Baby James (1970), engineer Bill Lazerus, producer Peter Asher, Carole King on piano
  • Neil Young — debut album (1969) and On The Beach (1974) partially recorded here
  • Bill Withers — Just As I Am (1971); Booker T. Jones producing; Stephen Stills on guitar; one of the best-documented of Withers’ early recordings
  • Jackson Browne — For Everyman (1973), Late For The Sky (1974)
  • Earth, Wind & Fire — The Need Of Love (1971)
  • Rita Coolidge — multiple albums 1971–1977
  • Crazy Horse — debut album (1971)
  • Keith Jarrett / Jack DeJohnette — Ruta And Daitya (1973, ECM)

The Stones Session and Classic Rock Peak (1972–1978)

The Rolling Stones mixed Exile On Main St. at Sunset Sound in 1972 — the basic tracks had been cut at Nellcôte in France, but the mix sessions are specifically credited to the studio. Engineers Andy Johns and Glyn Johns are credited; Jimmy Miller produced.

Through the mid-to-late 1970s the studio became a workhorse for mainstream rock:

  • The Doobie Brothers — multiple albums from Takin’ It To The Streets (1976) through Minute By Minute (1978)
  • Warren Zevon — debut (1976)
  • Elton John — Blue Moves (1976)
  • Little Feat — Time Loves A Hero (1977)
  • Bonnie Raitt — Sweet Forgiveness (1977)
  • Van Halen — debut (1978) and Van Halen II (1979); eng. Donn Landee, prod. Ted Templeman

Open Questions

  • The exact relationship between Brian Ross-Myring and the Disney work — he appears to have been a staff engineer from at least 1962 through the mid-1970s, but the extent of his work on non-Disney sessions is not fully documented in Discogs.
  • Whether the Led Zeppelin IV Discogs credits represent original mastering or reissue mastering done at the studio.
  • Details on the studio’s room configurations, console history, and tape machine inventory — not available in Discogs.
  • Wayne Dailey is listed in the studio profile as a known engineer but no specific releases are indexed under his name in the company credits.
  • The full scope of activity between 1975 and 1982 extends well beyond the ~100 releases sampled here; Discogs has hundreds more pressings in this window.
