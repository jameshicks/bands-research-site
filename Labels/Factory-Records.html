Factory Records

Founded: Manchester, 1978 Closed: November 1992 (receivership); formally dissolved December 1998 Based: Manchester, UK Catalog prefix: FAC (all releases, including non-musical items) Founders: Tony Wilson, Alan Erasmus House Producer (early): Martin Hannett Designer: Peter Saville

Overview

One of the most influential independent labels in UK music history. Home to Joy Division, New Order, A Certain Ratio, The Durutti Column, Happy Mondays, and many others. Defined by Tony Wilson’s refusal to use conventional contracts (“we don’t believe in them”), Peter Saville’s design language, Martin Hannett’s production aesthetic, and the FAC numbering system applied to every artifact the label touched — records, posters, buildings, legal agreements, even the company’s own insolvency (FAC 501 = Factory’s catalogue; FAC 502 = the Factory company; FAC 511 = the unfinished Factory headquarters).

Founders and Key Figures

  • Tony Wilson (Anthony Howard Wilson, b. 20 Feb 1950 Salford, d. 10 Aug 2007; Discogs ID 331381) — journalist, Granada TV presenter (“So It Goes”), co-founder. The animating intelligence of Factory: brought the philosophy, the contacts, the TV platform, and the willingness to lose money on principle. Also managed The Durutti Column.
  • Alan Erasmus (Discogs ID 3853027) — co-founder with Wilson; co-founded The Durutti Column with Wilson in 1978; managed early Factory acts; quieter presence but essential to the operation
  • Martin Hannett (James Martin Hannett, b. 31 May 1948, d. 18 Apr 1991; alias Martin Zero, ID 227772; Discogs ID 80561) — house producer 1978–82; created the Factory sound (cavernous, spatially manipulated, drum-machine driven). Joy Division’s Unknown Pleasures and Closer, New Order’s Movement. Fell out with Wilson over the Joy Division royalties; sued Factory 1982; relationship never fully repaired. Returned to produce Happy Mondays’ Bummed (1988). Died 1991, age 42.
  • Peter Saville (b. 1955 Manchester; Discogs ID 1069010) — graphic designer; created the Factory visual identity from FAC 2. Worked in a Constructivist/modernist mode — stark typography, art reference, no conventional band imagery. As influential in design terms as the music was in sonic terms.
  • Rob Gretton (Robert Leo Gretton, b. 15 Jan 1953 Wythenshawe, d. 15 May 1999 Manchester; Discogs ID 236150) — New Order / Joy Division manager; Factory partner from 1980; co-founded The Haçienda; ran Robs Records. Died age 46. Discogs credits beyond management: co-produced Section 25 “Girls Don’t Count” (FAC 11, 1980, uncredited) with Ian Curtis; engineer assists on A Factory Quartet (1980); produced (uncredited) A Certain Ratio’s “The Still Reflex / Fade Out” on Les Disques du Crépuscule (1981); produced Section 25’s Always Now (1991); contributed design concept (title and concept) for the New Order Heart And Soul box set (London Records, 1997). He also had pre-Factory producer credits on Slaughter & the Dogs’ It Won’t Sell! (Rainy City Records, 1977) and Downpour (Streets Ahead Records, 1979) — showing he was an active producer before the Factory era.
  • Mike Pickering — DJ at The Haçienda; later formed T-Coy and then M People; bridge between Factory’s post-punk era and the dance music era

History

1978–1980: Formation

Grew out of Wilson and Erasmus promoting gigs at the Russell Club (Royce Road, Hulme, Manchester) — initially a one-off night called “The Factory” in May 1978. Acts: Joy Division, The Durutti Column, Cabaret Voltaire (Sheffield), Orchestral Manoeuvres in the Dark. The first Factory Records release was the A Factory Sample double 7” EP (FAC 2, January 1979): Joy Division (“Digital” / “Glass”), The Durutti Column (“No Communication” / “Thin Ice”), John Dowie, Cabaret Voltaire (“Baader-Meinhof” / “Sex In Secret”). All produced by Martin Hannett (billed as Martin Zero); sleeve designed by Peter Saville; mastered by George Peckham.

The FAC numbering system was applied from the start to everything — not just records. FAC 2 was a Durutti Column gig poster. FAC 3 was a Joy Division EP. This became the defining eccentricity: a philosophy made visible through bureaucratic absurdism.

Martin Hannett produced Joy Division’s Unknown Pleasures (FAC 10, June 1979) and Closer (FAC 25, July 1980). These two albums defined Factory’s sound and reputation internationally.

1980: Factory Benelux

Tony Wilson visited Brussels’s Plan K venue on 26 April 1980 — where Michel Duval and Annik Honoré were promoting concerts. He struck an agreement to create Factory Benelux as a Benelux-territory imprint operating from Brussels under Les Disques du Crépuscule’s infrastructure. The first FBN release was A Certain Ratio’s “Shack Up” 7” (August 1980). See Factory Benelux.

1980–1982: Expansion and the Haçienda

Ian Curtis died 18 May 1980 (day before Joy Division’s first US tour). The band reconvened as New Order — Factory’s most commercially significant act, eventually funding the label through the 1980s.

The Haçienda (FAC 51) opened 21 May 1982 — a nightclub at 11–13 Whitworth Street West, Manchester, co-owned by Factory and New Order. Designed by Ben Kelly. It hemorrhaged money for years before becoming the epicenter of the UK acid house and rave scene 1988–90. The Haçienda’s losses were covered by New Order’s earnings — a source of permanent tension.

Martin Hannett sued Factory in 1982 over his unpaid equity stake, receiving a settlement but severing the producing relationship.

1983–1992: Commercial years

Factory’s commercial peak. New Order’s “Blue Monday” (FAC 73, March 1983) became the bestselling 12” single in UK history. Ironically, the die-cut sleeve cost more to manufacture than the retail price, so the label lost money on every copy sold.

Happy Mondays signed in 1984. Their run from Bummed (1988) through Pills ‘n’ Thrills and Bellyaches (1990) bridged post-punk and the rave era and brought Factory its greatest mainstream success.

Factory launched sub-labels: Factory Classical (FAC(X) prefix), Ikon (video releases), Factory America, Factory Too (planned but premature).

1992: Collapse

Factory entered receivership 23 November 1992 with debts of approximately £2.5 million. The immediate cause: Happy Mondays’ Yes Please! LP, recorded in Barbados at enormous expense, sold poorly. London Records acquired the Factory name and some catalog; Wilson received nothing. The FAC numbering continued ironically — FAC 501 is the Factory catalogue, FAC 502 the Factory company.

Factory Communications Ltd. was formally dissolved December 1998.

Post-Collapse Legacy

Factory Too (Discogs label ID: 32853) — Wilson revived the Factory name in 1994 as a London Records sublabel. 65 releases/pressings on Discogs (unique masters: 12). Dominant act: Hopper (5) (5 releases); also The Durutti Column and The Space Monkeys. The 6ths (Stephin Merritt project) also appeared. Quietly wound down ~1998.

LTM Recordings — from 1997, James Nice of LTM Recordings began systematically reissuing Factory catalog on CD. Nice had worked at Les Disques du Crépuscule in Brussels in the late 1980s.

Factory Records Ltd. (2010–2013) — incorporated 29 June 2010 (Companies House no. 07298302; registered address: 24e Norwich Street, Dereham, Norfolk NR19 1BX — James Nice’s home county). Companies House. Officers on incorporation: James Nice (director + company secretary), Peter Saville (director), both appointed 29 June 2010; Alan Erasmus (director) appointed 24 August 2010. No resignations were filed; the company dissolved with those three directors intact. Business activities registered: sound recording reproduction, book publishing, music publishing, and television programming/broadcasting.

Oliver Wilson and the 2010 company. Oliver Wilson is the son of Anthony H. Wilson (Tony Wilson, d. 2007). He is named in some press accounts as associated with Factory Records Ltd. — Wikipedia’s Factory Records article states the company was formed “in association with Alan Erasmus and Oliver Wilson (son of Tony)” — but this claim is unsourced in Wikipedia and contradicted by the formal record. He does not appear in the Companies House officers list as a director. The PSC (persons with significant control) register shows no filings for this company, so formal shareholding data cannot be confirmed via that route. The annual returns for periods ending June 2011 and June 2012 both include full shareholder lists (the company had £40 of share capital — indicating four £10 shares or similar tiny issuance), but those PDF documents were not directly accessible for inspection. All other sources describing Oliver Wilson’s activities during this period frame him as a cultural custodian of his father’s archive — he partnered with the Smolensky Gallery to exhibit Tony Wilson’s personal ephemera and is engaged in a social enterprise using Tony Wilson’s music holdings as a community resource — not as a music business operator. Assessment: “Oliver Wilson” in press coverage of the 2010 company is loose attribution, likely reflecting his moral/family authority over the Wilson name and the family’s estate stake in the original Factory catalog, rather than any formal directorial or shareholding role in Factory Records Ltd. (07298302). If he held any shares, it was a nominal stake consistent with the £40 total capital — not a controlling interest. Sources: Companies House officers (confirmed 2026-07-26); Companies House PSC (confirmed 2026-07-26); Wikipedia: Factory Records (as of 2026-07-26, unsourced claim); The Vinyl Factory; I Love Manchester.

The company’s sole confirmed release was a double-vinyl reissue of Section 25’s From the Hip (originally FACT 90, 1984), issued 28 May 2012 under the Factory Records imprint, with artwork reworked by Peter Saville; the deluxe edition added a bonus 12” coupling two versions of “Looking from a Hilltop” (one remixed by Bernard Sumner and Donald Johnson of A Certain Ratio, one by Stephen Morris). Wikipedia: From the Hip

The company was dissolved 5 March 2013. Last accounts filed covered the period to 30 June 2011 — covering a period before the Section 25 release. No further releases under the Factory Records Ltd. entity are known. No licensing deals, compilations, films, or TV placements have been attributed to it in the press.

After dissolution, James Nice redirected Factory-related activity to the revived Factory Benelux imprint (FBN), which he relaunched in 2012 with the blessing of original founders Michel Duval and Annik Honoré. Factory Benelux has since become the operative vehicle for reissues of Factory-associated catalog and new recordings by Factory-affiliated acts (Section 25, Crispy Ambulance, Marsheaux, Marnie, Deux Filles, etc.). See Factory Benelux.

Mute / The Grey Area — also hold and reissue elements of the Factory catalog.
