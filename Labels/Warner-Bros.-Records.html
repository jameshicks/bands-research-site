Warner Bros. Records

Discogs Label ID: 1000
Founded: 1958, Burbank, California
Parent: Warner Bros. Records Inc. (ID: 90718) → Warner Music Group (ID: 2345)
Corporate lineage: Warner Communications → Time Warner → Warner Music Group
Scale: ~149,855 releases in Discogs; 47 sublabels listed

Major American record label founded as the music division of Warner Bros. Pictures. One of the “Big Four” major labels throughout the rock era. Relevant to this vault primarily through jazz, alternative rock, avant-garde, and art-rock signings, and through the long tenures of Mo Ostin and Lenny Waronker.

The Ostin–Waronker Era (1960s–1994)

Mo Ostin (born Morris Meyer Ostrofsky; Discogs ID: 453501) ran Warner Bros. Records from the early 1960s, first as president and later as chairman. He built the label’s reputation for creative A&R by giving artists unusual autonomy — Jimi Hendrix, Neil Young, Van Morrison, Joni Mitchell, the Grateful Dead, and later Randy Newman and Rickie Lee Jones all benefited from this approach.

Lenny Waronker joined the label in the mid-1960s and rose from staff producer to head of A&R (1970) to president (1982–1992), with Ostin remaining as chairman. Their working relationship was one of the most consequential in American music:

  • Waronker’s production base was at Reprise Records through the 1970s: Randy Newman (full Reprise catalog 1968–1983), Gordon Lightfoot (peak years 1970–1978), Ry Cooder (1970–1974), Maria Muldaur (1973–74, with Joe Boyd), Rickie Lee Jones (debut, 1979, and Pirates, 1981).
  • On the WB imprint specifically: Van Dyke Parks Song Cycle (1967), Harpers Bizarre (1967–1969), James Taylor (mid-1970s), Paul Simon Hearts And Bones (1983), Eric Clapton Behind The Sun (1985).
  • Waronker’s regular production partner was Russ Titelman; their long association defines the sound of the label’s singer-songwriter wing.

Ostin and Waronker departed together in 1992–1994 to co-found DreamWorks Records, where they signed Rufus Wainwright and provided A&R direction to Elliott Smith.

Jazz Connections

Herbie Hancock — Mwandishi Period (1969–1972)

Herbie Hancock recorded for Warner Bros. during his most adventurous phase. The Mwandishi period albums — Fat Albert Rotunda (1969), Mwandishi (1971), Crossings (1972) — represent his move from Blue Note hard bop toward electric fusion and avant-garde electronics. The full period was collected in Mwandishi: The Complete Warner Bros. Recordings (Warner Archives, 1994).

Personnel across the Mwandishi band included Eddie Henderson (trumpet), Julian Priester (trombone), Bennie Maupin (woodwinds), Buster Williams (bass), Billy Hart (drums), and Patrick Gleeson (synthesizers). David Sanborn appears on Crossing.

Hancock remained at WB through the late 1970s for commercial fusion work (Head Hunters, 1973, actually on Columbia; Sunlight, 1978, on Columbia), then returned as a WB/Qwest artist for Future Shock (1983) on Columbia/CBS — his Discogs entries show significant catalog overlap across labels. His WB discography: 151 releases cataloged on Discogs.

Sire Records — Post-Punk and New Wave Hub

Sire (Discogs ID: 27031; parent: Warner Music Group) is the primary WB-family label for post-punk and new wave. Founded 1966 by Seymour Stein and Richard Gottehrer; acquired into the Warner system c.1978. Key vault-adjacent Sire artists:

  • Talking Heads — entire studio catalog 1977–1988 on Sire (Talking Heads: 77, More Songs About Buildings And Food, Fear Of Music, Remain In Light, Speaking In Tongues, Naked). Distributed by WB in the US; European releases on EMI. See Talking Heads for full personnel.
  • Ramones — Sire; Discogs artist ID 135478; 181 releases, first year 1977.
  • David Byrne — solo work on Luaka Bop/Sire/WB through 1997; Rei Momo (1989), Uh-Oh (1992), David Byrne (1994). His own Luaka Bop imprint is distributed by Sire/WB. Subsequent solo albums moved to Nonesuch (Grown Backwards, 2004) and later collaborations.
  • New Order — 234 releases on WB (Qwest/WB US pressing); 1985 onward. See Electronic.
  • Ministry — Sire through 1992 (see above).
  • k.d. lang — Sire 1989–2000 (Absolute Torch And Twang, Ingénue).

The Sire/WB configuration is the key infrastructure link between the New York post-punk scene (CBGB era: Talking Heads, Ramones, Television) and the major label system.

Avant-Garde and Performance Art

Van Dyke Parks

Van Dyke Parks recorded Song Cycle (Warner Bros., 1967) with Lenny Waronker producing — one of the most elaborately orchestrated avant-pop albums of the era, critically celebrated and commercially marginal. His WB catalog continued through Discover America (1972), Clang Of The Yankee Reaper (1975), Jump! (1984), and the Brian Wilson collaboration Orange Crate Art (WB, 1995). See Van Dyke Parks.

Devo Signing (1978)

Devo signed with Warner Bros. Records for the Americas and Japan in early 1978, releasing their debut Q: Are We Not Men? A: We Are Devo! on 28 August 1978 (Virgin handled UK/Europe). The signing came about through a sequence of external endorsements rather than through conventional A&R discovery:

  • David Bowie and Iggy Pop received a Devo demo tape (via the wife of Michael Aylward, guitarist of Tin Huey) and became vocal champions of the band. Bowie proclaimed at Devo’s 1977 NYC debut that they were “the band of the future” and initially planned to produce them in Tokyo. [source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/devo-q-are-we-not-men-a-we-are-devo/ (as of 2026-09-29)]
  • A multi-label bidding war followed: A&M dropped out, Island was rejected, Bowie’s own Bewlay Brothers production company made an offer (declined by the band as underfunded), and both Virgin and Warner Bros. competed for the deal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q._Are_We_Not_Men%3F_A:_We_Are_Devo! (as of 2026-09-29); https://www.pastemagazine.com/music/devo/devo-50th-anniversary-essay (as of 2026-09-29)]
  • Bowie’s lawyer Stan Diamond is named in at least one source as the intermediary who connected Devo with Warner Bros. for the direct deal. [source: https://www.pastemagazine.com/music/devo/devo-50th-anniversary-essay (as of 2026-09-29)]
  • After Bowie withdrew from production due to prior commitments (filming Just a Gigolo), Brian Eno produced the debut at Conny Plank’s studio in Cologne, Germany, October 1977 – February 1978, with Warner Bros. paying for the band’s gear to be shipped and holding first refusal on the finished album. Bowie later remixed most tracks (uncredited). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q._Are_We_Not_Men%3F_A:_We_Are_Devo! (as of 2026-09-29)]
  • Manager Elliot Roberts (Neil Young’s manager) represented Devo in the deal. Warner Bros. later pressured Roberts directly — “Make these guys make a hit or we’ll see you in court” — which contributed to the more commercial direction of Freedom of Choice (1980). [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2016/12/15/magnet-classics-the-making-of-devos-freedom-of-choice/ (as of 2026-09-29)]
  • Warner Bros. bought out the remainder of Devo’s contract after the poor reception of Shout (1984).

Lenny Waronker’s role — not confirmed by web sources

The vault’s prior note flagged “Lenny Waronker involvement in signing” for Devo. As of a thorough September 2026 web trawl:

  • No publicly available source — Wikipedia (Devo, Waronker, Gerald Casale, Q: Are We Not Men?, Freedom of Choice articles), AllMusic, Ultimate Classic Rock, Paste Magazine, Trouser Press, Magnet Magazine, Encyclopedia.com, the Rock Hall Waronker page, the USC Waronker profile, nor Peter Ames Carlin’s Sonic Boom (WB Records history, 2021) — names Waronker as the internal WB champion of the Devo signing.
  • Waronker was head of A&R at Warner Bros. from 1970. As such, any signing in 1978 would have required his involvement or at minimum his sign-off, but no source documents his specific contribution to this deal.
  • The consistent narrative across all sources attributes the external pressure for the signing to Bowie and Iggy Pop’s endorsements and to manager Elliot Roberts’s negotiations — not to internal WB A&R initiative.
  • The most likely source for specifics would be: (a) Waronker’s multi-part Discograffiti podcast interview (4 parts, episodes 244–247, host Dave Gebroe, 2025) — episode contents not indexed or searchable online; (b) the Musician magazine Waronker interview (Mark Rowland, July 1994); (c) the Mo Ostin Collection or Jeff Gold Collection at the Rock Hall Library.

Conflict / gap note: The existing vault entry attributed “Waronker involvement in signing” without a source. No web source confirms this. The claim may originate from the Discograffiti podcast episodes or from Sonic Boom — both require direct access to verify. Do not treat Waronker’s championing of the Devo signing as confirmed until a sourced account is found.

The Band — The Last Waltz (1978)

Warner Bros. released The Band’s The Last Waltz (1978), the triple-LP document of their 1976 farewell concert at Winterland, San Francisco. Co-produced by Robbie Robertson, Rob Fraboni, and John Simon. Rick Danko credited on bass, violin, and vocals throughout. The film was directed by Martin Scorsese.

His Name Is Alive — Mouth by Mouth (1993, US co-release)

WB co-released Mouth by Mouth (1993, His Name Is Alive) in the United States alongside 4AD, catalog number 9 45214-2. The US pressing had samples excised for clearance reasons and omitted the final track “The Homesick Waltz.” See Mouth by Mouth.
