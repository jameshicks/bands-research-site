Overview

Fierce Panda is a UK indie label founded in 1994 with the modest intention of releasing a single compilation EP — Shagging In The Streets — and then closing up shop. Instead it grew into one of British indie’s most prolific early-release platforms, giving first or early singles to Coldplay, Keane, Placebo, The Music, and Embrace, as well as the unlikely top-100 Oasis interview single Wibbling Rivalry. The catalogue peaked in the 2000s with around 160 releases that decade before tapering significantly in the 2010s; the label also extended its reach through four sub-imprints: Cool For Cats Records, Label Fandango, Livid Meerkat, and Rabid Badger.

Founding

Fierce Panda was founded in April 1994 following a conversation in The Blue Posts pub on Tottenham Court Road between three NME journalists: Simon Williams, John Harris, and Paul Moody. The original plan was to release a single compilation EP — Shagging In The Streets, a tribute to the New Wave of New Wave scene — and then close the label. Moody and Harris departed after that first release, keeping to the original plan; Williams continued and over the next twelve months put out four more EPs. Long Live Vinyl, 2019 | Suffolk News, 2021

Wibbling Rivalry (NING 12, 1995)

The circumstances of the Wibbling Rivalry release explain themselves once the founding story is known. John Harris — one of Fierce Panda’s three co-founders — was also the NME journalist who conducted the source interview. On 7 April 1994, Harris recorded Noel and Liam Gallagher arguing at the Forte Crest Hotel in Glasgow; the interview captures a heated dispute over a February 1994 ferry incident in which Liam had started a drunken brawl. Wikipedia, Wibbling Rivalry, as of 2026-10-04

By the time Fierce Panda decided to release the recording, Harris had already moved on from the label (he left after the inaugural Shagging In The Streets EP). The connection was nonetheless direct: Harris had co-founded the label with Williams, and Williams was the one who took the recording forward as a release. The single was issued in 1995 under the artist name “Oas*s” to sidestep any implication of an official Oasis release. Williams chose the title — a pun on “sibling rivalry” plus the Britpop-era slang “wibble” — in keeping with his habit of punny catalogue titles. Wikipedia, Fierce Panda Records, as of 2026-10-04

The label was initially reluctant given that Oasis were signed to Creation Records, which by then was distributed by Sony. Williams later recalled: “We were quite worried. Then I got a call from Sony’s lawyers and I thought, ‘Uh-oh, this is it!’ But they just said they loved it and asked me to send them a copy. And Noel was great about it.” Both Gallaghers approved the release. Wikipedia, Wibbling Rivalry, as of 2026-10-04

Wibbling Rivalry reached #52 on the UK Singles Chart on 25 November 1995, the highest chart position ever achieved by an interview single. The cover featured a photograph of the Kray twins.

Summary: This was not a purely opportunistic deal — the release flowed from an internal connection. Harris co-founded Fierce Panda and later conducted the interview in his NME role; Williams, who had kept the label running after Harris departed, recognised the value of the recording and pushed it through despite the Creation/Sony complication.

Note on the brief: The original research brief attributed the interview to Mark Lamarr. This is incorrect. The interviewer was John Harris (NME). Mark Lamarr is not connected to this recording by any source found.

The 2010s Output Drop — Web sources

The steep fall from ~160 releases in the 2000s to ~62 in the 2010s reflects a combination of a deliberate strategic pivot (2006) and a structural market collapse (from ~2007) — not a single cause.

1. The 2006 pivot away from one-off singles

By 2005–06 Williams had grown disillusioned with the label’s traditional model. In a 2009 Drowned In Sound interview he explained: “we got bored of the whole process of putting out singles purely to help bands get major-label deals — which they would have got anyway with or without us.” Dead Disco’s “Automatic” (NING 190, 2006) was announced as the last one-off single. Williams acknowledged the pivot came naturally: the label had “actually been putting albums out for years but didn’t know how to market them.” By 2009 they were approaching their 50th album. Drowned In Sound, 2009

This decision alone compressed the catalogue dramatically: the label had been producing a high volume of cheap 7”/CD-single releases through the 1990s and 2000s. Shifting to albums-only (or albums-primary) means fewer catalogue numbers per unit of effort.

2. The 2007 A&R pipeline collapse

In the Long Live Vinyl profile (2019) Williams described a second blow that hit around the same time: “Everything just turned to shit. I think around 2007 was about the last time major labels actually signed guitar bands.” Fierce Panda’s developmental model depended on this pipeline — sign an emerging indie act, put out a single or two, watch the majors come calling. When majors stopped signing guitar acts, bands that would previously have moved on and freed up roster space stayed put, and new acts had less commercial incentive to seek early-career indie singles deals at all. Long Live Vinyl, 2019

3. Digital and streaming disruption

In the 2009 Drowned In Sound piece Williams noted that majors now had the upper hand because “they can afford resources and personnel for entire online departments while I struggle to find enough time to update news stories for the Panda website.” He observed that “so-called ‘indie’ acts have no need for ‘indie’ labels, not even for the first little 7”.” Drowned In Sound, 2009

By 2022 (NME, Pandamonium! interview) Williams described how streaming fragmented visibility: “With streaming, the label is completely irrelevant. You can have your own playlist, but at least for the charts, it says it’s on this label — Spotify doesn’t give a fuck.” The label had previously “could fairly routinely get in the top 75, certainly the top 200,” but chart accessibility collapsed when streaming replaced downloads: “then downloads impacted, and within six months, 100 to 200 was stuck week after week.” NME, 2022

The Music Week 25th-anniversary piece (2019) adds Williams calling the current environment “probably about five million times harder now” to operate an indie label. He also identified overextension as a common indie-label killer: “that attempt to have success that destroys them, because they spend fortunes.” Fierce Panda survived in part by not chasing breakout hits. Music Week, 2019

4. Vinyl logistics as a physical ceiling

One practical constraint on volume: Williams noted the storage and financial exposure of vinyl overstock — “our office would have to be the size of Belgium ‘cos you’d have so much overstock” — and recounted a financially ruinous Eastern European vinyl deal. The shift to vinyl-led album releases (as opposed to cheap CD-singles runs) raised the financial stakes per release and made high volume harder to sustain. Long Live Vinyl, 2019

5. Williams’s 2019 health crisis

The NME 2022 piece briefly notes that following a 2019 health crisis the label went into what Williams described as hibernation: “we were literally going into hibernation, it was all over.” This explains the near-zero output 2019–2020 before Ash’s return signing revitalised the label. NME, 2022

Summary assessment

The drop is primarily explained by three converging factors: (a) a deliberate 2006 decision to abandon the high-volume one-off singles model that had inflated the 1990s–2000s counts; (b) the collapse of the major-label guitar-band A&R pipeline from ~2007, which removed the commercial logic underpinning early-singles deals; and (c) streaming economics making small-label visibility structurally harder. The lower 2010s total is not evidence of a label in crisis — the catalogue numbers show continuous if slower releases throughout — but rather reflects a fundamentally different release philosophy and a market that no longer rewarded the original model.

No source found in which Williams explicitly states a target release count or describes a deliberate numerical ceiling on 2010s output. The reduced volume appears to be the practical result of the above factors rather than a stated strategic cap.

John Harris’s post-EP involvement

No public source documents any formal or informal role for John Harris with Fierce Panda after the inaugural Shagging In The Streets EP. The Wikipedia article on John Harris (critic), the Fierce Panda Wikipedia article, the Long Live Vinyl 2019 label profile, and the Music Week 2019 interview all confirm only that Harris and Paul Moody co-founded the label and then departed as planned after that first release. Harris is not mentioned in any subsequent Fierce Panda context in any source found. The Wikipedia article on Wibbling Rivalry confirms Harris as the NME interviewer but says nothing about his relationship to the release decision or whether he retained any role at the label. Wikipedia, Wibbling Rivalry, as of 2026-10-05 | Wikipedia, John Harris (critic), as of 2026-10-05

Assessment: Available sources support only that Harris departed cleanly after the first EP. The Wibbling Rivalry connection runs through the pre-existing personal relationship between Harris and Williams (NME colleagues and label co-founders) rather than through any continuing formal role Harris held at the label. Whether Williams sought Harris’s consent before releasing the recording, or Harris proactively offered it, is not documented in any public source. The mechanics are addressed in Pandamonium! (2022) but no reviewer or interviewer has quoted the relevant passage.

Williams’s 2019 health crisis

Identified across multiple sources. The crisis was an extended suicide attempt on or around 30 December 2019, which Williams describes in Pandamonium! (2022) under the heading “the Grand Malarkey.” He was hospitalised at the Royal Free for the first two weeks of January 2020 and monitored for possible liver failure. The book also addresses his father’s suicide and a long preceding period of depression. Entertainment Now review of Pandamonium!, November 2022 | NME interview, September 2022 | N S Ford review, 2022

The vault’s existing entry in “The 2010s Output Drop” section (from the previous web pass) already notes the crisis briefly; this entry provides the specifics that were not previously in the file.

Wibbling Rivalry acquisition mechanics

No public source specifies the acquisition mechanics — who approached whom, whether Harris transferred the tape to Williams, or when that conversation happened. The NME interview, the Joyzine 2022 interview, the book reviews, and all Wikipedia articles are silent on this point. The only publicly documented facts are: (a) Harris conducted the interview in his NME capacity on 7 April 1994; (b) Williams released it as NING 12 in 1995 under the label they had co-founded; (c) Williams recalled being worried about Creation/Sony’s reaction before receiving approval from both Gallaghers and Sony’s lawyers. The question of whether Harris formally handed over the recording or consented to its use is answerable only by reading Pandamonium! directly — no reviewer or interviewer has quoted the relevant passage on this point.

2006 singles pivot and distribution

No public source connects the 2006 decision to cease one-off singles to a concurrent change in distributor. Williams’s own accounts (Drowned In Sound 2009, Long Live Vinyl 2019) frame the pivot entirely in terms of label philosophy and market conditions, not logistics or distributor relationships. The only distributor named in any available source for Fierce Panda is Essential Music & Marketing (current as of Wikipedia’s article); no historical distributor sequence is documented publicly. Whether the singles model was tied to a distributor deal that was ending could not be confirmed or ruled out from available sources.
