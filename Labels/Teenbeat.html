Overview

Teenbeat is an independent arts organization founded in 1984 by Mark Robinson — frontman of Unrest — while he was a student at Wakefield High School in Arlington, Virginia. Rooted in the Washington, D.C. indie scene, the label became home to Unrest, Tuscadero, and Air Miami, working across indie rock, lo-fi, alternative rock, and experimental pop. Its catalogue is concentrated heavily in the 1990s, when the label issued the bulk of its output across 77 releases, before activity dropped sharply in the 2000s and slowed further into the 2010s. Teenbeat operates under several sublabels, including Teen-Beat Originals and the Teenbeat Annual Sampler Series.

Notes (web)

Release decline after the 1990s

No single wind-down event has been documented; the sharp drop in output after the late 1990s appears to be the cumulative result of several overlapping factors:

Robinson’s relocation and design career. Around 1999 Robinson moved from Washington D.C. to Cambridge/Boston. The move was driven by professional opportunities in design — he told a 2004 interviewer that D.C.’s largest employers were government and lobbying firms, whereas Boston offered a range of companies. He went on to design roughly 30–50 book covers a year for Houghton Mifflin (later credited as assistant art director at HarperCollins), which became his primary income. All Teenbeat work is done pro bono alongside the day job. (markprindle.com interview; Boston Globe, 2010-07-02)

Considered closing but chose to continue. On moving to Cambridge, Robinson “briefly toyed with the idea of shutting down Teenbeat to focus on his design career,” but ultimately decided against it, saying “I stick with it because I love doing it.” The label was never formally wound down; it was kept alive deliberately, just at much lower volume. (Boston Globe, 2010-07-02)

Loss of the 1990s infrastructure. The high-output 1990s coincided with Robinson having “a whole bunch of great interns that helped make everything happen.” Post-move, the label became a true one-man operation run from home, with Robinson handling mail orders, artwork/design, and sometimes recording himself. The structural support that enabled 20 releases in a single year (1993’s peak) simply no longer existed. (Saki Store blog interview, 2012; Boston Globe, 2010-07-02)

Ongoing activity on a smaller scale. Robinson continued assigning catalogue numbers into the 2020s (e.g., Versus (2) Let’s Electrify! at Teen-Beat 552 in 2021; Jonny Cohen Hamsters For Kerry at 599 in 2024) and told a 2025 interviewer he “continues to operate the Teen-Beat label” and spends “most of [his] time designing and creating new things with Teen-Beat catalog numbers.” (Psychedelic Baby Magazine, 2025-04)

Summary: The decline was deliberate in the sense that Robinson chose a smaller, sustainable model over closure — not a wind-down, but a structural shift from a scene-supported micro-label to a personal arts project run alongside a full-time design career.
