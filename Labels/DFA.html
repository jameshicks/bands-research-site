Overview

DFA Records — full name Death From Above — is a New York punk-funk label co-founded around 2002 by James Murphy, Tim Goldsworthy, and Jonathan Galkin. The name came from the nickname Murphy gave to the PA rig he ran as live sound engineer for Six Finger Satellite: a deliberately overwhelming setup he called “Death From Above.” The catalogue of roughly 40 releases clusters entirely in the 2000s and spans disco, electro, house, leftfield, and indie rock, with LCD Soundsystem as the flagship act and in-house engineer Eric Broucek and the production duo The DFA among its core contributors. The label also runs two sublabels, DFA White Out Series and Death From Abroad.

Tim Goldsworthy departure — notes (web)

The Goldsworthy split from DFA played out in two distinct phases: a gradual operational withdrawal c. 2007–2010, followed by a financial/legal rupture in 2013. No source documents a clean “departure date” or a single creative disagreement as the cause.

Phase 1 — gradual withdrawal (c. 2007–2010)

Goldsworthy progressively reduced his involvement with DFA from around 2007 onward, relocating to the UK. By 2010 he acknowledged he had “done very little work with DFA in the past two years.” His stated reasons were practical: the bulk of his production work was UK-based, and he preferred the UK as a place to raise his family. (Resident Advisor, Feb 2010; The Quietus, Feb 2010)

Jonathan Galkin told Resident Advisor in February 2010 that Goldsworthy had “moved away to somewhere in the United Kingdom, without informing anyone here at DFA. We have had no word from him since he left the United States.” Goldsworthy disputed that framing, saying “I haven’t left” and noting that Murphy himself was rarely at the DFA office because he was always touring or in the studio — by that logic, Murphy would have “left nine years ago or something.” (Resident Advisor, Feb 2010)

The available record does not document a specific creative or personal falling-out between Murphy and Goldsworthy at this stage. A 2003/2004 Tape Op interview shows the two with evident warmth and complementary strengths; no tension is on record from that period. (Tape Op #38)

There is one data point suggesting an earlier emotional fracture: When The Rapture left DFA for Universal/Interscope around 2003–2004, Murphy was quoted (in the Meet Me in the Bathroom documentary/oral history context) as saying “Tim [Goldsworthy] and I felt broken” — suggesting the loss of the Rapture was a shared blow rather than a point of conflict between them. (Hotpress, 2022, citing Meet Me in the Bathroom)

Phase 2 — lawsuit (2013)

In March 2013 Murphy and DFA Records filed a $93,899 lawsuit against Goldsworthy in Manhattan Civil Supreme Court, alleging: (a) breach of contract and unjust enrichment; (b) failure to perform services he was paid for; (c) outstanding loans owed to the company; (d) improper use of the DFA company credit card; (e) unauthorized withdrawals from DFA’s bank accounts. Murphy stated his “attempts to amicably resolve this matter were rebuffed.” (NME, March 2013; DNAinfo, March 2013)

The suit did not allege creative differences. The dispute was entirely financial/contractual. Goldsworthy remained listed as a managing member of DFA at the time of the suit. No public resolution or outcome of the case has been found in any source checked (as of 2026-09-27).

”How Do You Sleep?” — Murphy / Goldsworthy connection

The song “How Do You Sleep?” from LCD Soundsystem’s American Dream (2017) is widely reported to reference Murphy’s unresolved feelings about Goldsworthy, its title echoing John Lennon’s 1971 attack on McCartney. Murphy has not publicly confirmed this in any source found. The attribution appears in music press analysis (Roland Articles) and is described as “rumoured” in Wikipedia. The Meet Me in the Bathroom oral history is said to contain material on the vitriol between the two, but the specific passages are not quoted in any source available online.

Effect on label direction after Goldsworthy

Following Goldsworthy’s withdrawal, DFA continued releasing records but the production-duo identity — Murphy and Goldsworthy as “The DFA” — effectively ceased. By the early 2010s the label was “pretty much solely run by Galkin,” with Murphy occupied by LCD Soundsystem touring and side interests. Galkin maintained the label’s curated catalogue around synthpop, disco, and punk while broadening the roster. (Bandcamp Daily, label profile)

The Galkin chapter itself ended in summer 2020: Murphy dismissed Galkin as a minority partner, changed the office locks, and cited the label’s “insane burn rate,” cash injections that “were supposed to be temporary loans that never were loans,” and failures toward artists. Galkin sued for breach of contract and fiduciary duty in August 2020, and subsequently founded FourFour Records, taking unfinished projects including a Black Dice album. (Far Out Magazine, 2020; NME, 2020)

What the sources do NOT establish

  • The exact year Goldsworthy formally ceased being active with DFA (2007 per NME; 2010 per Quietus/RA; still listed as managing member at March 2013 lawsuit)
  • Whether the 2013 lawsuit was settled, withdrawn, or went to judgment
  • Any direct Murphy quote explicitly naming Goldsworthy as the subject of “How Do You Sleep?”
  • Any documented creative disagreement between Murphy and Goldsworthy (all available sources frame the rupture as operational/financial)
