Ogun Records

Discogs label ID: 36211
Parent entity: Ogun Recording Ltd. (Discogs ID: 437649)
Founded: 1973, London
Founders: Harry Miller (bassist), Hazel Miller (manager), Keith Beal (sound engineer)
Country: UK
Address: 36 Duboyne Road, London NW3 2YY, England
Genre: Free jazz, free improvisation, avant-garde jazz

Named after a Yoruba deity connected with work and iron. The label’s name was chosen to reflect the South African exile community’s connections to African culture and spiritual traditions.

History

Founding and the South African exile context (1973–1978)

Ogun was founded in 1973 by South African expatriate bassist Harry Miller (born Harold Simon Miller), his wife Hazel Miller, and sound engineer Keith Beal. Its founding was inseparable from the South African exile music community that had gathered in London from the mid-1960s onward — a community centred on Chris McGregor and the Blue Notes, who had left South Africa after apartheid made their racially mixed band’s public performance legally impossible.

The label issued its first release in 1974: Live At Willisau (OG 100) by Chris McGregor’s Brotherhood Of Breath, a live recording from the Willisau Jazz Festival. That same year Harry Miller released his debut leader album Children At Play (OG 200), the second release in the catalog. The early roster interwove South African exiles — including Dudu Pukwana, Louis Moholo, Johnny Dyani, and Mongezi Feza — with core British free improvisers: Evan Parker, Mike Osborne, Elton Dean, Keith Tippett, Lol Coxhill, and Radu Malfatti, among others.

The period 1974–1979 was the label’s most intensive LP release phase. Keith Beal served as the primary recording engineer across this era, and his production credit appears on most early releases. Chris McGregor co-produced Brotherhood of Breath sessions; Harry Miller produced or co-produced his own albums. The photographer George Hallett, a South African exile himself, provided sleeve images for multiple early releases, lending the catalog a visual coherence.

Inactivity and revival (1980–1986)

When Harry and Hazel Miller relocated to the Netherlands in the early 1980s, the label effectively went dormant. Harry Miller was killed in a car accident in the Netherlands in 1983, a loss that halted the label entirely. In 1986, Hazel Miller began reactivating Ogun with new LP releases, with assistance from John Jack (ID: 461480) of Nato — the French free jazz label that had also been close to this community. This collaboration enabled the label to resume and eventually transition to CD-only releases.

CD era and ongoing catalog (1990–present)

In 1990 Hazel Miller changed all new releases to CD format. The pace of releases slowed to one to three per year, but the label remained consistent. The early 1990s brought two landmark releases under the The Dedication Orchestra — a large ensemble formed specifically to honour the South African exile musicians who had died: Spirits Rejoice (1992, OGCD 101) and Ixesha (Time) (1994, OGCD 102/103). The Dedication Orchestra was a who’s-who of the Ogun world, uniting British free improvisers with surviving South African exile musicians under the direction of Louis Moholo, Django Bates, Keith Tippett, and Mike Westbrook.

Louis Moholo (later Louis Moholo-Moholo) became the most active artist on the label from the late 1980s through the 2010s and 2020s. His various groups — Viva La Black, the Octet, Septet, and Unit — documented his continued central role in the music. As of 2025, Ogun had released over 50 catalog items, with the roster continuing to include Moholo alongside newer figures such as Alexander Hawkins.

Key Artists

South African exile community

  • Chris McGregor (ID: 285389) — pianist, composer, leader of the Blue Notes and Brotherhood of Breath. Two solo albums on Ogun: In His Good Time (1979, OG 521) and posthumous releases. Died 1990.
  • Blue Notes (ID: 168701) — McGregor’s South African band, which relocated to Europe in 1964. Ogun issued several archival recordings: Blue Notes For Mongezi (1976, OGD 001/002), Blue Notes In Concert Vol. 1 (1978, OG 220), Blue Notes For Johnny (1987, OG 532), Legacy: Live In South Afrika 1964 (1995, OGCD 007).
  • Chris McGregor’s Brotherhood Of Breath (ID: 202310) — the large-ensemble project that united South African exiles with British free jazz players. The label’s first release was their Live At Willisau (1974, OG 100). Procession (1978, OG 524) is their second Ogun studio document.
  • Louis Moholo / Louis Moholo-Moholo (ID: 314299) — drummer, the longest-running presence on the label. Recorded with the Octet (Spirits Rejoice!, 1978), the Viva La Black group (Louis Moholo’s Viva La Black, 1988; Exile, 1991), and numerous subsequent groups into the 2020s.
  • Dudu Pukwana (ID: 374059) — alto saxophonist, Blue Notes member; appears throughout the Brotherhood sessions and the Blue Notes tribute recordings.
  • Johnny Dyani (ID: 314301) — bassist, Blue Notes member; compositions appear on multiple Ogun releases. Died 1986. Subject of the Blue Notes tribute Blue Notes For Johnny.
  • Mongezi Feza (ID: 266836) — trumpeter, Blue Notes member; died 1975. The tribute album Blue Notes For Mongezi (1976) was the first memorial release.
  • Harry Miller (ID: 272603) — bassist, co-founder; Children At Play (1974, OG 200) was the second release in the catalog. Also leader of Harry Miller’s Isipingo and member of dozens of Ogun-adjacent groups.

British free improvisation scene

  • Evan Parker (ID: 42708) — soprano and tenor saxophonist; appears on Brotherhood of Breath recordings (Live At Willisau, Procession), Louis Moholo Octet (Spirits Rejoice!, 1978), the Dedication Orchestra, and as a duo with John Stevens.
  • Mike Osborne (ID: 374058) — alto saxophonist; first solo album on Ogun was Border Crossing (1974, OG 300). One of the label’s earliest and most-credited figures.
  • Elton Dean (ID: 235033) — alto saxophonist; Ogun releases with his Quartet, Ninesense, and Unlimited Saxophone Company. Appears throughout the catalog from 1977 onward.
  • Keith Tippett (ID: 235032) — pianist; Keith Tippett’s Ark released Frames (Music For An Imaginary Film) (1978, OGD 003/004), a large-ensemble double album. Also a music director for the Dedication Orchestra.
  • Lol Coxhill (ID: 60742) — soprano saxophonist; appears on Dedication Orchestra albums, and Coxhill On Ogun (1998, OGCD 008) collects earlier and new solo recordings. His engagement with Ogun spans 1977–1998.
  • John Stevens (2) (ID: 292493) — drummer; recorded with Parker on Ogun in 1977–78. See also the posthumous John Stevens releases.
  • Radu Malfatti (ID: 53818) — trombonist; appears on Brotherhood, Moholo Octet, Frames, and other early sessions.
  • Mark Charig (ID: 299473) — trumpet/cornet; recurring presence across Brotherhood sessions and Dedication Orchestra.
  • Julie Tippetts (ID: 65721) — vocalist; appears on Frames, Dedication Orchestra, and with Keith Tippett.
  • Harry Beckett (ID: 126153) — trumpet; present in Brotherhood and various Ogun sessions.
  • Kenny Wheeler (ID: 142047) — trumpet; appears on Louis Moholo Octet and Dedication Orchestra recordings.
  • Hugh Hopper (ID: 235031) — bassist; co-produced Frames, appears on Rogue Element (1978, OG 527).
  • Phil Minton (ID: 202316) — vocalist; Dedication Orchestra and various sessions.
  • Maggie Nicols (ID: 239931) — vocalist; Frames, Dedication Orchestra.

Connections to the South African Exile Community

The Blue Notes — McGregor, Pukwana, Moholo, Dyani, Feza, and others — had left South Africa in 1964 following the Johannesburg mixed-race jazz ban. They settled in London via Copenhagen and other European stops, becoming a nucleus for a broader exile scene that also drew in Harry Miller (himself South African). Ogun was in many ways the documentary archive of this community.

The 1976 double album Blue Notes For Mongezi was the first of a series of memorial recordings issued after exile musicians died. Mongezi Feza died in 1975; Johnny Dyani in 1986; Harry Miller in 1983; Chris McGregor in 1990. The 1992 and 1994 Dedication Orchestra albums — deliberately titled in honour of all the departed — are the largest-scale such memorials, assembling 30+ players to perform arrangements of the deceased musicians’ compositions. Sleeve notes for these records were written by critics including Valerie Wilmer (author of As Serious As Your Life, the essential text on the Black American and South African free jazz community in Britain) and Richard Williams.

The ANC activist and writer Z. Pallo Jordan provided sleeve notes for multiple early Ogun releases (Children At Play, Blue Notes For Mongezi), indicating the label’s direct links to the South African liberation movement in exile.

Connections to the British Free Improv Scene

While centred on the South African exile community, Ogun was also fully embedded in the British free improvisation scene. Evan Parker, John Stevens, Mike Osborne, Elton Dean, Radu Malfatti, Keith Tippett, Lol Coxhill, and Maggie Nicols were all central figures in the overlapping world that also produced Incus Records (Bailey/Parker/Oxley), the Spontaneous Music Ensemble (Stevens), and Emanem (Davidson). The difference was that Ogun’s releases consistently placed these musicians in dialogue with the South African exile tradition — rhythmically, melodically, and compositionally.

The Brotherhood of Breath represents the most sustained fusion: a large ensemble in which McGregor’s South African-rooted writing and the spontaneous energy of the exiles combined with the technical range of British free jazz players. The resulting sound, heard clearly on Live At Willisau and Procession, is distinct from either tradition alone.
