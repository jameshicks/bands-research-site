Independent record label from Manchester, UK, between 1978 and 1992.

On 19 May 1978, the TV presenter Anthony H. Wilson started The Factory, a series of club nights at the PSV/The Russel Club in Hulme, Manchester. Among the bands billed for the four opening nights were The Durutti Column, Cabaret Voltaire, and Joy Division. The poster for the evenings was designed by Peter Saville (2), who soon became a partner of Wilson and Alan Erasmus. In October 1979, producer Martin Hannett joined as a director too, followed in 1980 by Rob Gretton (manager of Joy Division and New Order) as fifth partner.

The club night was a success and led to the release of the double 7” EP "" in January 1979. This EP marked the beginnings of Factory as a record label and featured bands that played at The Factory. When Wilson told Saville that their EP would be FAC 1, Saville replied that his first poster for the club was FAC 1 and the EP would be FAC 2.

Factory set up its first headquarters in Erasmus’ flat on 86 Palatine Road, Didsbury, Manchester, where it resided until 1990 (a blue BBC plaque was erected there in June 2017). It then moved to 1 Charles Street, Manchester, and was allocated catalogue number FAC 251.

The company Factory Communications Ltd. (FCL) was officially established on 24 October 1980.

In 1981, Factory started to license releases to other labels/countries.

In October 1989, Factory launched the label Factory Classical.

Overview

Factory was an independent record label founded in Manchester, UK, that ran from 1978 to 1992. It grew out of a club night series launched by TV presenter Anthony H. Wilson at the Russel Club in Hulme, with early partnerships shared among Wilson, Alan Erasmus, designer Peter Saville, producer Martin Hannett, and manager Rob Gretton. The label’s catalogue — spanning New Wave, Post-Punk, Synth-pop, and Indie Rock — was concentrated in the 1980s and built around Joy Division and New Order, with Factory’s unconventional approach including giving artists ownership of their own recordings. By 1991 financial pressures brought the label to the brink, and it ceased operations in 1992, leaving behind a network of sublabels including Factory Classical, Factory Benelux, Factory (US), and Factory America.

Collapse (1992) — Haçienda losses and bankruptcy

Factory Communications Ltd declared bankruptcy in November 1992 with total debts exceeding £2 million. The collapse had two direct causes beyond the Haçienda: Happy Mondays’ Yes Please! sessions in Barbados consumed roughly £380,000, and New Order’s comeback album Republic cost approximately £400,000–£430,000 — both vastly over budget. A London Records/Warner Bros. deal that might have rescued the label fell through when it emerged that New Order, not Factory, owned their own back catalogue; Factory had no rights to sell.

The Haçienda (FAC 51) was a separate but financially entangled entity. Co-owned by Factory and New Order, it opened in 1982 and almost never broke even. It lost up to £50,000 a month in its early years, sustained almost entirely by New Order record sales routed through Factory. The club’s structural problem was that ecstasy culture drove drink revenue down to negligible levels even on full nights; alcohol sales are a nightclub’s primary margin. Peter Hook later claimed cumulative losses of up to £18 million over the Haçienda’s lifetime (1982–1997) — though that figure covers the full 15-year run, not just the period up to Factory’s 1992 collapse. Estimates for the pre-collapse period range from £6–9 million total, with losses of over £10,000 a month cited as a floor for most of the venue’s existence. (The Haçienda, Wikipedia, as of 2026-08-10; live4ever.uk.com)

The Haçienda’s losses drained Factory indirectly: New Order profits that should have been label income were continuously diverted to subsidise the venue, compressing Factory’s working capital over a decade before the 1992 crisis hit. The nightclub itself staggered on until 1997 before closing.

Tony Wilson’s personal assets. No source confirms that Wilson lost his house or other personal property in the bankruptcy. Wikipedia notes he “made little money from Factory Records or the Haçienda, despite the enormous popularity and cultural significance of both endeavours,” and his 2007 probate estate was valued at £484,747 (including a city-centre flat on Little Peter Street) — suggesting he was not left destitute, though not wealthy. The bankruptcy was a corporate insolvency (Factory Communications Ltd), not a personal bankruptcy against Wilson individually; as a TV presenter with a separate income, his personal exposure was limited. No biography or press source found to date names a specific asset (house, property) as lost. (Tony Wilson, Wikipedia, as of 2026-08-10)
