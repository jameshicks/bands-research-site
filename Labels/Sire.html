Sire

American record label. Discogs label ID: 27031. Parent: Warner Bros. Records / Warner Music Group (Discogs ID: 2345). Label Code: LC 3228 / LC 03228.

Overview

Sire Records was founded in 1966 by Seymour Stein (born Seymour Steinbigle) and Richard Gottehrer. Its first release was a single by The Jam (cat. S-5001, 1968). In its earliest phase Sire distributed through London Records (1968–1972), then Dot Records/Famous Music (1972), then ABC Records (1974). The label’s pre-punk identity centered on British progressive and blues rock acts licensed for the American market: Barclay James Harvest, Focus (2), Climax Blues Band, and Renaissance (4) — the latter providing Sire its first UK Top 10 hit with “Hocus Pocus.”

In 1976 Sire became one of the first labels of any scale to sign artists from the CBGB/NYC new wave scene. The initial punk/new wave signings — Ramones, Talking Heads, Richard Hell & The Voidoids, The Dead Boys, The Flamin’ Groovies — defined the label’s reputation for the following decade. The Saints (2) (Australia) and The Rezillos (Scotland) came shortly after, demonstrating Seymour Stein’s international reach even before the label had secured stable UK distribution.

In 1977 a US distribution deal was signed with Warner Bros. Records, and Warner purchased the label outright in 1980. European rights were sold to Polygram for 1976–1978 (UK releases via Phonogram, continental via Polydor); from late 1978 each key European market was separately licensed — Germany (WEA Records), UK (WEA Records), France (WEA Records), etc. In November 1981 WEA International bought all European distribution rights except Italy.

The 1980s expanded the roster into British post-punk and synth-pop: Depeche Mode, Soft Cell, The Smiths, The Cure, Echo & The Bunnymen, Yazoo, Modern English, The Pretenders, Madness, The Beat (2), Tom Tom Club. In 1982 Sire signed Madonna, who became its most commercially dominant act. In 1985 the roster included The Replacements, The Cult, and Ministry (early).

In 1995 US distribution temporarily shifted from Warner Bros./Reprise to Elektra, while Sire remained within Warner Music Group. In 2000 Sire merged with the US division of London Records to form London-Sire Records; that partnership dissolved in April 2003 and Sire was re-established as a standalone Warner imprint under Warner Bros. Records.

The Smiths: Licensing Structure (Rough Trade / Sire)

The Smiths’ US releases on Sire were the product of a label-to-label licensing arrangement, not a direct signing of the band to Sire. The structure, as established by multiple sources, was that Rough Trade Records (UK) retained the band’s recording contract and ownership of the master recordings; Rough Trade then licensed those finished masters to Sire for US (and some other non-UK) distribution.

How the deal was initiated

Seymour Stein’s own account, from a 2015 Music Week interview (paywalled; opening paragraph visible), confirms that Geoff Travis at Rough Trade telephoned him to pitch the band: “In 1983, I got a call from Rough Trade’s Geoff Travis, raving about this band The Smiths.” musicweek.com The Magnet Magazine retrospective (2006) corroborates that “Travis, for instance, brokered their deal in the U.S. with Sire Records.” magnetmagazine.com Both accounts point to Travis as the initiator and broker, consistent with a label-to-label deal rather than a direct band signing.

The licensing vs. direct-signing question — primary source confirmed

Morrissey’s Autobiography (Penguin Classics, 2013) contains the primary account of the New York meeting and deal. The exact language from the book, recovered from a search snippet quoting the text directly:

“Geoff shuttles the band to New York (in row 62, cattle class), where Seymour Stein awaits with a deal to sign the Smiths (ostensibly) to Sire Records. Seymour is in fact signing Rough Trade for licensing access to the Smiths.”

The parenthetical “ostensibly” is Morrissey’s own qualifier — he explicitly frames the band-level signing as the surface presentation, and the Rough Trade licensing deal as the substantive reality. Later in the book Morrissey quotes Stein as saying “We have no idea how you’re selling so many tickets!” and glosses this as confirmation that Sire “had done nothing at all to pitch the Smiths in the US,” consistent with a label that had acquired finished masters by license rather than developed the act through its own A&R. (Source: search snippet quoting Autobiography text — the specific page numbers are not given in online sources; the relevant passage covers the late 1983 New York trip / Seymour Stein meeting.)

Wikipedia’s Smiths article uses the phrase “The Smiths signed with the American record label Sire Records in late 1983” and recounts that Stein agreed to buy Marr a 1960 Gibson ES-355 as a deal sweetener — language that frames it as a band-level signing. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Smiths (as of 2026-10-09)

Conflict note (resolved to the degree possible without the physical contract): Wikipedia’s “signed with Sire” phrasing and Morrissey’s “ostensibly” / “in fact signing Rough Trade” are in tension. Morrissey’s first-person account is the closer primary source: the deal was structurally a Rough Trade licensing arrangement, with any band-facing paperwork being nominal. The contractual signatory on the Sire side was almost certainly Rough Trade Records Ltd. as licensor, not The Smiths as a directly-signed act — but no copy of the actual contract has surfaced in any accessible source to confirm the signatory names in the document itself.

Weight of evidence: The preponderance of sourced accounts supports the finished-master licensing model. No source documents Sire providing a separate recording budget, separate producer approval, or any A&R input into how Smiths albums were made. All Smiths recordings on Sire (US) were produced under Rough Trade’s direction in the UK and released to Sire as completed masters.

No separate Sire A&R relationship

No source found in this research locates any Sire-specific A&R activity for The Smiths: no separate Sire recording sessions, no Sire-appointed producer, no Sire creative input on any of the four studio albums or the compilations (Hatful of Hollow, Louder Than Bombs). The US album credits on Sire pressings reproduce the Rough Trade credits without addition or amendment, which is consistent with delivered finished masters rather than a co-production arrangement.

Sire and the Phil Spector Sessions (End of the Century, 1979–1980)

Background

End of the Century (Sire SRK 6077, released February 4, 1980) was the Ramones’ fifth studio LP and their most expensive by a wide margin. Their debut had cost $6,400; Road to Ruin (1978) remained in four figures. Sire authorized a production budget of approximately $200,000 for Phil Spector — roughly 30 times what the band’s earlier records had cost. Marky Ramone’s memoir Punk Rock Blitzkrieg: My Life as a Ramone (2015) describes Stein paying Spector “on the order of a quarter million dollars to produce it and put the band over the top” (Rolling Stone excerpt: source).

Label rationale

Sire and Stein approved the Spector collaboration explicitly seeking commercial crossover after four albums that had built critical standing and a devoted following but had not broken through to mass sales. The label was, in the words of one account, “eager for some long-awaited ROI on the band” (moondogmotel.substack.com). The gamble had a contractual dimension: Spector’s deal stipulated he would produce two albums for the Ramones, but neither Spector nor the label exercised the option for a second record — the collaboration ended after this single project (moondogmotel.substack.com).

Stein’s awareness of the sessions

Stein was present enough during the recording to witness or be informed of Spector’s methods in detail. His most direct public statement about the sessions — and the only one confirmed by a primary source (Wikipedia’s End of the Century article, which cites it) — concerns Spector forcing Johnny Ramone to repeat his guitar part for “Rock ‘n’ Roll High School” for hours on end: “To Johnny, this must have been like the Chinese water torture” (Wikipedia, End of the Century). The quote acknowledges full awareness of the conditions but carries no suggestion that Stein intervened over Spector’s session conduct itself.

Did Sire intervene?

The clearest claim of label intervention concerns the mixing phase rather than the recording sessions. A 2014 review by Post Punk Monk states that “$700,000 was plowed into the album before Sire Prez Seymour Stein pulled the plug and stopped the endless remixing that Spector was wont to indulge in” (postpunkmonk.com, 2014). The Post Punk Monk reviewer attributes this to “Joey Ramone reported” — meaning the reviewer is relaying a claim made by Joey Ramone, not inventing it — but gives no citation for where Joey Ramone made the statement (no interview, book, or liner-note reference is named).

Outcome

End of the Century became the Ramones’ best-selling album, reaching #14 in the UK and #44 on the Billboard 200. The single “Baby, I Love You” reached the UK Top 10. Whatever the cost overruns, the commercial result was the strongest of the band’s career to that point — though whether production costs were recouped is unconfirmed. Sire proceeded to release Pleasant Dreams (1981) without Spector, produced by Graham Gouldman.
