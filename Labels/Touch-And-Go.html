Touch And Go Records

American independent record label. Discogs ID: 819. Parent entity: Touch And Go Records, Inc. (ID 319752). P.O. Box 25520, Chicago, IL 60625.

Founded 1981 in Lansing, Michigan by Tesco Vee and Dave Stimson as an outgrowth of Touch And Go fanzine, which the duo had been publishing to document their local hardcore punk scene. Necros bassist Corey Rusk joined shortly after to help run the label; after Vee moved to Washington DC in 1983, Rusk assumed full control. In 1987 Rusk moved the label to Chicago, Illinois. In February 2009 Rusk announced the label would be cutting back on activity — ceasing new signings and ending distribution services for other labels — citing the economic climate.

Identity: hardcore punk in early years (1981–1986), shifting to noise rock, post-punk, math rock, and experimental music through the 1990s and 2000s. Home to some of the most influential US underground records of the 1980s–2000s, including Big Black’s Songs About Fucking, Slint’s Spiderland, and Shellac’s entire catalog.

History

Lansing Hardcore Origins (1981–1986)

Tesco Vee and Dave Stimson launched Touch And Go as a 7” label tied to their fanzine of the same name. The earliest releases document the Michigan/Midwest hardcore scene of the early 1980s: Necros (I.Q. 32, T&G #3, 1981), The Fix (Jan’s Rooms, T&G 5, 1981), Negative Approach (Negative Approach, T&G 7, 1982), Meatmen (Blood Sausage EP, T&G 6, 1982). Corey Rusk (bassist in Necros) became a key operational partner early on.

Tesco Vee departed for Washington DC in 1983; Rusk took sole control. The label continued through the mid-1980s with Midwestern hardcore and early noise acts. The Virgin Prunes (Ireland) appeared in 1986 (T&G LP9C), an early sign of the label’s willingness to look beyond the immediate regional scene.

Chicago Move and Noise Rock Peak (1987–1993)

Rusk relocated Touch And Go to Chicago in 1987. This period marks the label’s most celebrated era, coinciding with the rise of noise rock and the AmRep/Chicago underground:

  • Big Black — Steve Albini’s band was already on the label before the move; Songs About Fucking (TG #24, 1987) became a defining album of the genre. Posthumous compilations followed.
  • Butthole Surfers — Texas psych-noise; on the label from Psychic… Powerless… Another Man’s Sac (1984) through Hairway To Steven (1988).
  • Scratch Acid — Austin noise rock; Berserker EP (T&G #18, 1987); The Greatest Gift retrospective (1991).
  • Rapeman — Albini’s post-Big Black trio; Two Nuns And A Pack Mule (TG LP #36, 1988).
  • Shellac — Albini’s third and ongoing band; began with The Rude Gesture (A Pictorial History) (TG 123, 1993); all subsequent albums on the label.
  • Killdozer — Wisconsin noise rock; six albums 1985–1994.
  • Slint — Louisville/Chicago; Spiderland (T&G LP#64, 1991); Tweez reissued 1993; Untitled 10” (TG132, 1994); all reissues and box sets through 2024.
  • The Jesus Lizard — Chicago noise rock; all major albums 1990–2000 (T&G debut: Chrome EP 1989).
  • Urge Overkill — Chicago; Jesus Urge Superstar through Stull EP (1989–1992).
  • Die Kreuzen — Milwaukee hardcore/post-hardcore; five albums 1984–1994.
  • Tar — Chicago noise rock; Clincher (1993), Toast (1993).
  • Don Caballero — Pittsburgh math rock; For Respect (1993) through American Don (2000).

The Nirvana / Jesus Lizard split 7” (Puss / Oh, The Guilt, TG83, February 1993) was a high-profile moment; Nirvana’s sole Touch And Go release. The single’s origin traced back to a show Nirvana and the Jesus Lizard played together at Maxwell’s in Hoboken, NJ — around 1990 or 1991, when Chad Channing was still Nirvana’s drummer and Nevermind had not yet been released. David Yow recalled it as the first time he had seen or met Nirvana; he was immediately impressed, saying the next day he and drummer Mac McNeilly were singing “In Bloom,” a song they had never heard before. That same night, standing in the hallway between the venue and the restaurant at Maxwell’s, Cobain and Yow discussed doing a split single — modelled on how Sub Pop had released a split with Sonic Youth and Mudhoney, but on Touch And Go instead. (Nirvana had their own prior connection to T&G: Cobain had sent demo copies to the label back in 1988.) Nirvana then signed to Geffen and Nevermind came out, which complicated matters. Geffen “begrudgingly” permitted a very limited release. The Jesus Lizard, meanwhile, debated the optics: as Yow put it, “we had to figure out, well, do we want to do this and look like we’re riding on Nirvana’s coattails, or we could just do it and not worry about it, which is what we ended up doing.” The single sold strongly and reached #12 on the UK singles chart — the Jesus Lizard’s only UK chart entry and Nirvana’s sixth. — David Yow in Village Voice, November 2011; Grammy.com, September 2023.

Post-Rock and Broadening (1993–2002)

  • Girls Against Boys — New York; Venus Luxure No.1 Baby (TG117, 1993) through House Of GVSB (1996).
  • Polvo — Chapel Hill noise rock / post-rock; Cor-Crane Secret (1992) through Shapes (1997).
  • Blonde Redhead — New York art rock; Fake Can Be Just As Good (1997) through Melody Of Certain Damaged Lemons (2000) on T&G.
  • Dirty Three — Melbourne instrumental post-rock; Dirty Three (TG147, 1995) through She Has No Strings Apollo (2002).
  • The Black Heart Procession — San Diego; 2 through The Spell (1999–2006).
  • The For Carnation — Louisville; self-titled LP (TG214, 2000); Promised Works retrospective (2007).
  • Storm And Stress — Pittsburgh free jazz/noise; 1997–2000.
  • Brainiac — Dayton, Ohio; Internationale (1995) through Electro-Shock For President (1997).
  • Man Or Astro-Man? — Alabama surf/sci-fi; multiple albums 1996–2000.
  • Bedhead — Dallas slowcore; reissues 2001.

2003–2008: Indie Expansion and Wind-Down

  • TV On The Radio — Brooklyn; Young Liars EP (2003) through Dear Science (2008); one of their last major signings.
  • CocoRosie — New York/Paris; La Maison De Mon Rêve (2004) through God Has A Voice (2008).
  • !!! — Sacramento dance-punk; Louden Up Now (2004) through Myth Takes (2007).
  • Yeah Yeah Yeahs — NYC; early EP (2002) and Machine single only.
  • Pinback — San Diego; Offcell (2003), Summer In Abaddon (2004), Autumn Of The Seraphs (2007).
  • Nina Nastasia — New York folk; The Blackened Air (2002) through Dogs (2004).
  • Ted Leo / Pharmacists — Living With The Living (2007) and related singles.
  • Calexico / Iron And Wine — In The Reins split (TG290, 2005).
  • Sally Timms — In The World Of Him (TG269, 2004); also a Touch And Go roster artist (Mekons connection).

In February 2009 Rusk announced the label would scale back significantly, ceasing new signings and ending its distribution services for other labels (Merge, Drag City, Kill Rock Stars, Jade Tree, and others), which had been a significant part of the business. Crystal Antlers’ Tentacles (April 2009) was the final new release on the label for the foreseeable future.

Quarterstick Records

Sublabel (ID 2507), also based in Chicago, same address and phone as T&G. Founded to release quieter, more indie-folk oriented material that didn’t fit the core T&G aesthetic. Home to The Black Heart Procession, Bedhead, The New Year, Dirty Three (some releases), Seam, Red Stars Theory. Has its own Bandcamp and social accounts.

2009 shutdown — new signings and distribution (web research)

The question of whether Touch and Go ceased new signings in 2008 as a separate prior action from the February 2009 distribution announcement is answered by the contemporary press coverage: no. All coverage from February 2009 treats the two as a single simultaneous announcement.

  • Billboard: “Touch & Go To Cease New Signings, Distribution” — headline explicitly names both actions together; February 2009.
  • Newcity Music: “Touch and Go to cut distribution” (February 18, 2009) — Corey Rusk statement: “the current state of the economy has reached the point where we can no longer afford to continue this lesser known, yet important part of Touch and Go’s operations.” The piece frames the label as “returning to its roots and focusing solely on being an independent record label” — suggesting it would continue releasing existing artists’ music (Shellac, Jesus Lizard, Ted Leo) rather than signing new ones.
  • Chicago Sun-Times: “End of an era: Touch and Go Records will stop distributing smaller indie labels” (February 18, 2009) — same date; headline focused on distribution of other labels; article body not accessible.
  • Crystal Antlers’ Tentacles (April 7, 2009) is documented as the final new release on the label, per Wikipedia’s Tentacles article.

No source found documenting a distinct 2008 decision to stop new signings that preceded the February 2009 announcement. The vault’s earlier text “In 2008 Rusk announced…” was an error; corrected to February 2009 throughout.

Steve Albini’s relationship with the label — beyond artist and engineer

Question: Did Albini hold any A&R, curatorial, or advisory role at Touch And Go beyond being a recording artist on the label?

Answer: No formal role is documented. The relationship was close but defined by three functions — musician (Big Black, Rapeman, Shellac), recording engineer for other T&G bands, and personal friend of Corey Rusk — not by any operational or A&R capacity.

The primary source is Corey Rusk’s memorial statement published on the Touch And Go website on May 7, 2024, the day after Albini died. Rusk’s full text reads in part:

“He was a member of more bands on Touch and Go than any other musician. He played on more Touch and Go releases than anyone else. He recorded more bands on Touch and Go than any other engineer. He was a cherished friend of mine and a champion of my record label for 40 years. … We signed new bands, and Steve recorded some of them. Once Steve’s dream studio, Electrical, was completed, our bands started recording there.”

The phrase “We signed new bands, and Steve recorded some of them” is the most direct statement of how the relationship worked: Rusk made signing decisions; Albini’s role in that process was as engineer of the resulting sessions, not as talent scout or selector. Rusk describes Albini as a “champion of my record label” — an advocate in public, not an internal A&R operator.

The Stop Smiling Magazine 25th anniversary piece (2006, stopsmilingonline.com/story_detail.php?id=652) contains a quote from Albini consistent with this: “I’ve handed some recordings to him, but he’s the one who falls for them ultimately.” This confirms that Albini occasionally passed recordings to Rusk’s attention — an informal recommender function — but explicitly frames Rusk as the sole decision-maker. The Stop Smiling URL returned a connection error during this research session; the quote is drawn from search-engine snippet text that attributed it to that piece.

The “unofficial A&R rep” claim: Multiple search-engine result summaries characterize Albini as having “acted as an unofficial A&R rep for Touch and Go, passing on sessions by artists like Nastasia.” This claim could not be confirmed from any directly readable primary source. The Nastasia case (Albini engineered Dogs on Socialist Records in 1999–2000; T&G later signed Nastasia and issued The Blackened Air in 2002) is consistent with Albini having informally flagged her work to Rusk, but no interview with Nastasia, Albini, or Rusk retrieved during this session explicitly states that Albini recommended her to T&G. The Wikipedia articles for both Dogs and The Blackened Air attribute the signing to T&G’s interest following her debut’s word-of-mouth traction and John Peel airplay, with no mention of Albini as intermediary.

Summary: Albini’s relationship with Touch And Go was as deep as any artist’s relationship with an independent label could be — 40 years, three bands, engineering work across the roster, and genuine personal friendship with Rusk — but no formal advisory, curatorial, or A&R role is documented in any primary source. The informal passing of recordings to Rusk’s attention is confirmed by one Albini quote, framed explicitly as Rusk’s call to make.

Sources:

  • Corey Rusk, “Remembering Steve Albini,” Touch And Go Records (May 7, 2024)
  • Stop Smiling Magazine, “A look back at 25 years of Touch and Go” (2006) — URL returned connection error; Albini quote sourced via search-engine snippet attribution
  • Wikipedia: The Blackened Air (Nina Nastasia) — no mention of Albini as intermediary in signing
  • Wikipedia: Dogs (Nina Nastasia) — signing attributed to word-of-mouth and John Peel airplay

Tesco Vee’s ownership stake after the 1983 handover

Question: Did Tesco Vee retain any ownership stake in Touch and Go Records after handing it to Corey Rusk in 1983?

Answer: No. The handover was a clean transfer with no retained financial interest. Tesco Vee has stated this explicitly, and no source across multiple interviews and historical accounts documents any ongoing ownership stake, royalty arrangement, or equity position.

The most direct evidence is a Chunklet interview with Tesco Vee published around 2010 to coincide with the release of Touch and Go: The Complete Hardcore Punk Zine ‘79–‘83 (Bazillion Points). When asked (in substance) whether he had anticipated the label becoming so successful, Vee replied: “No way… or like I said I would’ve kept a percentage!” The remark is self-deprecating but unambiguous: he did not negotiate any financial stake at the time and is clear that he did not retain one. The Chunklet URL (https://www.chunklet.com/interview-tesco-vee-and-touch-go-magazine-30-years-later/) returned a 404 during this research session; the quote is drawn from a search-engine snippet that directly attributed it to that interview. The quote’s substance is consistent with every other source reviewed.

Supporting context from other sources:

  • Wikipedia (Tesco Vee article, as of 2026-08-08): “After handing Touch and Go over to Rusk in 1983, Tesco moved to Washington, DC, and began to focus on his own punk band.” No ownership stake is mentioned. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesco_Vee
  • Wikipedia (Touch and Go Records article, as of 2026-08-08): States Vee “handed Touch and Go over to Rusk and his wife Lisa” in 1983; no retained interest mentioned. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touch_and_Go_Records
  • Stereogum obituary for Dave Stimson (2024): Notes that when Vee departed for DC in late 1982/1983, “Rusk and his future wife Lisa Pfahler ended up running the label”; Vee reconnected with the label’s history only “when the book came on in 2010.” No financial relationship described. https://stereogum.com/2504205/touch-go-co-founder-dave-stimson-has-died/news/
  • Chicago Punk Database (Corey Rusk entry): States Rusk has been “the owner of Touch and Go Records” since 1982, with no co-owner or prior-owner financial interest mentioned. https://punkdatabase.com/wiki/Corey_Rusk
  • Mark Prindle interview with Tesco Vee (markprindle.com/vee-i.htm): Contains the line “thank god for Touch and Go! They are honest, and have integrity and are a rare bird in an industry wasteland” — framing the label in the third person as something separate from him, not as an entity with which he has financial entanglement.

Multiple further interviews (Aesthetics for Birds 2014, Antigravity Magazine, RVA Mag) contain no mention of a retained ownership stake.

Summary: The 1983 transfer was effectively a gift — Vee handed the label name and operation to Rusk without negotiating equity or royalties. Vee’s own quoted words confirm he received nothing and, in retrospect, acknowledged he should have kept a percentage if he had known what the label would become. The Connections table entry for Corey Rusk in this file — “sole owner 1983–2008” — is accurate.

Nirvana / Jesus Lizard split single origin (Puss / Oh, The Guilt, TG83, 1993)

Question: How did this split single come about — whose idea was it, and what was the context for pairing Nirvana with the Jesus Lizard?

Answer: The idea originated between Kurt Cobain and David Yow at a show the two bands played together at Maxwell’s in Hoboken, NJ, at some point in 1990 or 1991, before Nevermind was released and while Chad Channing was still Nirvana’s drummer.

David Yow recounted the origin directly in a 2011 Village Voice interview: “We didn’t really tour with them. Over the years, we had three shows with them; none of it was really a tour. Once in Hoboken at Maxwell’s [in ‘90 or ‘91]…” He described it as the first time he had ever seen or met Nirvana, and said he was immediately struck: the next morning, he and Mac McNeilly were both singing “In Bloom” — a song they had only heard once the night before — unprompted. “I was just so impressed because I wasn’t that familiar with Nirvana and I knew it was Seattle and grunge and I don’t like Soundgarden and I didn’t care much for Mudhoney… But I thought [Nirvana] were fuckin’ great.” That same night, Yow continued: “it was that night, standing in the hallway that led between the venue and the restaurant [at Maxwell’s], Kurt and I talked about doing a split single. You know, a few years later… it was a while, but yeah.” — Village Voice, Brad Cohan interview, November 4, 2011, https://www.villagevoice.com/q-a-scratch-acids-david-yow-on-reunions-nirvana-and-book-the-jesus-lizard-coffee-table-book/

The split was modelled on the Sub Pop format — specifically the precedent of Sub Pop releasing a split with Sonic Youth and Mudhoney — but the two bands chose Touch And Go as the label, since the Jesus Lizard were already on the roster there. Nirvana had their own prior connection to Touch And Go: Kurt Cobain had sent demo copies to the label in 1988, before Nirvana had a deal. — Grammy.com, Gillian Gaar, September 21, 2023, https://www.grammy.com/news/why-1993-was-nirvanas-biggest-year-in-utero-concert-footage-videos-kurt-cobain/

The complication was that Nirvana subsequently signed to Geffen and Nevermind became a mainstream phenomenon. Geffen “begrudgingly” permitted a very limited release. — midnight punk blog, https://midnightpunk.wordpress.com/2012/06/05/the-jesus-lizard-puss-nirvana-oh-the-guilt/ (secondary source; claim consistent with release history and distribution scarcity)

The Jesus Lizard debated whether to proceed given the size of the gap in fame between the two bands by 1992. Yow: “We had to figure out, well, do we want to do this and look like we’re riding on Nirvana’s coattails, or we could just do it and not worry about it, which is what we ended up doing.” — Grammy.com, Gillian Gaar, September 21, 2023 (quoting Yow); also cited in Wikipedia’s article on the single as of 2026-08-08, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puss_/_Oh,_the_Guilt

Key facts established:

  • Idea: originated between Cobain and Yow at Maxwell’s, Hoboken, c. 1990/91 — it was their idea jointly, proposed by Cobain in conversation with Yow the same night they first met
  • Label: Touch And Go chosen because the Jesus Lizard were already on the roster; Nirvana had their own 1988 demo-sending history with T&G
  • Complication: Geffen signed Nirvana, Nevermind changed their status, Geffen permitted only a limited release
  • Jesus Lizard’s deliberation: they weighed the “coattails” optics and decided to proceed regardless
  • Result: released February 1993, TG83; reached #12 UK singles chart; notable distribution scarcity despite demand

Primary source: David Yow, Village Voice interview, November 4, 2011 — direct quotes about Maxwell’s, the hallway conversation, and first impressions of Nirvana. Secondary source for coattails quote: Grammy.com / Gillian Gaar, 2023, https://www.grammy.com/news/why-1993-was-nirvanas-biggest-year-in-utero-concert-footage-videos-kurt-cobain/ Note: No direct statement from Corey Rusk or Touch And Go about brokering the arrangement was found. The framing from all available sources places the initiative with Cobain and Yow directly; Rusk’s role was as the label that released it, not as the party who proposed the pairing.
