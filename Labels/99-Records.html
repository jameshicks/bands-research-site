99 Records

Location: 99 MacDougal Street, Greenwich Village, New York City 10012 Active: 1980–1985 (final release 1985; store closed early 1986) Discogs: Label ID 147578 Publishing: Nine Nine Music (ASCAP) Phone (original): (212) 777-4610

The central label of the NYC post-punk / No Wave / funk-punk moment. 14 catalogue entries across five years, almost no dead weight. The label emerged from a record store at 99 MacDougal Street — the same physical address — and collapsed after a lawsuit victory that yielded no money.

Key Figures

Ed Bahlman — founder and sole producer. Began selling rare British indie imports from a corner of Gina Franklyn’s boutique at 99 MacDougal, using Rough Trade connections. Glenn Branca urged him to start releasing records; the first release was Branca’s own “Lesson No. 1” (99-01, 1980). Bahlman produced every 99 release except the Branca EP (Mark Bingham / Branca) and the ESG A-side (Martin Hannett). After the label collapsed he withdrew entirely — refuses all interviews, has withheld master tapes from artists at any price, making authorised reissues impossible. Discogs ID: 54478. See Ed Bahlman.

Gina Franklyn — British designer; opened the “99” boutique at 99 MacDougal in the mid-to-late 1970s. The store became a downtown scene hub. She was the visual brain of the label: sleeve design, layout, and photography on at least 8 releases. See Gina Franklyn.

Howie Weinberg — mastering engineer at Masterdisk / Europadisk. Mastered virtually every 99 release; his runout stamps (“HW”) are primary authenticity markers. See Howie Weinberg.

Don Hünerberg — tape/recording engineer on most sessions from 1981 onward, including all Liquid Liquid studio work. See Don Hünerberg.

Context

Origins

The label operated out of the same building as the 99 MacDougal Street record store. Gina Franklyn had opened a British fashion boutique there; Ed Bahlman carved out a corner to sell UK indie imports (via Rough Trade). Glenn Branca, a regular, pushed him to start a label. Branca’s “Lesson No. 1” EP (99-01, 1980) was the result.

ESG / Factory Co-release

Tony Wilson heard ESG opening for A Certain Ratio and signed them to Factory. Martin Hannett produced three studio tracks at Eastern Artists, New Jersey, mixed at Strawberry Studios, Manchester. Factory issued FAC 34 in the UK; Bahlman added three live cuts from Hurrah (NYC) for the US 12”. The 99-04 EP and FAC 34 are the same physical object carrying both catalog numbers. ESG’s debut on Rated X Records (“Moody / Starchild,” 1981) is a separate, earlier release.

”White Lines” / Liquid Liquid / Sugar Hill

The Optimo EP (99-11, 1983) contains “Cavern” (bass: Richard McGuire; drums: Scott Hartley; marimba/roto-toms: Dennis Young; vox/perc: Salvatore Principato). Sugar Hill Records’ house band re-performed the bassline — did not sample it — for Grandmaster Flash & Melle Mel’s “White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It)” (1983). Bahlman sued Sugar Hill. The court found in 99’s favour and awarded $660,000 in damages. Sugar Hill declared bankruptcy on 22 November 1985 before paying anything. 99 Records collected nothing, having spent approximately $60,000 in legal costs — which drained the store. Liquid Liquid had already split before the case resolved. See Liquid Liquid, Sugar Hill Records (hip-hop), Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five.

Glenn Branca / Neutral Records Split

When Branca brought Sonic Youth to Bahlman for signing, Bahlman declined. Branca departed 99 and founded Neutral Records to release Sonic Youth’s debut and his own subsequent work. 99-01 and 99-001LP (The Ascension) were his only 99 releases.

The Ascension — Recording and Ensemble Context

The Ascension (99-001LP, 1981) was recorded at The Power Station, a studio in Hell’s Kitchen, Manhattan. The group gained access at little cost because bassist Jeffrey Glenn had a personal connection to the studio’s owner and engineers. Specific session dates within 1981 are not documented in available sources. Engineer and mixer: James Farber; mastering: Howie Weinberg. source: Wikipedia, The Ascension (Glenn Branca album), as of 2026-06-05; Superior Viaduct product page

The ensemble had been touring through 1980 following Branca’s debut EP (Lesson No. 1, 99-01) before entering the studio. The four-guitarist lineup — Branca, Lee Ranaldo, Ned Sublette, and David Rosenbloom — was assembled to explore resonances generated when guitar strings tuned to the same pitch were played at high volumes. All four are credited as electric guitarists; there is no differentiation of individual parts in available sources (liner notes not accessible). source: The Wire essay

Lee Ranaldo — At the time of recording, Ranaldo was not yet in Sonic Youth (formed 1981). He came to Branca’s orbit through the downtown avant-garde scene; Thurston Moore first saw Ranaldo performing in Branca’s guitar ensemble at the Noise Fest in June 1981. Ranaldo played mainly electric guitar in Branca’s ensembles, including some of the harmonic guitars Branca designed and built. After The Ascension sessions he co-founded Sonic Youth with Moore and Kim Gordon, all of whom continued working with Branca into 1984. source: Wikipedia, Lee Ranaldo; Red Bull Music Academy interview

Ned Sublette — Sublette (born 1951, New Mexico) had moved to New York in 1976 and was active as a composer and guitarist in the downtown experimental scene, working with John Cage, LaMonte Young, and others alongside his work with Branca. He is credited as one of the “fellow composers” in the Ascension band, distinguishing him and David Rosenbloom from Ranaldo as composer-performers rather than sidemen. After the Branca period, Sublette moved away from rock-adjacent work toward country-Afro-Caribbean fusion and became a leading scholar of Cuban music; his connection to the No Wave / guitar-orchestra scene of 1980–81 is a relatively underdocumented chapter. source: Wikipedia, Ned Sublette; Lovely Music bio

Vivien Goldman — “Dirty Washing”

Goldman was a UK music journalist (NME, Sounds) with deep PiL and post-punk connections. Her “Dirty Washing” EP is a postcard from that world: Keith Levene (PiL) on guitar, Robert Wyatt on percussion, Steve Beresford on toy piano, Vicky Aspinall (The Raincoats) on violin. Design by Dave Hucker and Jean-Bernard Sohiez.

Maximum Joy / Y Records — US Licence and Reciprocal Exchange Deal

The 99-08EP (“Stretch”, 1981) was a US licence from Y Records (Bristol), not a direct 99 signing. The mechanism was a reciprocal exchange agreement brokered between Ed Bahlman and Dick O’Dell, who ran Y Records and managed Pigbag.

Bahlman had become a fan of Pigbag’s “Papa’s Got A Brand New Pigbag” single and wanted to release it in the States. The deal as it was conceived: 99 would release Y artists in the USA; Y would provide UK distribution / release of 99 artists (Liquid Liquid being the intended beneficiary on the 99 side). According to Terry Tolkin (99 associate), by the time the arrangement was finalised, so many import copies of the first Pigbag single had already sold in the US that Bahlman decided to wait for the follow-up. When he received the tape of the second Pigbag single, he did not like it and declined to release it. He had, however, already committed to releasing a record by O’Dell’s own band — Maximum Joy — to facilitate the deal. The result was “Stretch” (99-08EP), recorded at Berry St. Studios, London, 8–9 July 1981, produced by Dick O’Dell with Maximum Joy and John Walker.

The Y side of the exchange was never fulfilled. No Liquid Liquid release on Y Records or via Y/Rough Trade is documented; no 99 artist received a UK release through Y as a result of the deal. Maximum Joy’s “Stretch” was the sole product of the arrangement.

The release is therefore correctly characterised as a US licence of a UK-produced record (Y Records / Rough Trade orbit → 99 Records for North America), not a direct 99 commission. The UK release of “Stretch” was on Y Records (distributed by Rough Trade). The 99-08EP pressing covers the US market only.

source: The Vinyl Factory, “Something Like A Phenomenon: The complete 99 Records story” — Terry Tolkin quoted directly on the Pigbag / Y Records exchange mechanics. source: Maximum Joy Bandcamp — recording details (Berry St. Studios, July 1981).

Singers & Players — War Of Words

On-U Sound collective produced by Adrian Sherwood; the 99-002LP was their US release. The record features a remarkable cast: Bim Sherman, Prince Far I, Jah Woosh, Ari-Up (The Slits) on vocals; Keith Levene on guitar; Style Scott (Roots Radics) on drums; Dennis Bovell as recording engineer.

The Congos — “At The Feast / Music Maker”

The 99-06EP solves the apparent gap in the catalog sequence. The Congos (Cedric Myton and Congo Ashanti Roy) were a Jamaican roots reggae duo; their US release on 99 was engineered and mixed by Boris Gardiner, with Carlton “Santa” Davis on drums. Credited as “Congo” on the label.

Counterfeits

“99 Records (3)” on Discogs catalogs counterfeit editions. Primary tells: no Masterdisk or Europadisk runout stamps; thin/standard card sleeves instead of heavy cardstock; the ‘99’ logo circles-within-nines are slightly larger than on originals; colors on labels fade toward the edges. Optimo and the ESG and Liquid Liquid EPs are most frequently faked.

Legacy

DFA Records founders cite 99 as a foundational influence. LCD Soundsystem chose Liquid Liquid to open their Madison Square Garden farewell show. ESG have been sampled by Public Enemy, Wu-Tang Clan, Beastie Boys, and TLC; their records fed into Detroit techno via Carl Craig. Glasgow’s Optimo Music (JD Twitch + JG Wilkes) named themselves after the “Cavern”-era EP.

Individual artist reissues exist (Light In The Attic for ESG and Liquid Liquid; ROIR for Bush Tetras) but a comprehensive official label retrospective is impossible: Bahlman holds the master tapes and will not license them at any price.
