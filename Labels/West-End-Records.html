West End Records

Discogs ID: 1325
Founded: December 1976
Location: New York City, NY (multiple addresses: 254 W 54th St 1976–1979; 250 W 57th St 1979–1990; 60 Carmine St 1990–1998; later midtown/Chelsea addresses through 2015+)
Founders: Mel Cheren (Melvin Cheren, 1933–2007) and Ed Kushins
Parent (current): Verse Music Group LLC → BMG Rights Management (2015–present)
Sublabels: West End Blue, West End Xprs. (ID 78618), Solid Old Gold (ID 939216), Spire Records (ID 344724), West End Tracks (ID 2249845)

Classic NYC disco and early dance label. One of the defining independent labels of the late 1970s New York dance scene, with a catalog stretching from 1976 to the present under successive ownership structures. The label’s golden era — roughly 1978 to 1984 — produced some of the most significant 12” dance records of the period, circulated primarily through the Paradise Garage and the DJ/record-pool economy Mel Cheren had helped build at Scepter Records.

Overview

West End launched in December 1976 with Mel Cheren and Ed Kushins at the helm. Cheren had come from Scepter, where he was among the first to release extended instrumental mixes on 12” B-sides and had co-founded one of the first record pools (with David Mancuso, Vince Aletti, and Steve D’Aquisto). He understood the DJ economy from the supply side before most labels knew there was an economy to understand.

The label’s relationship with the Paradise Garage — the defining NYC club from roughly 1977 to 1987 — was structural, not incidental. Cheren was the club’s financial backer; his former partner Michael Brody owned and operated the venue; and resident DJ Larry Levan maintained a creative relationship with West End that shaped how the label’s records sounded. Levan remixed, mixed, and produced a substantial portion of West End’s core catalog. His DJ sets at the Garage functioned as the primary promotional vehicle: a record that worked at the Garage circulated from there through the national DJ pool network.

The label was reactivated in 1998, reduced its presence after Cheren’s death in December 2007, then reactivated again. Currently operates under BMG Rights Management (since 2015).

Discogs profile notes that West End’s label design inspired the creation of East End, East End (2), and Bitter End Records.

Key People

Mel Cheren — Co-Founder, CEO (Discogs ID: 458298)

Melvin Cheren (1933–2007). Career began 1959 at ABC-Paramount; rose to head of production at Scepter Records before co-founding West End. Financial backer of the Paradise Garage. Producer and executive producer on dozens of West End releases. AIDS activist; founded the 24 Hours For Life nonprofit in 1987. Died December 7, 2007. Published memoir Keep On Dancin’: My Life and the Paradise Garage (2000). See Mel Cheren for full biography.

Ed Kushins — Co-Founder (People/Ed Kushins)

Co-founded West End in December 1976 alongside Cheren. See Ed Kushins.

Larry Levan — Mixer, Producer, DJ (Discogs ID: 3483)

Born Lawrence Philpot, 1954, Brooklyn; died November 8, 1992, Manhattan. Resident DJ at the Paradise Garage 1977–1987. Mixed or produced at least 44 releases across the West End catalog (Discogs credits summary). Kenton Nix — Producer, Songwriter, Arranger (Discogs ID: 48722)

Born July 13, 1954, Tennessee. The label’s dominant in-house creative force. Producer, arranger, and primary writer on the bulk of Taana Gardner’s West End output, as well as Ednah Holt, Sparque, and others. Discogs credits him as producer on 83 releases across the West End catalog — more than any other figure. Co-executive produced the 1999 Larry Levan retrospective alongside Cheren. Publishing company: Kenix Productions / Kenix Music. Also credited as “Kenix Music” or “Y. Dah” (aliases).

Arthur Russell — Producer, Writer (Discogs ID: 23524)

Iowa-born cellist, composer, and producer. Produced Loose Joints’ “Is It All Over My Face?” and “Pop Your Funk” (1980) with Steve D’Aquisto. Written-by credit on 10 West End releases. The Loose Joints 12” is West End’s most historically discussed release — one of the key documents of the downtown/club crossover. See Arthur Russell for full biography.

François Kevorkian — Editor, Mixer (Discogs ID: 20662)

Uncredited editor on “Is It All Over My Face?” (WES-22129, 1980). His editing work on the original pressing went uncredited; confirmed in later reissue documentation.

Tom Moulton — Mixer, Producer, Mastered By (Discogs ID: 3481)

Credited on 44 West End releases (mixer/engineer category). His role divides clearly by era:

  • 1976–1981: Active mixing and production — virtually the entire first run of West End 12”s, plus production credits on Michele’s Magic Love LP (1977) and “Can’t You Feel It” (1977), “Hey Everybody (Party Hearty)” (1981), and “Why?” (1981). Credited as “Mixed By [A Tom Moulton Mix]” on the first West End split 12” (WES 12101, 1976).
  • 2000–2003: Mastering-only role on the label’s reactivation period: Larry Levan Live At The Paradise Garage (2000), “Work That Body” reissues (2000), and Larry Levan Live At The Legendary Paradise Garage (2003).

See Tom Moulton for full discography.

Nick Martinelli & David Todd — Mixer, Producer (Discogs ID: 577831)

Mixed or co-produced 27 releases across the catalog, particularly the early 1980s period. Production team frequently on Raw Silk and related acts.

Frank Filipetti — Engineer (Discogs ID: 153219)

Recording and mix engineer on 29 releases. Key engineer on the Taana Gardner albums and the Sparque sides.

Henry Batts — Producer, Executive Producer (Discogs ID: 183954)

Associate and executive producer on 54 West End releases. Frequent co-credit alongside Kenton Nix on the Taana Gardner material.

Key Releases — Personnel Detail

Michele (Chantal Sitruk) — Magic Love LP / “Can’t You Feel It” / “Disco Dance” (1977–1978)

Background. Michele was the recording pseudonym for Chantal Sitruk, a Tunisian-born French singer. Pierre Jaubert discovered her on the streets of Paris in the early-to-mid 1970s: he overheard her talking to a friend, liked her voice, and offered her work. (Source: Wikipedia — Chantal Curtis, as of 2025-10-17; Wikipedia — Pierre Jaubert, as of 2025-10-17)

Recording. In 1977, Sitruk and Jaubert recorded four tracks together: “Can’t You Feel It,” “Magic Love,” “Hold Me Squeeze Me,” and “Disco Dance.” Tom Moulton handled mixing, and Jaubert served as primary producer. The four-song album (Magic Love, WE-103) was released on West End Records in the US and on Epic Records in France. (Source: discovinyl blog; DiscoSnob blog)

The Michele pseudonym. Sitruk was incarcerated at the time of the recordings’ release — reportedly due to drug use linked to her boyfriend, Philippe Briche — and could not promote the project. Jaubert therefore released the recordings under the name “Michele.” For album covers and television appearances, a different woman (also named Michele) was used; accounts differ on whether this was purely commercial (“some say it was commercial, her being ‘more attractive’”) or simply a practical substitute during Sitruk’s absence. (Source: Wikipedia — Chantal Curtis, as of 2025-10-17; discovinyl blog)

“Can’t You Feel It” (WES 1208, 1977). The lead single from the Magic Love LP. Peaked at No. 3 on the American disco charts in November 1977 per the discovinyl blog (no contemporaneous chart citation available to verify). (Source: discovinyl blog)

“Disco Dance” (WES 12110, 1978). A remix by Patrick Cowley was issued in 1978 and is described as a collectible pressing. (Source: discovinyl blog; Shazam — “Disco Dance (Original Patrick Cowley 12” Mega Mix)”)

Later career. After the Michele recordings, Sitruk continued working with Jaubert under her own name “Chantal Curtis.” In 1979 they recorded “Get Another Love,” which spent three weeks in the British charts, peaking at No. 51. Jaubert used the Lafayette Afro Rock Band on that album. (Source: Wikipedia — Chantal Curtis, as of 2025-10-17; Wikipedia — Pierre Jaubert, as of 2025-10-17)

Death. Sitruk was killed in Israel in 1985, shot by a bullet intended for her boyfriend Philippe Briche. (Source: Wikipedia — Chantal Curtis, as of 2025-10-17; discovinyl blog)

Distribution and Promotion Model

West End operated in the pre-consolidation independent dance music economy. Key structural features:

Record pools. Cheren had co-founded one of the first record pools at Scepter with David Mancuso, Vince Aletti, and Steve D’Aquisto. Record pools were the mechanism by which labels distributed promotional 12”s to club DJs in exchange for feedback and floor-test results. West End used this infrastructure from day one.

The Paradise Garage as A&R. Larry Levan’s floor at the Garage functioned as a test press environment. Records that Levan played and that the crowd responded to became priorities. This was not a formal arrangement — it was structural: Cheren’s financial interest in the club and his creative relationship with Levan meant that what worked at the Garage worked for West End, and vice versa.

12” single format. West End was one of the early labels to commit to the 12” single as a primary format rather than a promotional supplement. The WES 12xxx and WES 22xxx catalog sequences represent near-continuous 12” single production from 1976 through 1984.

DJ mixes on LPs. The 1982 Master Mix Medley - 7th Anniversary Edition (WE-109) by Tony Humphries and the 1999 Levan retrospective (WES 2001-1) reflect the label’s later practice of releasing DJ-curated documents of its own catalog.

The Paradise Garage Circuit

The Paradise Garage (NYC) opened 1977, closed September 1987. Michael Brody owned and operated it; Mel Cheren was its financial backer; Larry Levan was resident DJ throughout its run.

The overlap between the Garage’s regular playlist and the West End catalog is not coincidental. Key records in Levan’s rotation — “Work That Body,” “When You Touch Me,” “Heartbeat,” “Don’t Make Me Wait,” “Is It All Over My Face?” — were West End releases. Levan received mixing and production credits on many of them. His mixes of “Heartbeat” and “When You Touch Me” (the “Club Version” and “Party Version” designations on the “Heartbeat” 12”) were designed for the Garage system, which Richard Long had custom-built for Levan’s use.

Childhood friends with Frankie Knuckles, who left NYC for Chicago and helped catalyze house music there — a direct genealogical line from the Garage to house.

Genre and Style Range

West End’s catalog is primarily disco and early electronic dance, but with notable adjacent releases:

  • Early electro: Hashim’s “Al-Naafiysh (The Soul)” (1983) is one of the period’s earliest electro records, co-released on Arista/Streetwave UK.
  • Early rap: Sweet G’s “A Heartbeat Rap” (WES 22134, 1981) is among the label’s earliest hip-hop-adjacent releases.
  • Post-disco / proto-house: The Peech Boys’ “Don’t Make Me Wait” (1982) is routinely cited as proto-house; Raw Silk’s “Do It To The Music” (1982) is catalogued as Boogie/Funk/Disco.
  • Funk / Soul: Bettye LaVette’s “Doin’ The Best That I Can” (1978) drew the soul tradition directly into the disco remix format, with a Walter Gibbons remix.
  • Downtown crossover: Loose Joints’ “Is It All Over My Face?” (1980) remains the label’s clearest connection to the downtown NYC avant-garde scene.

Legacy and Post-Golden-Era Activity

The label’s primary creative period runs 1977–1984. After 1984, new releases become sparse until the 1990 Black Riot single (WE9001). The label was formally reactivated in summer 1998; new artists in the reactivation period include Linda Clifford, Marty Thomas, and Blaze, alongside archival Taana Gardner and Masters At Work remix packages.

The 25th anniversary edition (2001) and the Larry Levan retrospective (1999) were the two most significant late-period releases from a historical standpoint.

Mel Cheren’s 2000 memoir Keep On Dancin’: My Life and the Paradise Garage remains the primary first-person account of the label and its era.

The 2007 documentary The Godfather of Disco (adapted from the memoir) documents Cheren’s life through interviews with figures from the dance music community. It premiered at the Minneapolis/St. Paul International Film Festival and won the Emerging Filmmaker Award. Cheren died December 7, 2007, before wider release.

West End’s label design is cited by Discogs as having inspired East End, East End (2), and Bitter End Records.
