Homestead Records

Oct 17, 202612 min read

Homestead Records

Discogs ID: 11706 Parent label: Dutch East India Trading (ID 9986) Founded: 1983 Address: P.O. Box 570, Rockville Centre, NY 11571 (later 11571-1570) Active: 1983–1996 Total Discogs releases: 785 (728 unique masters)

One of the defining American underground labels of the 1980s. A sublabel of the Dutch East India Trading distribution company, Homestead launched or broke a remarkable concentration of US indie and alternative acts — Big Black, Sonic Youth, Dinosaur Jr., Green River, Sebadoh, Naked Raygun, Daniel Johnston — and licensed key early Nick Cave & The Bad Seeds records for American distribution. Manager Gerard Cosloy left the label in the late 1980s and co-founded Matador Records, creating the direct institutional thread of American indie from Homestead through to the 1990s Matador era.

Structure

Homestead was a sublabel of Dutch East India Trading (Discogs 9986), a Rockville Centre, NY distribution and import company that also ran Grass Records, Napalm Records (2), Rockville Records, and Dutch East Wax Records. Dutch East India Trading ceased active operations approximately 2000–2002 (website last updated 2003); it did not file for formal bankruptcy but wound down informally amid disputed financial obligations to artists and consignment partners. See “Dutch East India Trading: Collapse and Aftermath” below.

Catalog numbers ran in an HMS ### sequence, beginning with HMS 001 (The Blackjacks, 1984) and running past HMS 230 by the mid-1990s. CD catalog numbers followed a HMS ###-2 or HMSCD### pattern.

Era by Era

1983–1985: Launch

Homestead opened with a range of regional US acts — Dogmatics (Boston), The Nomads, Salem 66 — before quickly finding its identity as a clearinghouse for American hardcore-adjacent underground rock. Key first-wave signings:

  • Big Black (Chicago): Racer-X EP (HMS 007, 1985), first of 30 Homestead titles
  • Sonic Youth (NYC): Bad Moon Rising (HMS 016, 1985); Death Valley ‘69 single with Lydia Lunch (HMS 021)
  • Dinosaur Jr. (Amherst MA): Dinosaur (HMS 015, 1985); Repulsion (HMS 032, 1985)
  • Green River (Seattle): Come On Down (HMS 031, 1985) — lineup included Jeff Ament, Stone Gossard, Mark Arm, Steve Turner; proto-Pearl Jam and Mudhoney personnel
  • Nick Cave & The Bad Seeds (licensed US): The Firstborn Is Dead (HMS 026, 1985); Tupelo single (HMS 029)
  • Naked Raygun (Chicago): Throb Throb (HMS 008, 1985)
  • Squirrel Bait (Louisville KY): debut EP (HMS 028, 1985); members later formed Bastro and Slint
  • Swans: Young God (HMS 017, 1985)

1986–1988: Peak

The label’s densest creative period. Big Black’s Atomizer (HMS 043, 1986) and Sonic Youth’s continued catalog solidified Homestead’s reputation. Key additions:

  • Bastro (members of Squirrel Bait): Rode Hard and Put Up Wet (HMS 111, 1988)
  • Big Dipper: Heavens (HMS 086, 1987), produced by Sean Slade and Paul Q. Kolderie
  • Einstürzende Neubauten: US licensing, 80=83 (HMS 063, 1986)
  • Foetus: Nail (HMS 041, 1985)
  • Happy Flowers: began a 27-release run on Homestead from 1987
  • Daniel Johnston: Hi, How Are You (HMS 117, 1988)
  • Screaming Trees: Beat Happening / Screaming Trees split (HMS 110, 1988)
  • Salem 66, Live Skull, Volcano Suns, Death Of Samantha, Phantom Tollbooth — all active on the label through this era

1989–1991: Transition

Gerard Cosloy departed and co-founded Matador Records with Chris Lombardi. Several key Homestead acts moved to Matador or majors. Sebadoh became the label’s flagship act:

  • Sebadoh: The Freed Man (HMS 145, 1989); Weed Forestin (HMS 158, 1990); III (HMS 168, 1991) — Lou Barlow, Eric Gaffney, Jason Loewenstein; engineered/mixed by Sean Slade

The late-period label also picked up international artists: The Verlaines, The Chills, The Clean, Tall Dwarfs, The Cakekitchen (all New Zealand), Beat Happening (Olympia WA).

1992–1996: Wind-down

Catalog ran through HMS 237. Later signings included Trumans Water, Bratmobile, Seam, and a jazz-leaning late-catalog phase with David S. Ware Quartet, William Parker, Ivo Perelman. Label activity diminished through the mid-1990s. The final release was Ivo Perelman’s Cama de Terra (HMS 237-2, 1996), a free-jazz trio session with Matthew Shipp and William Parker. Steven Joerg, who managed Homestead in its final period, left at the end of 1996 to found his own jazz-oriented label. [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, 2006-08-25]

Dutch East India Trading continued operating as a distributor after Homestead stopped releasing records, selling merchandise to stores until 2002 and last updating its website in 2003. The company’s end was not a formal bankruptcy filing — Tenenbaum wound the operation down informally. See “Dutch East India Trading: Collapse and Aftermath” below for detail. [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, 2006-08-25]

Dutch East India Trading: Collapse and Aftermath

How DEIT collapsed

Dutch East India Trading did not file for formal bankruptcy. According to Barry Tenenbaum, the company’s demise was driven by the economics of the late-1990s music industry: “when music became free on the Internet and (with) so many labels selling direct to our accounts all over the world, it became a very difficult environment starting in the late 1990s. Then all of our big accounts started going bankrupt and Dutch East lost everything.” [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, Magnet Magazine, 2006-08-25]

Former Homestead manager Ken Katkin offered a sharply different account: “He had a warehouse full of records on consignment, and … one day took off with all that stuff. He can’t be found because he didn’t declare bankruptcy. He simply stole all the stuff.” Katkin also noted that during his own tenure (1991–1992), “Tenenbaum cut no royalty checks to artists during his two years at the label and often neglected to provide bands with biannual accounting statements.” [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, Magnet Magazine, 2006-08-25]

Tenenbaum himself acknowledged that royalties across most of the catalog went unpaid, framing it as advances not being earned back: “Lots of royalties were paid, but it’s unlikely that more than one or two releases after HMS125 (Giant Sand’s 1988 album The Love Songs) or so ever earned back their advances.” [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, Magnet Magazine, 2006-08-25]

A separate source (Steev Riccardo, in a 2024 interview about Giant Records, another DEIT sublabel) corroborated the pattern: “Barry Tenenbaum from Dutch East India disappeared off the face of the earth. He screwed over a lot of people.” [source: https://rettman.substack.com/p/interview-steev-riccardo-part-ii]

DEIT ceased selling merchandise to stores in 2002 and last updated its website in 2003. The Freeport Memorial Library’s Long Island music history guide (citing the royalties case involving Uncle Tupelo / Rockville Records as a legal dispute that contributed to the collapse) places DEIT’s cessation of operations at “around 2000.” [source: https://libguides.freeportlibrary.info/c.php?g=1500070&p=11208578]

Conflict note: The Magnet Magazine (2006) article, which draws on Tenenbaum’s own statement and Katkin’s account, implies the operational wind-down ran from roughly 2000 (active business effectively ceased) through 2002–2003 (merchandise sales ended, website went dark). The LibGuides source says “around 2000.” No formal court bankruptcy filing has been identified in any source. The earlier note in this file (“went bankrupt around 2000”) overstates the formality — the collapse was an informal dissolution with disputed financial misconduct, not a declared insolvency.

Effect on Homestead Records

Homestead stopped releasing records in 1996, before DEIT’s effective collapse. The label’s 1996 closure was driven by the wind-down of activity under Steven Joerg’s management, not directly by DEIT’s financial failure — Joerg left at the end of 1996 to found AUM Fidelity (a jazz label), and no successor manager was appointed.

However, the aftermath of DEIT’s collapse created lasting legal complications for Homestead. As of 2006, Tenenbaum stated: “Homestead is technically in a very complicated legal situation and will remain so for a long time.” [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, Magnet Magazine, 2006-08-25]

Key documented consequences:

  • Unpaid royalties: No royalty checks were cut to artists for most of the catalog. Katkin confirmed this was already the pattern in 1991–1992; Tenenbaum’s framing of unrecouped advances is disputed by artists who believe catalog sales were substantial enough to generate royalties owed.
  • Master tape disputes: Ownership of Homestead master recordings became contested after DEIT’s collapse. The Magnet article (2006) notes: “claims of unpaid royalties, disputes over ownership of master tapes — have carried the Homestead saga a decade past its expiration date.” A lawyer circulated an offer to bring collective legal action against Tenenbaum on behalf of aggrieved artists, either to reclaim masters of out-of-print recordings or collect royalties — contingent on enough artists joining to make the case viable. [source: https://magnetmagazine.com/2006/08/25/homestead-records-frontier-days/, Magnet Magazine, 2006-08-25]
  • Rockville Records / Uncle Tupelo precedent: Uncle Tupelo’s first two albums (No Depression, 1990; Still Feel Gone, 1991) were released on Rockville Records, another DEIT sublabel. Rockville refused to pay royalties despite reported combined sales of ~40,000 copies. Uncle Tupelo eventually regained rights through a lawsuit and reissued both albums via Legacy Records in 2003. This case established that legal recourse against DEIT/Tenenbaum was viable, though expensive. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anodyne_(album); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Depression_(album)]

Summary

No formal bankruptcy filing by Dutch East India Trading has been confirmed in any source. The collapse was an informal dissolution — Tenenbaum ceased operations approximately 2000–2002 without satisfying outstanding obligations to artists or consignment partners. Homestead Records’ 1996 closure predated the DEIT collapse, but the parent company’s disintegration left Homestead’s catalog in an unresolved legal limbo: masters in disputed ownership, royalties unpaid across most of the catalog, and no formal proceedings to adjudicate claims.

Connections

  • Dutch East India Trading — parent distribution company; ceased operations ~2000–2002 without formal bankruptcy filing
  • AUM Fidelity — founded by Steven Joerg (December 1996/January 1997) after leaving Homestead; avant-garde jazz focus; no Homestead catalog absorbed
  • Matador Records — founded by Gerard Cosloy after leaving Homestead; carries forward the institutional lineage
  • Gerard Cosloy — label manager; later Matador co-founder
  • Sonic Youth — early catalog (1985–1989); left for DGC/major
  • Big Black — Steve Albini’s primary vehicle; 30 Homestead releases 1985–1990
  • Dinosaur Jr. — core Homestead act; later Sire/WEA
  • Green River — proto-Pearl Jam / proto-Mudhoney; Come On Down (1985) features Jeff Ament, Stone Gossard, Mark Arm
  • Sebadoh — late-period flagship; 31 releases
  • Nick Cave & The Bad Seeds — US licensing for Mute/UK releases
