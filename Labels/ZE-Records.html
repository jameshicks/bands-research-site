ZE Records

Founded: 1978, New York City Founders: Michael Zilkha and Michel Esteban Active: 1978–1984 (original); 2003–present (revival, from France / Brazil) Distribution: Buddah/Arista → Island Records (primary); Ariola (Europe); Celluloid (co-releases); Sire, CBS, various territory deals Genre: No Wave, mutant disco, electro, avant-funk, post-punk Catchphrase (house term): “Mutant Disco”

Founders

Michel Esteban (b. 1951, France)

Studied graphic arts in Paris and at the School of Visual Arts, New York. Ran Harry Cover — a Paris shop selling US/UK rock merchandise whose basement served as a rehearsal space for French new wave bands. Between 1975–76 published Rock News, one of the first European punk magazines covering the London/New York/Paris movements simultaneously. Arrived in the NYC downtown orbit via his relationship with Anya Phillips (James Chance’s manager/partner), and his signing of the French post-punk group Marie Et Les Garçons. He was also the partner of Lizzy Mercier Descloux, who became ZE’s most critically significant French artist. Post-ZE, continued working with Descloux and singer Lio. Revived ZE in 2003 from France, later operating from Salvador da Bahia, Brazil.

Michael Zilkha (b. 1954, UK)

Oxford graduate; British-born of Iraqi descent. His father Selim Zilkha owned the Mothercare retail chain. Worked in New York publishing in the mid-1970s and wrote for the Village Voice. After ZE closed, he joined his father in Zilkha Energy — a Houston-based oil and gas company sold to Sonat in 1998 for approximately $1 billion. They then built Zilkha Renewable Energy (wind power), sold to Goldman Sachs in 2005 (renamed Horizon Wind Energy) and then to Energias de Portugal in 2007. Zilkha remains based in Houston. He reportedly retained the ZE masters throughout and eventually returned master recordings and publishing rights to artists.

How they met: The John Cale connection

Esteban approached John Cale to produce Marie Et Les Garçons. In 1977, Cale had launched SPY Records with manager Jane Friedman (distributed by I.R.S. Records; Esteban designed the SPY logo). Cale introduced Esteban to Zilkha. In 1978, Esteban and Zilkha left SPY to found ZE together. SPY continued with the Necessaries, Lester Bangs, and Model Citizens before closing in 1980. Cale himself later became one of ZE’s most prolific artists — 64 releases across three albums in the 1980s.

The name: ZE = initials of Zilkha and Esteban (order disputed between sources).

Concept: Mutant Disco

Zilkha’s own description: “The first album I put out was James White and the Blacks, which was the Contortions slowed down to a disco beat.” The stated aim was to assault disco and drag it down to the Lower East Side — or equivalently, to drag No Wave uptown to the dancefloor. The house term became “Mutant Disco”, formalized in the 1981 compilation Mutant Disco: A Subtle Discolation of the Norm.

John Peel called ZE “the best independent record label in the world” (Melody Maker, 1980). The Face called it “the world’s most fashionable label” (1982).

The label signed writers, painters, and filmmakers alongside musicians. It deliberately crossed racial, genre, and national lines: French artists alongside NYC no-wavers, street art adjacent to early hip-hop, sophisticated humor threading through everything.

Distribution Arrangements

Buddah/Arista (1978–79): Earliest releases distributed via Buddah Records (itself distributed by Arista from 1978).

Island Records (primary, ~1979–84): The central deal — and the most consequential. It came via a personal connection: Chris Blackwell (Island founder) was a friend of Anna Wintour, then a fashion editor and Esteban’s girlfriend. ZE releases on Island used the ILPS catalog prefix: Buy (James Chance) was ILPS 7002; Suicide’s Alan Vega · Martin Rev was ILPS 7007. European distribution ran via Ariola (Island’s European partner). Kid Creole’s Off the Coast of Me went through Island’s Antilles subsidiary; Fresh Fruit in Foreign Places through Sire.

Celluloid Records (parallel co-releases, early 1980s): Jean Karakos’s Celluloid — French-originated, part-based in NYC — had a co-release arrangement with ZE. Alan Vega’s solo debut carried both label logos; Was (Not Was), Suicide, and Lydia Lunch material appeared on both imprints. Not an exclusive deal — territory or artist-specific co-releases.

Various territory sub-licenses with Sire, Universal/Polygram, EMI, Nippon Columbia throughout the period.

No New York (Antilles, 1978) — context

The Brian Eno-produced No Wave document — four bands, four tracks each: The Contortions, Teenage Jesus And The Jerks, Mars, DNA. Not a ZE release (Antilles was an Island subsidiary). ZE’s founding circle is inseparable from it nonetheless. Anya Phillips co-organized it; James Chance was its central figure; Esteban was in that orbit. One account has Eno telling Chance “nobody would sign that but a desperate man” when Chance showed him the ZE contract — Chance signed anyway.

ESG (often associated with this cluster) were on 99 Records, not ZE.

The August Darnell / Kid Creole axis

August Darnell (born Thomas Darnell Browder) and cousin Coati Mundi (Andy Hernandez) were the label’s commercial engine. Kid Creole And The Coconuts — a theatrical Latin/Caribbean/funk revue — generated 269 releases on ZE, easily the label’s largest catalog. Darnell also produced Cristina’s early ZE material (and co-produced or influenced much of the label’s dance output). The Kid Creole project peaked in the UK in 1982.

Suicide on ZE

Alan Vega and Martin Rev were properly signed to ZE (not merely distributed). The 1980 album was produced by Ric Ocasek of The Cars — a deliberate push toward synthetic, Moroder-influenced disco. This caused tension with the band. Alan Vega’s concurrent solo debut was a co-release with Celluloid Records. The Suicide/ZE pairing illustrates the label’s willingness to position confrontational underground acts within commercial genre frameworks.

Material / Bill Laswell

Material — Bill Laswell’s avant-funk collective — released “Bustin’ Out / Over & Over” on Ze/Island (1981) and the Temporary Music series. The albums Memory Serves and One Down were on Celluloid Records, not ZE proper, but the Ze connection defines the period. Memory Serves assembled Henry Threadgill, George Lewis, Sonny Sharrock, Olu Dara, Fred Frith, Billy Bang; One Down added Nile Rodgers, Archie Shepp, Oliver Lake, Nona Hendryx, and a pre-debut Whitney Houston. Laswell went on to produce Herbie Hancock’s “Rockit”, Mick Jagger, Motörhead. The Material era is his early NYC avant-garde moment.

Breakfast Club / Dan Gilroy — Madonna Connection

The Breakfast Club’s five ZE releases (1984) place the label at the edge of one of the better-documented origin stories in 1980s pop. The connection is oblique — ZE signed the band after Madonna had already left it — but it threads through the same downtown NYC milieu that ZE inhabited throughout its run.

The backstory. Dan Gilroy (vocalist/guitarist) and his brother Ed Gilroy (guitarist) were New York musicians who also performed comedy routines as “Bil and Gil.” In 1979, Dan met Madonna Ciccone — then 20, recently arrived in New York, working as a dancer — at a party. They became romantically involved. Madonna moved into the abandoned synagogue in Corona, Queens, where the Gilroy brothers lived and rehearsed. The space served as studio, rehearsal room, and residence; the band slept in the basement and used the meeting hall to write and record. (Wikipedia — Madonna, as of 2025-07-01; Wikipedia — Dan Gilroy (musician), as of 2025-07-01)

Madonna’s role in the band. Dan Gilroy taught Madonna guitar. In the Breakfast Club, she played drums, guitar, and eventually sang lead on some material — Gilroy reportedly let her front the band on her own compositions after she pushed for it. The lineup at its core was: Dan Gilroy (vocals/guitar), Ed Gilroy (guitar), Madonna (drums/vocals), and Angie Smit (bass). (Wikipedia — Breakfast Club (band), as of 2025-07-01)

Her departure. In November 1980 Madonna ended her relationship with Dan Gilroy and left the Breakfast Club. She reunited with drummer Stephen Bray — a former boyfriend from her time in Michigan — and the two formed a new band, Emmy (later Emmy and the Emmys), based at the Music Building in Manhattan. From there Madonna built the demo tape that led to her Sire Records signing in 1982. Bray went on to co-write and co-produce several tracks on Like a Virgin (1984) and True Blue (1986). (Wikipedia — Madonna, as of 2025-07-01)

After Madonna: ZE. The Breakfast Club regrouped. By the mid-1980s the core lineup was the Gilroy brothers plus Gary Burke (bass) and Stephen Bray (drums) — both Burke and Bray had also passed through Emmy and the Emmys, making the personnel overlap explicit. The band signed with ZE Records and released the single “Rico Mambo” b/w “Walk It Off” in 1984 — distributed by Island Records (ZE’s primary distribution deal), issued as 7” and 12” in multiple territories. It did not chart. (Wikipedia — Rico Mambo, as of 2025-07-01; Discogs release 587409)

The ZE signing came as the label was winding down (Esteban had left NYC in 1982; ZE closed in 1984). Breakfast Club subsequently moved to MCA Records, released their self-titled album in 1987, and scored a US Top 10 hit with “Right on Track” (Billboard Hot 100 no. 7), earning a Grammy nomination for Best New Artist in 1988 — by which point Madonna was three albums into her own solo career.

What ZE’s role actually was. ZE did not discover Breakfast Club or shape their sound; the label was their first commercial stepping stone after the Corona Queens years. The Madonna connection pre-dates ZE by four years. The interest for this vault is that ZE — which ran on the Danceteria/Mudd Club axis and explicitly bridged no-wave and the dancefloor — provided the band’s first released recordings, making it a factual node in Madonna’s extended biography even though she had been gone from the lineup for roughly three years before ZE entered the picture. Dan Gilroy later had a long-term relationship with actress Shelley Duvall (1989–2024). (Wikipedia — Dan Gilroy (musician), as of 2025-07-01)

Foetus / JG Thirlwell

JG Thirlwell (Australian-born; London and NYC-based) released Hole (1984, as Scraping Foetus Off The Wheel) on ZE Records as a US co-release with his own Self Immolation imprint; PVC Records was also credited for US distribution. This is one album, not multiple releases — Discogs shows multiple pressings of the same record. Places him in the same catalog as Suicide and James Chance during his confrontational industrial period.

John Cale on ZE

Three studio albums: Music for a New Society (1982), Caribbean Sunset (1983), Comes Alive (1984). The Cale/ZE pairing is less surprising given that Cale was the original SPY Records connection that introduced Esteban to Zilkha — a relationship that ran throughout.

The Downtown NYC Scene

Mudd Club (77 White Street, TriBeCa, 1978–1983) — primary venue. ZE artists including The Contortions and Lydia Lunch performed there. Mixing No Wave, post-punk, art world, and fashion in one room.

Danceteria, the Peppermint Lounge, Club 57, the Pyramid — venues where Mutant Disco material circulated.

Paradise Garage (84 King Street, Manhattan, 1977–1987) — DJ Larry Levan’s room; a documented nexus for ZE catalog material. Two specific connections are confirmed:

  1. “Deputy of Love” played live, 1979. A recording of Levan’s DJ set at the Paradise Garage’s second anniversary (1979) — captured on a reel-to-reel by Lenny Fontana — includes Don Armando’s Second Avenue Rhumba Band’s “Deputy of Love” in the tracklist. This is the earliest documented instance of a ZE release at the Garage. (Passion of the Weiss / powmag.net, as of 2026-07-28)

  2. Larry Levan remix of Kid Creole, 1983. Levan produced a remix of Kid Creole And The Coconuts’ “There’s Something Wrong in Paradise” (from the 1983 album Doppelganger), issued as a 12” single. The remix was later compiled on Mutant Disco Volume 3: Garage Sale (ZE, 2004, cat. ZE.014CD). (Discogs master 88424; Forced Exposure ZE.014CD; SoundCloud)

  3. Esteban’s own framing. The liner notes to Mutant Disco Volume 3: Garage Sale (written by Michel Esteban) state explicitly: “The title of ‘Garage’ comes from ‘Paradise Garage’, the now mythical club at 84 King Street… The turntables were under the magic touch of Larry Levan,” and that “many producers would test their mixes on the dance floor at the Garage” — implying the overlap between ZE’s production world and Levan’s room was intentional and ongoing, not coincidental. (ZE Records website — Mutant Disco Box Set, as of 2026-07-28)

The ZE Records Mutant Disco Vol. 2 liner notes also name Levan alongside Arthur Russell as a “disco alchemist” in the same tradition as the label. No other specific Levan remixes of ZE catalog material have been confirmed; his primary label association was West End Records, not ZE.

Anna Wintour — then a fashion editor; Esteban’s girlfriend; her social connection to Chris Blackwell produced the Island Records deal. A demonstration of how tightly the downtown art/fashion/music world was networked.

Revival (2003–present)

Esteban re-established ZE in France in 2003. Focus on deluxe remastered reissues; 40+ albums reissued. JD Twitch (Optimo, Glasgow) compiled a ZE retrospective (2003). Strut Records released Ze 30: ZE Records Story 1979–2009 in 2009. A companion ZEvolution: ZE Records Re-Edited featured remixes by Idjut Boys and Todd Terje. Light in the Attic reissued Lizzy Mercier Descloux’s Press Color (2015). Soul Jazz Records also licensed ZE material.
