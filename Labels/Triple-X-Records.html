Overview

Triple X Records is an American independent label founded in late 1985 by Dean Naleway, Peter Heur, and Charley Brown, and operates under the Triple X Entertainment umbrella. Rooted in punk, hardcore, alternative rock, and goth rock, the label built its catalogue primarily across the late 1980s and 1990s, with the 1990s alone accounting for the bulk of its roughly 229 releases. Notable signings have included Jane’s Addiction and Perry Farrell, reflecting its ties to the Los Angeles underground scene. Release activity dropped sharply after 2000, with only a handful of titles appearing in the 2000s and a small handful more in the 2020s.

Notes (web)

Founding — Greenworld connection

Triple X was started by three former employees of the bankrupt Greenworld Distribution: Dean Naleway, Peter Heur, and Charley Brown (4). According to Diffuser.fm’s 2017 anniversary piece, the three “got themselves fired in order to collect unemployment, which they used to start Triple X,” then maxed out their credit cards and sold their cars to fund Jane’s Addiction’s debut. (diffuser.fm)

Jane’s Addiction signing story

Charley Brown (4) discovered Jane’s Addiction at the Powerhouse club in Los Angeles and kept attending shows until his co-founders were also convinced. According to a May 1987 Music Connection interview with Perry Farrell, Brown approached the band — “Look, we got a record label” — and Farrell responded by proposing a one-record deal that also gave Triple X a short-term management role: releasing one record together while Triple X managed the band “so we can keep the ball rolling.” (janesaddiction.org/gallery/articles/music-connection-in-deep-with-janes-addiction-may-18-1987/)

Triple X initially sought a longer contract — three to five albums, depending on the source — but Farrell pushed back and negotiated a 50-50 profit-split on a single album only. (diffuser.fm)

The self-titled debut was recorded live at the Roxy Theatre with overdubs at The Edge Studio and released 15 May 1987. It triggered a major-label bidding war resulting in the band signing with Warner Bros. Records. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane’s_Addiction_(album))

Farrell’s stated reasoning to Warner Bros.: “We told Warner we definitely wanted to sign, but we wanted to come out on our own label or an indie first and then grow organically from there.” The strategic credibility this gave the band is notable — many have argued that Soundgarden and others subsequently chose indie debuts in part because of how well the Triple X release worked for Jane’s Addiction. (diffuser.fm)

Charley Brown as manager

After the album, Charley Brown (4) transitioned into becoming Jane’s Addiction’s first manager through Triple X Management, a parallel entity under the Triple X umbrella. The record deal therefore directly spawned the management operation. (diffuser.fm)

Perry Farrell’s influence on the label’s direction

No source directly states that Farrell shaped Triple X’s ongoing A&R direction or artist roster beyond the single release. The 1987 Music Connection interview shows Farrell was enthusiastic about the founders (“Those guys are unbelievable. There’s three of them, so when they show up, they’re all over the place”), but the relationship appears to have been the one album plus the management arrangement. Triple X also released Psi Com’s record (Farrell’s pre-Jane’s Addiction band) — catalogued as 1993, though Exclaim! notes it was “reissued after almost eight years,” suggesting an earlier original release. (exclaim.ca) Whether the Psi Com release reflects Farrell’s ongoing ties to the label or simply Triple X exploiting back-catalogue it already held is unconfirmed.

Output decline in the 2000s — what is and is not known

No source — including Wikipedia, the Wikipedia-sourced articles, or industry histories found in this search — directly explains the drop from roughly 167 releases in the 1990s to 23 in the 2000s. What the record shows:

  • Triple X released only a handful of titles per year after 2001, with a complete gap between 2005 and 2009.
  • Wikipedia describes the label as “dormant since the late 2000s.” (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_X_Records)
  • In 1996, Interscope Records sued Triple X over the First Round Knock Out Dr. Dre compilation, alleging unlawful use of Dr. Dre’s name and likeness. The lawsuit failed and the compilation stayed on the market, but the legal pressure — combined with a similar 1994 Concrete Roots release — may have strained relationships with distributors or chilled the label’s appetite for contentious releases. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Round_Knock_Out)
  • No ownership change, distribution change, or named financial event has been identified as the proximate cause of the decline. The broader context of CD sales collapse after 2000 (US CD sales fell from ~942 million units in 2000 to ~240 million by 2011) and closure of ~2,680 US record stores between 2005–2009 is consistent with the timing, but no source attributes the Triple X contraction specifically to these industry-wide forces. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000s_in_the_music_industry)
