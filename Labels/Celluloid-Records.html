Celluloid Records

Founded: 1976, New York City Founder: Jean Karakos (a.k.a. Jean Georgakarakos; Discogs artist ID: 403913) Active: 1976–1989 Base: New York / Paris Genre: No Wave, avant-garde, hip-hop, world music, jazz Discogs label ID: 5195 (main label “Celluloid”); parent Celluloid Records (2) ID: 109358; holding company Celluloid Inc. ID: 468602

French-originated label with a New York base, founded by Jean Karakos. One of the key institutional bridges between the NYC no-wave/downtown scene and European avant-garde distribution. Co-release partner with ZE Records for select artists in the early 1980s. By release count (2,000+), Celluloid’s largest single strand is West and Central African music — Touré Kunda, Fela Kuti, and Manu Dibango dominate the later catalog.

History

Origins

Jean Karakos (Jean Georgakarakos, 1940–2017) had a deep track record before Celluloid. In 1960 he founded Star Success (Discogs: 938758), a French pop/twist imprint. In 1964 he founded Joc (Discogs: 163390), which licensed American folk and blues for the French market (Pete Seeger, Lightnin’ Hopkins, Big Bill Broonzy, Pentangle). He ran a network of French record shops called Pop Shop through which he sold imported American LPs, giving him both market intelligence and a distribution infrastructure.

In 1967 he co-founded BYG Records (Discogs: 13232) in Paris with Jean-Luc Young and Fernand Boruso. BYG became one of the key documentary free jazz labels of the late 1960s, recording Don Cherry, Archie Shepp, Sun Ra, Anthony Braxton, and A.A.C.M. members during the Actuel Festival period (1969). Karakos produced or supervised the core BYG Actuel sessions, accumulated over 100 producer credits there, and maintained that relationship throughout his life — the BYG/Charly reissue program carries his credit as recently as 2024–2025.

He established Celluloid in New York in 1976, also launching the short-lived imprint Tapioca (Discogs: 48269) simultaneously. Early signings included Metal Urbain (French punk) and James Chance (no-wave).

ZE Records Co-Release Period (early 1980s)

In the early 1980s, Celluloid entered a French territorial licensing arrangement with ZE Records (Michael Zilkha and Michel Esteban). The available evidence — record label text on physical pressings and regional release tables across multiple albums — points consistently to a territory deal rather than per-artist negotiations: ZE retained the sound recording copyright and Celluloid handled the French-market press and release.

Key evidence for the territorial model:

  • Lydia Lunch — Queen Of Siam (ZEA33-006, 1980) was the US release on ZE; the French pressing (CEL 2-6561, 1981) carries the text ”℗ 1980 Ze Records, Inc. Made in France”, confirming ZE as rights-holder and Celluloid as French territorial presser. [source: Discogs release/575311; worthpoint.com/worthopedia/lp-lydia-lunch-queen-siam-ze-records-149919876]
  • Alan Vega — Alan Vega (1980): US release on ZE Records / PVC (PVC 7915); France on Celluloid (CEL-529 568). Wikipedia’s release table lists these as separate regional entries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Vega_(album)]
  • Was (Not Was) — “Wheel Me Out” (1980) is described as “their first release for the ZE label”, yet the French pressing appeared on Celluloid. The full debut LP went out on ZE/Island; French 7” came out on ZE/Celluloid. [source: zerecords.com; search result text citing ZE/Celluloid pairing for France]
  • Suicide — select releases also crossed labels on this territorial basis.

The arrangement was not exclusive to Celluloid: ZE already held a broader international licensing deal with Island Records (and via Island, Ariola for European territories outside France). Celluloid’s role was specific to France, which made geographic sense — both Michel Esteban and Jean Karakos were French, and Celluloid had existing French pressing infrastructure from its LTM-era continental releases. Both Wikipedia and French Wikipedia describe the labels as having shared scene origins: “Both ZE Records and Celluloid were created by French people fascinated by the multi-ethnic and multi-genre melting pot of New York: Michel Esteban and Jean Karakos.”

No source found in web research explicitly states “territorial licensing agreement” in those terms; the territorial model is inferred from (a) consistent physical pressing evidence (ZE copyright on Celluloid-pressed French copies) and (b) regional release tables showing distinct US (ZE/PVC or ZE/Island) and France (Celluloid) entries for the same master. No source suggests separate per-artist deal negotiations for each ZE act — the pattern is consistent across Vega, Lunch, Was (Not Was), and Suicide. [sources: Wikipedia “Celluloid Records” https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celluloid_Records (as of 2026-08-24); Wikipedia “Alan Vega (album)” https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Vega_(album) (as of 2026-08-24); grokipedia.com/page/ZE_Records]

Conflict / open question — Alan Vega sleeve logos: The existing vault entry states Alan Vega’s debut “carried both Celluloid and ZE logos on the sleeve.” At least one Discogs pressing (r3510748, CEL-529 568) lists only Celluloid. Whether some pressings carry dual logos or whether this was a single-label French pressing needs sleeve-level confirmation. The territorial model is not contradicted either way: dual logos on a French pressing would simply mean ZE required logo co-credit on licensed copies.

Bill Laswell / Hip-Hop Expansion (1982–mid-1980s)

Bill Laswell’s involvement from 1982 onward redirected Celluloid toward hip-hop and cross-genre experimentation. This brought Afrika Bambaataa, Grandmaster Flash, and Material’s later albums to the Celluloid catalog. Laswell’s departure eventually weakened the label.

  • Material — Memory Serves and One Down on Celluloid (not ZE)
  • Afrika Bambaataa — selected releases
  • Herbie Hancock adjacency via Laswell production connections

Bernard Zekri and the Hip-Hop Pivot

Bernard Zekri (Bernard Zekri Ouiddir; b. 28 August 1955, Algiers) was the key cultural broker who connected Jean Karakos to the Bronx hip-hop scene and shaped Celluloid’s early-1980s hip-hop catalog.

Zekri arrived in New York around 1980, initially working at a restaurant (the Wine Bar, East Village) before becoming a writer for the French magazine Actuel. He embedded himself in the downtown and hip-hop club circuit — Danceteria, Club Negril, the Roxy — and befriended Fab 5 Freddy, with whom he shared living space with graffiti artist Futura 2000. His close relationship with Afrika Bambaataa and the Zulu Nation earned him an official “Bronx Pass” — Bambaataa’s endorsement giving Zekri access throughout hip-hop’s South Bronx territory. [source: https://stockx.com/news/hip-hop-and-france/, as of 2026-07-13; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Zekri, as of 2026-07-13]

When Karakos arrived in New York in September 1980 (having already run BYG Records in Paris), he met Zekri and the two co-founded Celluloid Records together. Karakos employed Zekri as talent scout and fixer for the hip-hop side of the catalog. Zekri introduced Karakos to Afrika Bambaataa, Futura 2000, and Grand Mixer D.St. — the three introductions that anchored Celluloid’s hip-hop series (CEL 155–CEL 176). [source: https://medium.com/the-shadow-knows/celluloid-records-the-french-label-who-released-the-freshest-rap-records-of-1982-c3805ff3ed79; https://stockx.com/news/hip-hop-and-france/]

Production credits (1979–1985): French Wikipedia credits Zekri as a rap producer from 1979–1985 with the following specific Celluloid productions:

  • Change The Beat (Fab 5 Freddy / B-Side, 1982, CEL 156) — co-produced / songwriter credit alongside Material. Discogs credits him as songwriter (confirmed: Discogs release 51966).
  • The Wildstyle (Time Zone, 1983, CEL 165) — produced. Discogs credits him as producer (confirmed: Discogs release 52057). Note: “Wild Style” is the title as listed in French sources; the Discogs/Celluloid catalog title is The Wildstyle.
  • The Escapades of Futura 2000 (Futura 2000, 1982, CEL 155) — produced. [source: Discogs release 333830; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Zekri]

The 1982 NYC Rap Tour: In November 1982, Zekri co-organized the New York City Rap Tour with Karakos and Kool Lady Blue, with logistical support from Europe 1 radio. The tour is considered the first large-scale hip-hop stage event outside the United States. It ran: Paris (Bataclan, 21 November; Hippodrome de Pantin / Le Palace, 27 November “Grande nuit Rap”), Lyon, Metz, Belfort, Mulhouse, Strasbourg, London, and Los Angeles. The lineup included Rock Steady Crew, Futura 2000, Dondi, Phase 2, Rammellzee, Fab 5 Freddy, Grand Mixer DXT, Afrika Bambaataa, and The Infinity Rappers. Zekri simultaneously wrote a week-long series of articles for La Libération (24–31 October 1982, “Week in Rap”) explaining hip-hop culture for French readers. [source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Rap_Tour, as of 2026-07-13; https://stockx.com/news/hip-hop-and-france/]

After his Celluloid years Zekri became a journalist at Actuel (grand reporter, 1985–1994), worked at Canal+, then served as editorial director at I-Télé/CNews (2001–2008), and later as director at Radio Nova (2016–2019). [source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Zekri]

Kaoma and “Lambada” (1989)

In 1989, alongside Olivier Lorsac (Discogs: 581694), Karakos assembled Kaoma (Discogs: 226971), a Brazilian-French group fronted by Loalwa Braz. The group’s “Lambada” became a worldwide commercial phenomenon, released through Epic/CBS rather than Celluloid. The record generated enormous revenue and immediate controversy over uncleared sampling of Bolivian folk music. This was Karakos’s most commercially successful project, though it arrived just as Celluloid itself was being wound down.

Closure (1989)

By 1989, Karakos sold the struggling label and operations ceased. He went on to found Distance Records (1995) and Musisoft Next (1997) under the Karakos Productions & Publishing umbrella.

Post-1989 Catalog History

Karakos sold Celluloid to American businessman John Matarazzo “for a symbolic dollar” to cover the label’s debts. Matarazzo failed to repay Celluloid’s obligations to Société Générale, the French bank that held a mortgage over the entire recording, publishing, and branding catalog. The bank reclaimed those rights.

In 1994, Société Générale sold the catalog back to Karakos and his associates via a company called Adageo (formally: Adageo BV, which purchased all rights to the Celluloid catalog and name). The catalog therefore returned to Karakos’s control roughly five years after the nominal sale. [source: Wikipedia, “Celluloid Records” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celluloid_Records, as of 2026-03-27); Wikipedia, “Jean Georgakarakos” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Georgakarakos, as of 2026-07-13)]

By 2023, Karakos Productions & Publishing (KP&P) had secured rights to exploit the Celluloid NY catalog, making KP&P the current stated rights-holder for reissue and licensing purposes. The KP&P-hosted Celluloid site (karakosproductions.com/celluloid/en/) lists licensing/sync contact via contact@celluloid and represents the catalog with a roster including The Last Poets, Time Zone, Fab 5 Freddy, Futura 2000, Manu Dibango, and Material. [source: https://www.karakosproductions.com/celluloid/en/, as of 2026-07-13]

Conflict / open question — Charly Records: The domain celluloid-records.com resolves to a Charly Records (UK) page that lists Celluloid as one of “numerous legendary labels” in their portfolio alongside BYG Records, Sun Records, and Immediate. Charly is itself controlled by Snapper Music. It is unclear whether Charly holds a separate or older licensing agreement predating or co-existing with KP&P’s 2023 rights claim, or whether the domain merely reflects a lapsed/expired arrangement. No acquisition or licensing date between Charly and Celluloid appears in trade press. [source: celluloid-records.com (via Charly/Snapper), as of 2026-07-13; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charly_Records, as of 2026-07-13] Needs resolution: contact/trade-press source to confirm which entity currently controls licensing for the US hip-hop catalog specifically (Charly has historically focused on French market reissues).

A significant mid-period reissue program was run under license with Strut Records (UK): the 2013 double-CD/vinyl compilation Change The Beat: The Celluloid Records Story 1979–1987 (Strut STRUT102CD/LP) was released in conjunction with Karakos and Celluloid, with liner notes by Vivien Goldman. [source: strut.k7store.com/artist/146504-celluloid-records; testpressing.org/magazine/change-the-beat-the-celluloid-records-story-sleevenotes-by-vivien-goldman]
