June Of 44

Origin: Louisville, KY / New York, NY
Active: 1994–2000; reunited 2018–present
Genre: Post-rock, math rock, experimental
Label: Quarterstick Records (all releases)
Discogs ID: 264305
Also known as: June Of ‘44

Biography

Formation (1994)

June Of 44 coalesced from the wreckage of Rodan (3), the Louisville, KY post-hardcore band that had released Rusty on Quarterstick in 1994 before dissolving. Guitarist/vocalist Jeff Mueller (Rodan’s co-frontman alongside Jason Noble) and drummer Doug Scharin — who had come to Rodan from Codeine, the New York slowcore band — formed the nucleus of the new group. They were joined by guitarist/vocalist Sean Meadows, who had roots in Lungfish, and bassist Fred Erskine, who had previously been in Hoover (3) and The Crownhate Ruin.

The band’s geography reflected the diaspora: Mueller and Scharin were Louisville-connected while Meadows and Erskine were embedded in the post-hardcore scenes of the East Coast. The name “June of 44” has been cited as a reference to D-Day — the Normandy landings of June 6, 1944 — though the band have never offered a definitive account.

Quarterstick signed them immediately, folding June Of 44 into the Louisville/Chicago axis that the label had been building since Rodan’s Rusty.

Debut and Early Records (1995–1996)

The debut LP Engine Takes To The Water (1995, QS32CD) was recorded by James Murphy — the same James Murphy who would later found LCD Soundsystem and DFA Records. This identification is confirmed by two independent indicators: (1) the Wikipedia article on Engine Takes to the Water hyperlinks the “James Murphy” recording credit directly to the “James Murphy (electronic musician)” article [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_Takes_to_the_Water, as of 2026-08-04]; and (2) the Plantain Recording House — where the album was tracked December 12–15, 1994 — is documented as a studio “built and operated by James Murphy of LCD Soundsystem and DFA” [https://artistagraph.com/artistagraphs/1048997?studio=Plantain+Recording+House]. At the time Murphy was an obscure engineer based in New York; this credit predates his public profile by nearly a decade. The album established the band’s core vocabulary: interlocking guitar figures, off-kilter rhythmic displacements, and sung-spoken vocals that rarely resolve into conventional melody.

Production Notes: James Murphy at Plantain (1994)

Murphy’s other confirmed 1990s engineering work at Plantain includes tracks from Antietam’s Rope-A-Dope (recorded 1994, officially released 2021) — a New York noise-rock band unconnected to the Louisville/Quarterstick scene [source: https://antietamnyc.bandcamp.com/album/rope-a-dope, liner notes]. His broader mid-1990s profile includes live sound engineering for Sub Pop band Six Finger Satellite, and membership in bands Pony (1992–1994) and Speedking (1995–1997) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Murphy_(electronic_musician), as of 2026-08-04]. No evidence has been found that Murphy engineered any other Quarterstick Records artists (Rodan, Rachel’s, Shipping News, Codeine, or related acts) during this period — the June of 44 debut appears to be his sole connection to the Louisville/Quarterstick network. The engagement was likely opportunistic: Murphy owned the Plantain studio in Brooklyn, and June of 44’s members included East Coast-based musicians (Meadows, Erskine) who could have facilitated the booking.

The same year they also released the EP The Anatomy Of Sharks (1996, QS40), which shifted engineering duties to Robert Weston (Bob Weston) — Quarterstick’s recurring house engineer and bassist for Shellac and Volcano Suns. Weston would record all of June Of 44’s subsequent studio output.

Tropics And Meridians followed later in 1996 (QS44CD), again recorded by Weston. It was the most elaborate release yet in terms of physical packaging: the sleeve incorporated original stamp artwork by multiple artists including Greg King, Col. W. L. Todd, and Louis Kaufman — in keeping with Quarterstick’s house aesthetic of fine-art, letterpress-influenced packaging.

Mature Period (1997–1999)

Four Great Points (1998, QS54CD) marked a significant expansion of the band’s sonic palette. The Discogs credits show members playing well beyond guitar-bass-drums: Erskine added trumpet and “wood & steel pole,” Scharin expanded to percussion, Moog synthesizer, and sampler, Meadows played bells and “staircase,” and Mueller added a second Moog. The record moved toward something approaching post-rock free jazz — the kind of textural exploration that aligned them with contemporaries like Tortoise rather than pure post-hardcore.

Anahata (1999, QS64CD) continued in this vein. Erskine added vibraphone-adjacent keyboard work and vocals; Scharin played vibraphone. Bob Weston again recorded the sessions. This was the last studio record before the band effectively dissolved ca. 2000.

International / Split Releases (1999)

Two additional 1999 releases document the band’s reach into the European experimental underground. In The Fishtank (Konkurrent / Atomic Recordings, Netherlands) placed them in the “In The Fishtank” session series — a Dutch label imprint designed for adventurous live-studio recordings; the session was engineered and mastered by Zlaya Hadzich. A split single, Southeast Of Boston / Dexterity Of Luck (Bcore, Spain, 1999) shows the band releasing in Spain’s then-active independent scene.

Dissolution and Side Projects (1999–2017)

The band broke up in November 1999, shortly after the release of Anahata. No member has publicly named a specific trigger for the dissolution. In a 2022 Leo Weekly interview, Mueller explicitly downplayed concurrent side projects as a factor — “Not so much. We were all working in other projects in 1999” — suggesting the end was not driven by competing commitments alone. The most substantive member account comes from Mueller in a 2020 Rolling Stone interview discussing the Revisionist album: “The session for Anahata was pretty tough; many of the songs felt underdeveloped… it all just felt rushed and messy — I had very little grasp on how to organize and play my parts. We’d never afforded ourselves the time to thoroughly think through the writing process of several of our later songs.” [source: https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/june-of-44-new-album-revisionist-rerecorded-syntax-premiere-1011510/, 2020-07-09] In a 2026 Post-Trash interview, Mueller described the cessation simply: “We stopped making music in 1999.” [source: https://post-trash.com/news/2026/6/4/ucubunygoqoxx6a57jiph5fm2utpz0] The picture that emerges is of a band that ended quietly after a difficult final record rather than through a dramatic break — the cause unspecified but the Anahata sessions’ fraught nature appears to have been a contributing exhaustion. Anahata itself was recorded in January 1999 at Electrical Audio, Chicago [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anahata_(album), as of 2025-05-31].

After June Of 44 wound down, the members dispersed across several notable projects:

  • Jeff Mueller continued in Shipping News (with Jason Noble of Rodan) and remained active in Louisville’s experimental music community. He also runs Dexterity Letterpress.
  • Sean Meadows formed The Sonora Pine (with Tara Jane O’Neil, Kevin Coultas, and Samara Lubelski) and was briefly in Lungfish-adjacent circles.
  • Fred Erskine became a core member of HiM (Discogs ID: 13240 — not the Finnish metal band) — Doug Scharin’s large ensemble project — alongside a rotating cast that at times included Adam Pierce, Carlo Cennamo, Griffin Rodriguez, Joe Goldring, Josh Larue, Julie Liu, Jon Theodore, Phil Spirito, and others. Erskine was also in Hoover (3) and The Crownhate Ruin before June Of 44.
  • Doug Scharin built an extensive career as one of the most-traveled drummers in the post-rock underground, working with Mice Parade, Enablers, Rex, and numerous others in addition to co-leading HiM.

Reunion (2018–present)

The band reunited in 2018 with the original four members. In 2020, they released Revisionist (Adaptations & Future Histories In The Time Of Love And Survival) (La Tempesta Dischi, Italy). Basic tracking (tracks 1–7) was done by David Lenci, with Bob Weston returning to record track 8. The album was mixed by John McEntire of Tortoise — a fitting connection given both bands’ position in the 1990s post-rock landscape. A live document, Live In Tokyo (Imakinn Records, 2026), documents the reunion touring.

Open Questions

Research Queue

  • How did June of 44 come to record at James Murphy’s Plantain studio in December 1994 — was there a personal connection via the East Coast members (Erskine, Meadows), or did Quarterstick broker the booking? No interview or liner note has addressed this directly.
  • What other bands (besides June of 44 and Antietam) recorded at Plantain Recording House during Murphy’s Brooklyn-era operation (1993–c.1999)? A full Plantain client list would clarify Murphy’s scene adjacencies before DFA.
  • What was the specific November 1999 final show (venue, city, date)? The artinstl.com piece states the band “broke up” in November 1999 without specifying the last performance. [source: http://theartsstl.com/june-of-44-revisionist-adaptations-future-histories-in-the-time-of-love-and-survival-broken-clover/]
  • Was the Anahata stylistic departure (toward dub/soul/psychedelia, per DiCrescenzo’s Pitchfork review) a deliberate creative choice or a symptom of the band’s inability to cohere around the material? Mueller’s “rushed and messy” account suggests the latter — but no other member has commented on record.
