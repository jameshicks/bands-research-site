Little Feat

American rock band formed in Los Angeles in 1969. Discogs artist ID: 258557. Active 1970–present (with a break 1979–1987 following the death of founder Lowell George). Known for blending rock, R&B, funk, blues, country, and jazz into a distinctive Southern California sound driven by Lowell George’s slide guitar, Bill Payne’s keyboards, and a tight rhythm section.

Formation and Origins

Little Feat grew out of the dissolution of The Mothers of Invention when Lowell George and Roy Estrada departed (or were fired by Frank Zappa) in 1969. George had already been writing songs — including “Willin’,” which Zappa had rejected — and used the split as an opportunity to form his own band. The name is a pun on “little feet,” inspired partly by Lowell George’s small feet, partly referencing the Mothers.

Original lineup (1969–1970) crystallized around George, Estrada (bass), Richie Hayward (drums, who had played with George in The Factory and The Fraternity of Man), and Bill Payne (keyboards). Hayward and Payne were recruited through local Los Angeles connections.

Lowell George Era (1971–1979)

The band signed to Warner Bros. Records and released their self-titled debut in 1971, produced by Russ Titelman. The record established the core sound but sold poorly. Roy Estrada left after the debut, departing to join Captain Beefheart’s Magic Band.

For the second album Sailin’ Shoes (1972), the lineup expanded with the additions of Paul Barrere (guitar), Kenny Gradney (bass, ex-Delaney & Bonnie & Friends), and Sam Clayton (congas/percussion, also ex-Delaney & Bonnie). Sailin’ Shoes was co-produced by Ted Templeman and Lowell George; Lenny Waronker served as executive producer. This seven-piece lineup — George, Payne, Hayward, Barrere, Gradney, Clayton — would remain the core for the rest of the decade.

Dixie Chicken (1973) was produced by George alone and introduced New Orleans second-line rhythms more explicitly. Guest vocalists included Bonnie Raitt and Bonnie Bramlett. Cover art by Neon Park (Martin Muller) began here — Park would design most of the band’s Warner Bros. sleeves. Dixie Chicken sold badly on release and the band briefly dissolved; members scattered to session work before regrouping.

Feats Don’t Fail Me Now (1974) was the commercial breakthrough, again produced by Lowell George. Backing vocalists included Bonnie Raitt and Emmylou Harris. The album reached the US Top 40.

The Last Record Album (1975), produced by George, was the first LP where the internal creative tension between George and the rest of the band became audible — Paul Barrere, Bill Payne, and others began contributing more original material, pulling the sound toward jazz-funk. Engineer George Massenburg worked on the sessions.

Time Loves A Hero (1977) marked a turning point: produced by Ted Templeman, it featured prominent contributions from the Tower of Power Horn Section (arranged by Greg Adams) and moved further toward the jazz-funk direction favored by Barrere and Payne. George was increasingly disengaged from the recording process.

Waiting for Columbus (1978) is the band’s landmark live double album, recorded at the Rainbow Theatre in London and the Lisner Auditorium in Washington, D.C. Produced by Lowell George, it captures the classic seven-piece lineup at their peak with Tower of Power horns and Mick Taylor guesting on one track. Recording by George Massenburg, Andy Bloch, and others; mixed at Sunset Sound Recorders and Westlake. The album reached #18 on the US charts, their highest charting release in the George era.

Down on the Farm (1979) was recorded before George’s death and assembled posthumously; George produced it as a final act. The cover painting was again by Neon Park.

Lowell George’s Death

Lowell George (born April 13, 1945, Hollywood, CA; born Lowell Thomas George) died June 29, 1979 in Arlington, Virginia, of heart failure exacerbated by drug use while on a solo tour. He had released one solo album, Thanks, I’ll Eat It Here, earlier in 1979. His death effectively ended the original band. They released Down on the Farm (1979) and the compilation Hoy-Hoy! (1981) before going on indefinite hiatus.

Hiatus and Reunion (1979–1988)

Band members pursued various projects during the hiatus. Bill Payne did extensive session work. The band reconvened in 1987–1988 with a new lineup; Lowell George was irreplaceable as a frontman and the band recruited Craig Fuller (ex-Pure Prairie League, ex-American Flyer) as the new lead vocalist.

Let It Roll (1988, Warner Bros.) was the reunion album. Produced by Bill Payne and George Massenburg, the six-piece lineup was Fuller, Payne, Hayward, Barrere, Gradney, Clayton, plus Fred Tackett (guitar/mandolin/trumpet, who had contributed to Dixie Chicken as a session player). Guest vocalists on the album included Bonnie Raitt, Linda Ronstadt, Bob Seger, and Shaun Murphy. The album reached the US Top 40.

Representing the Mambo (1990) and Shake Me Up (1991, on Polydor) followed. Craig Fuller eventually left and was replaced by Shaun Murphy as permanent vocalist.

Later Years (1990s–present)

The band moved through labels — Zoo Entertainment (Ain’t Had Enough Fun, 1995), SPV (Under the Radar, 1998; Chinese Work Songs, 2000) — while maintaining an active touring schedule. Hotcakes & Outtakes (2000, Rhino/Warner Archives) was a major four-disc retrospective box set.

Richie Hayward died August 12, 2010 (born February 6, 1946) after a long illness. Paul Barrere died October 26, 2019, after battling liver cancer.

Tony Leone took over on drums, and Scott Sharrard joined on guitar to replace Barrere. Rooster Rag (2012, Rounder Records) was among the later studio albums; Sam’s Place (2024) and Strike Up the Band (2025) appear on their own Hot Tomato Records imprint, with Bill Payne remaining the central figure and sole original member.
