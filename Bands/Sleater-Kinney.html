Sleater-Kinney

Discogs ID: 218269
Formed: 1994, Olympia, Washington, USA
Genre: Indie rock, riot grrrl, post-punk
Active: 1994–2006 (hiatus), 2014–present

Alternative rock band from the American Pacific Northwest. Formed in Olympia, Washington in 1994, later based in Portland, Oregon. Went on indefinite hiatus in 2006; reformed in 2014. Named after Sleater-Kinney Road in Lacey, WA.

Background

Originally a side project of Tucker and Brownstein. Both had prior bands: Tucker in Heavens To Betsy (2) (with Tracy Sawyer) and Cadallaca; Brownstein in Excuse 17. Laura Macfarlane had been in Australian band Ninetynine. Janet Weiss, who joined in 1997, was simultaneously a member of Quasi (2) with Sam Coomes.

The band was central to the Olympia riot grrrl scene and survived its mainstream-backlash cycle. Released early records on Chainsaw Records (Donna Dresch’s label); moved to Kill Rock Stars from Dig Me Out (1997) onward. After a 2004 session with Dave Fridmann, signed to Sub Pop for The Woods (2005). See “Sub Pop Signing” section below for how that deal came about.

Sleater-Kinney also released on Matador Records for European single distribution (1998–1999) and moved to Mom + Pop (2019) and then Loma Vista (2024) in the post-reunion era.

Sub Pop Signing (2004–2005)

After One Beat (2002), Sleater-Kinney’s contract with Kill Rock Stars expired. The band’s publicist and booking agent both retired around the same time, leaving the band’s situation in flux — Corin Tucker later noted: “There was a lot of stuff in question about the future of Sleater-Kinney.”

The Sub Pop relationship was initiated by Steve Manning, a Sub Pop rep who approached the band at a live show in fall 2004 and pitched them directly: “You guys should be on Sub Pop!” Brownstein later said the band chose Sub Pop for the enthusiasm they showed: “I think ultimately it boiled down to their excitement and their feeling that we belonged there.” (Seattle Times, May 30, 2005)

Sub Pop co-founder Jonathan Poneman was closely involved. On hearing the finished album, Poneman called Sub Pop general manager Megan Jasper and said: “I was really shocked, the first time I heard it. I remember calling Megan and saying, ‘Did she just do a guitar solo? What is going on?‘” (Seattle Times, May 30, 2005)

Recording with Dave Fridmann at Tarbox Road Studios, Cassadaga, NY began November 5, 2004 and ran through December 2004. The Woods was released May 24, 2005. The move to Sub Pop also gave the band access to wider distribution than Kill Rock Stars had provided. (Punknews.org, date of signing announcement c. late 2004; Stereogum, 2025)

Note on Steve Manning’s role: The Seattle Times article (May 2005) identifies Manning as a “Sub Pop rep” without specifying A&R or a more precise title. His exact role at Sub Pop in 2004 has not been confirmed in a secondary source.

Departure of Janet Weiss (2019)

Weiss announced her departure via Instagram on July 1, 2019 — six weeks before The Center Won’t Hold was released — citing the band’s “new direction.” Her initial statement was brief. Brownstein responded on Instagram on July 16, writing: “What am I supposed to say? She left. We asked her to stay. We tried. It’s hard and sad.” (Brooklyn Vegan, 2019-07-16)

Weiss gave her first full account in November 2019 on the podcast The Trap Set with Joe Wong. The core issue was creative parity, not specifically St. Vincent’s production: Weiss said she asked Brownstein and Tucker directly whether she was still a creative equal in the band, and they said no. She also described the album’s remote, GarageBand/Dropbox songwriting process as alienating — “The new record was made sort of without me” — and said she felt she could not authentically perform songs she had not contributed to. The band had attempted counseling to resolve differences before she left. (Willamette Week, 2019-11-20; Stereogum, 2019-11-20)

St. Vincent’s production was part of the broader context — The Center Won’t Hold shifted toward synth-pop and programmatic beats — but Weiss’s stated grievance was specifically about her diminished creative role within the band, not St. Vincent’s involvement per se. Press reporting at the time (Spin, NME, Consequence) framed the split as inseparable from the album’s sharp stylistic pivot, noting that Weiss was largely absent during recording. Brownstein confirmed this indirectly, noting Weiss had “raved about this album” yet still left. No party has identified St. Vincent’s involvement as the explicit cause of friction.
