Liquid Liquid

From: New York City Active: 1980–1984 (original); intermittent reunions from c. 2004 Label: 99 Records Discogs ID: 5454

Overview

Liquid Liquid were one of the two central acts on Ed Bahlman’s 99 Records label — the other being ESG. A four-piece whose sound combined post-punk minimalism with funk percussion and marimba, they occupied an unusual space in the downtown NYC scene: too rhythmically driven for No Wave purists, too abrasive and abstract for disco. They coined the term “big beat” for their own sound — a phrase that English producers would repurpose for something entirely different a decade later.

Their song “Cavern” (from the Optimo EP, 1983) became one of the most litigated records of the decade after Sugar Hill Records’ house band re-performed its bass line for Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five’s “White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It).” The subsequent lawsuit won 99 Records a $660,000 judgment it never collected — Sugar Hill declared bankruptcy before paying, and the legal costs contributed to the label’s collapse.

History

Liquid Idiot → Liquid Liquid (1980)

The predecessor band was Liquid Idiot — Scott Hartley and Richard McGuire plus Bob Seanus and Ken “Man” Caldeira. They released at least one 7” before reforming. The pivot to Liquid Liquid came from an open-percussion experiment: the band would invite audience members to bring their own instruments to gigs. At one such show, Dennis Young arrived with a marimba; they were impressed enough to invite him to join. Salvatore Principato completed the lineup.

The marimba became the band’s defining sound element — an unusual timbre for the post-punk downtown context, closer to West African or Caribbean percussion traditions than anything in the No Wave genealogy.

99 Records period (1981–1984)

Ed Bahlman’s 99 Records store at 99 MacDougal Street, Greenwich Village was the natural home. Bahlman produced all Liquid Liquid releases. Their four-EP run is compact and consistent:

“Bellhead / Push” (99 Records, 1981) — debut 7”; Bahlman producer, Howie Weinberg lacquer cut.

Liquid Liquid EP (99-07, 1981) — the self-titled 12”; core lineup plus extended session personnel: Richard Edson on trumpet, Al Diaz on metalphones, Bill Kleinsmith on congas. McGuire designed the sleeve.

Successive Reflexes (99-09, 1981) — recorded and mixed by Bruce Tovsky and Greg Curry; Bahlman produced and co-mixed.

Optimo (99-11, 1983) — the peak release. Contains “Cavern” and the title track. Don Hünerberg tape engineer; Bahlman producer; Howie Weinberg mastering. “Optimo” is the track that the Glasgow DJ/label collective Optimo Music named themselves after. The Optimo EP is the most-pressed 99 Records release (6 pressings), and also the most widely counterfeited.

The band split in 1984.

The “White Lines” lawsuit

The Sugar Hill Records house band did not sample “Cavern” — they re-performed the bass riff for “White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It)” (Grandmaster Flash & Melle Mel, 1983). Bahlman sued. 99 Records won: $660,000 in damages. Sugar Hill declared bankruptcy on November 22, 1985 before paying anything. 99 collected nothing and had spent approximately $60,000 in legal costs — which drained the store. Liquid Liquid had already split before the case resolved.

Dig We Must (99-13, 1985) — a posthumous compilation of unreleased material assembled by Bahlman after the breakup. Don Hünerberg engineered; Bahlman mixed and produced. The final 99 Records release.

Reissues and revivals

Mo Wax period (1997–1998): James Lavelle’s Mo Wax label reissued the 99 Records catalog. Liquid Liquid (Mo Wax, 1997) and Remixes (Mo Wax, 1998) brought the band to a new audience through the mid-90s rare groove and trip-hop world.

Rush Hour reunion (2004): “Signal Up Ahead / Flextone” (Rush Hour (4), Netherlands, 2004) — a new 12” single, the first new recording since the split.

Domino period (2008): Slip In And Out Of Phenomenon (Domino, 2008) — comprehensive retrospective compilation plus new material. The band produced the archival tracks themselves; Bruce Tovsky oversaw reissue production; Elliott Sharp contributed bass clarinet; Sasha Frere-Jones wrote the liner notes. Remixes (Domino, 2008) appeared alongside it.

LCD Soundsystem, Madison Square Garden (2011): LCD Soundsystem chose Liquid Liquid to open their farewell Madison Square Garden show — an explicit acknowledgment of the band’s influence on the DFA Records axis.

Extended Cast

Richard Edson — played trumpet on the self-titled EP and Slip In And Out. At the time of the EP (1981), Edson was the original drummer of Sonic Youth (1981–82), leaving before they recorded their first album. He subsequently became an actor, appearing in Ferris Bueller’s Day Off (1986) and Spike Lee’s Do the Right Thing (1989).

Al Diaz — played metalphones on the self-titled EP and Slip In And Out. Born 1959; first-generation NYC subway graffiti artist known as “BOMB-1.” Collaborated with Jean-Michel Basquiat on the SAMO© graffiti project in lower Manhattan, 1978–1980. Also played percussion on “Beat Bop” (1983), the hip-hop record produced by Basquiat for Rammellzee & K-Rob.

Richard McGuire — beyond Liquid Liquid, McGuire became a celebrated graphic artist. His 6-page comic strip Here (published in RAW magazine, 1989) was a formally radical work using overlapping time frames within a fixed domestic setting; it was expanded into a full graphic novel published by Pantheon in 2014. He is also a regular illustrator for The New Yorker.

Predecessor Band

Liquid Idiot — the pre-Liquid Liquid band: Scott Hartley, Richard McGuire, Bob Seanus, and Ken “Man” Caldeira. Released at least one 7”. Hartley and McGuire carried over into Liquid Liquid; the other two did not.

The Idiot Orchestra — larger NYC ensemble including all four Liquid Liquid members plus Ken Caldeira (ex-Liquid Idiot) and others; Big City (1980, self-released) features the core four.

”Cavern” / “White Lines” — Full Context

“Cavern” appears on Optimo (1983). Its bass riff — written by Richard McGuire — was the basis for Grandmaster Flash & Melle Mel’s “White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It)” (Sugar Hill Records, 1983). Sugar Hill’s house band re-performed the line rather than sampling it directly, which complicated the legal situation. 99 Records sued Sugar Hill Records for copyright infringement. The court found in 99’s favor and awarded $660,000 in damages. Sugar Hill Records declared bankruptcy on November 22, 1985, before paying any of it. 99 Records collected nothing and had spent approximately $60,000 in legal costs — which substantially drained the store and contributed to its closure.

Liquid Liquid had already split in 1984, before the case resolved. Ed Bahlman has since withheld master tapes from all licensing and reissue requests, at any price.
