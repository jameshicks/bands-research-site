Harmonia 76

Project name for the 1976 recording sessions of Harmonia (Hans-Joachim Roedelius, Dieter Moebius, Michael Rother) with Brian Eno. Discogs artist ID: 48044.

Rooted in the Cluster orbit — the sessions directly preceded and influenced Sowiesoso (1976) and Cluster & Eno (1977). Released much later as Tracks and Traces (Grönland Records).

Eno and the Sessions

Chronological note: Discreet Music came first

Discreet Music was released in 1975, a full year before the Forst sessions (September 1976). Eno’s ambient approach was therefore already established — the Harmonia collaboration reinforced and extended it rather than initiating it. Eno had also already played onstage with Harmonia at The Fabrik, Hamburg, in 1974, and had been vocal about his admiration for their records before any studio work occurred. (Wikipedia — Tracks and Traces, as of 2026-06-29)

The “most important band” claim

Eno is widely quoted calling Harmonia “the most important band in the world” (variant: “the world’s most important rock group”), attributed to the early-to-mid 1970s upon hearing their records. The original source of this quote has not been reliably traced in published interviews; it circulates via Wikipedia and secondary music press without a primary citation. No published interview or book passage has been located confirming the exact context or venue of this statement. This should be treated as widely attested but unverified at primary-source level.

The Forst sessions (September 1976)

Eleven days of communal recording in Forst, Lower Saxony, at Harmonia’s studio. No commercial release was planned; the sessions were improvisational and lo-fi, with no overdubs. Twenty-seven pieces were recorded. Money was tight — one of Eno’s practical contributions was bringing the recording tape, which was expensive. He brought a four-track recorder and a VCS3 synthesizer. The sessions ended when Eno left to work with David Bowie on the Berlin albums. (Wikipedia — Tracks and Traces, as of 2026-06-29; Grönland Records artist page)

What participants said

Michael Rother on the atmosphere:

“It was a very casual, a very relaxed situation…. There was no pressure as it was just four musicians throwing ideas around and sharing the joy of making some music, with no intention of releasing anything at the time.”

Rother also noted Eno was “very pleasant and interested,” not aloof. (Grönland Records artist page)

Hans-Joachim Roedelius on what Eno contributed:

“With Eno a spirit with great openness, endless joy of playing and an enormous wealth of experience in the field of popular new music came into play, and this was apparently able to open the already closed door.”

Roedelius also said Eno worked “so differently from how he normally works on Cluster & Eno,” and in a separate remark described the mutual learning: “I learnt a lot from working with [Eno]: to focus on certain things, and not put too much material on tape.” (Quietus Roedelius interview; Driving Me Backwards blog)

Roedelius also offered a pointed reversal of the usual influence narrative: “It changed the work of Brian Eno. It’s the other way around.” (Driving Me Backwards)

Eno’s documented account: what is and isn’t on the record

No published interview, liner note, or book passage has been located in which Eno explicitly connects the Forst sessions to Discreet Music or the Ambient series. This gap has now been directly investigated against the following sources (October 2026 web research):

Liner notes — neither release carries Eno-authored notes. The 1997 Rykodisc release liner notes were written by Stephen Iliffe. The 2009 Grönland reissue liner notes were also written by Stephen Iliffe. No Eno essay or statement has been found in either package’s accessible credits or descriptions. (Wikipedia — Tracks and Traces; Record Collector, October 2009, via MDTS)

More Dark Than Shark archive — checked, no explicit connection found. Three MDTS pages were examined:

  • eno_int_reccollec-oct09.html: Record Collector, October 2009 (Ian Shirley). Only Michael Rother is interviewed; Eno makes no statements.
  • eno_int_dminds-oct15.html: Dangerous Minds, October 2015 (Oliver Hall, interviewing Rother). The sole Eno quotes on record here are “the world’s most important rock band” and a description of Forst as “a small anarchic state” — neither connects Forst to ambient development. Eno is not the interviewee.
  • eno_int_q-feb97.html: Q Magazine, February 1997 — this page is actually a David Bowie interview; no Eno statements about Harmonia.
  • The MDTS interview index was checked; no entry specifically targeting Harmonia, Forst, or Tracks and Traces as subject of an Eno interview was identified. An index entry exists for “ENO/MOEBIUS/ROEDELIUS: AFTER THE HEAT” (May 1979, Trouser Press) covering the later Cluster & Eno collaboration, but not the 1976 sessions.

1997-era interviews — none located. No Eno interview from the period around the November 1997 Rykodisc release has been found in which he addresses the Forst sessions’ relationship to his ambient work. The MDTS archive does not appear to contain a contemporary 1997 Eno interview on this topic.

The “small anarchic state” quote is the only Eno-authored characterisation of Forst that has been located in any searchable source. It appears via the Dangerous Minds/MDTS page (Rother interview, October 2015) and does not touch on musical influence or ambient development. (MDTS/Dangerous Minds)

What is documented is the general influence trajectory: Harmonia’s albums Music Von Harmonia (1974) and Deluxe (1975) exerted influence on Eno’s musical thinking before the Forst sessions took place. Critics and secondary sources draw the line from Eno’s engagement with Cluster and Harmonia through to Another Green World (1975), the Berlin albums with Bowie, Music for Films, and the Ambient series — but these are critical observations, not Eno’s own documented statements connecting the specific 1976 recordings to that arc. (Wikipedia — Harmonia (band), as of 2026-06-29)

The audio evidence is suggestive: the 15-minute track “Sometimes In Autumn” on Tracks and Traces features extended drone qualities similar to Ambient 1: Music for Airports (1978), and the synth textures of the album anticipate the sonic palette of the Bowie Berlin sides. But the causal link in Eno’s own voice remains undocumented across all sources searched. (6 Album Sunday blog)

Open gap — status after October 2026 web research

The specific Eno-authored account (if it exists) connecting Forst 1976 to his ambient development has not been located despite direct checks of: MDTS archive (three pages including the two most relevant Tracks and Traces entries), 1997-era MDTS interview index, both reissue liner note attributions, and general search for 1997 Eno interviews on Harmonia. Remaining unread candidate sources: full text of the 1997 Rykodisc liner notes (Iliffe) — these could cite or quote Eno even though they are not by him; any Eno print interviews not archived at MDTS from the November 1997 release window (Wire, Mojo, Q from that period); the “ENO/MOEBIUS/ROEDELIUS: AFTER THE HEAT” Trouser Press piece (May 1979) which may contain Eno statements about the broader Cluster/Harmonia collaboration influencing his direction.

Conny Plank’s role — web research note

Short answer: Conny Plank is not credited on, and is not mentioned in connection with, the 1976 Forst sessions that became Tracks and Traces.

Sources checked (July 2026):

  • Wikipedia — Tracks and Traces (as of 2026-07-14): no engineering credits for the original 1976 sessions; remastering for the 1997 release credited to Eric Spitzer-Marlyn and Othmar Eichinger only.
  • Wikipedia — Conny Plank (as of 2026-07-14): lists Plank’s discography; Harmonia — Deluxe (1975) appears; Tracks and Traces / Harmonia 76 does not appear.
  • Tape Op — Michael Rother interview, issue 110: Rother discusses Plank’s work on Deluxe (brought his mobile 16-track MCI to Forst); no mention of Plank at the 1976 sessions.
  • Grönland Records — Harmonia & Eno 76 artist page: no Plank credit or mention.
  • Driving Me Backwards — Tracks and Traces (1997): no Plank mention.
  • 6 Album Sunday — Eno’s Forst Years: Plank mentioned only in the context of the 1979 Cluster album Grosses Wasser, not the 1976 sessions.

The consistent picture across all sources: the September 1976 sessions were self-recorded by the four participants (Eno, Rother, Moebius, Roedelius) on Eno’s four-track recorder at Harmonia’s Forst studio. No outside engineer was present. Plank’s documented Harmonia involvement ends with Deluxe (1975).

No source consulted explicitly states “Plank was not there” — the absence is inferential from: (a) no credit anywhere, (b) the self-recording account of the sessions, (c) Plank’s own discography not listing the album, and (d) Rother’s Tape Op interview discussing Plank’s Deluxe role without mentioning any role in 1976.

The liner notes to the 1997 Rykodisc release (written by Stephen Iliffe) have not been read directly — this is the one source that could definitively settle the question either way. If a copy of the physical liner notes becomes available, that should be checked.
