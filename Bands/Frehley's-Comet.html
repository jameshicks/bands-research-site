Frehley’s Comet

American hard rock band formed by Ace Frehley after leaving Kiss. Active 1987–1989 (with pre-band activity from 1985). Discogs artist ID: 610999

Overview

Frehley’s Comet was the vehicle through which Ace Frehley rebuilt his solo career in the mid-to-late 1980s following his 1982 departure from Kiss. The name combined Frehley’s surname with a reference to Halley’s Comet, which made its famous 1986 pass. The band released three proper studio/live albums under the Frehley’s Comet name on Atlantic/Megaforce Worldwide (1987–1988), with a fourth album (Trouble Walkin’, 1989) credited to Ace Frehley solo but using largely the same personnel.

The core lineup on the debut was Ace Frehley (guitar, lead vocals), Anton Fig (drums), John Regan (bass, backing vocals), and Tod Howarth (guitar, keyboards, lead and backing vocals). Produced by Eddie Kramer and Frehley, the debut LP was a commercial success, reaching the Billboard 200. A pre-formation promotional single, “Back in the Groove” (1985, Happy Frog Records), featured Frehley with Regan, Fig, Howarth, Richie Scarlet, and Arthur Stead on keyboards — the earliest documented Comet-era recording.

Jon Zazula (Megaforce) served as executive producer across the catalog. The label relationship placed Frehley’s Comet at Atlantic via Megaforce Worldwide, the distribution imprint of Megaforce Records, the New Jersey metal/hard rock label Zazula co-founded with his wife Marsha Zazula.

The HENDRING VHS drops “Words Are Not Enough” (the one studio bonus track on Live + 1, produced by Eddie Kramer) and adds six additional live performances — including two KISS-era Ace Frehley songs (“Cold Gin”, “Shock Me”) and three Frehley’s Comet singles (“Into The Night”, “Insane”, “It’s Over Now”). The conclusion: Live… + 4 is a distinct and larger live set on a different format (video vs. audio), not merely a repackage of Live + 1. It shares 4 of 5 Live + 1 tracks but expands the set considerably, particularly with KISS-era material.

Tod Howarth Departure — Research Notes (Web)

Howarth left before the Trouble Walkin’ sessions began. The departure was his own decision, driven by a label directive that removed him from songwriting. The sequence, sourced from Howarth interviews:

  1. Final Frehley’s Comet show: August 1988, opening for Iron Maiden. The Iron Maiden support tour also ended the band’s run as a working unit — they ran out of money and effectively went bankrupt on that tour. (Wikipedia: Frehley’s Comet, as of 2026-05-06)
  2. Label directive after Second Sighting: Atlantic / Megaforce told Frehley that the next album should have Ace handling all songwriting and lead vocals — no co-writing input from Howarth. (metalheadzone.com)
  3. Howarth’s decision: With songwriting royalties and publishing being his primary income (he had three children and a mortgage), he could not justify staying on as a salaried sideman with no writing credit. He approached Frehley directly, told him he needed to find a solo deal or form another band, and Frehley was reportedly supportive: “He understood and said, ‘I wish you wouldn’t go, but I understand what you have to do.‘” (metalheadzone.com)
  4. Jamie Oldaker also departed at the same time, before year-end 1988. (Wikipedia: Frehley’s Comet, as of 2026-05-06)
  5. Replacements: Richie Scarlet stepped into the guitar/vocal role Howarth had held; Sandy Slavin appears on one track. Anton Fig returned for most tracks of Trouble Walkin’.
  6. Name change: For the 1989 album Frehley also dropped the “Frehley’s Comet” band name, partly because concert promoters had been billing them as “Ace Frehley’s Comet” anyway. (Wikipedia: Frehley’s Comet, as of 2026-05-06)

Summary: The absence of Howarth on Trouble Walkin’ was not a silent creative shift — it was a label-driven restructuring that made Howarth’s continued participation financially untenable. Howarth made the call to leave; Frehley did not fire him. The timeline is clear: Comet disbanded as a touring entity in August 1988, Howarth and Oldaker exited before year-end, and the Trouble Walkin’ sessions proceeded in 1989 without them.

Source quality: The metalheadzone.com piece quotes Howarth directly. Wikipedia corroborates the August 1988 final show date and end-of-year departure. No contrary account found.
