Polyrock

Post-punk / new wave band. New York City. Formed 1978. Discogs ID: 119356.

Sibling band to The Dance, both emerging from the breakup of Model Citizens (3). Led by singer/guitarist Billy Robertson with vocalist Catherine Oblasney, guitarist Tommy Robertson, drummer Joseph Yannece, keyboard player Lenny Aaron, and bassist Curt Cosentino. Keyboard-heavy pattern-based sound strongly reminiscent of Philip Glass’s minimalism — Glass performed on their first two albums and produced them.

Both debut Polyrock (RCA Victor, 1980) and Changing Hearts (RCA Victor, 1981) were co-produced by Philip Glass and Kurt Munkacsi, with Glass adding additional keyboards. Romantic Me (RCA, 1980) also has a Munkacsi producer credit.

RCA Victor signing — context

Primary source — Glass’s own account. A ca.-1981 radio interview with Philip Glass, conducted by Brooke Wentz for Transfigured Night on Columbia University’s WKCR-FM, is the clearest account of how the deal was structured. An excerpt was published in Interview Magazine in December 2023 as part of promotion for Wentz’s book Transfigured New York: Interviews with Experimental Artists and Musicians, 1980–1990 (Columbia University Press, 2023). In the interview Glass states:

  • He got the idea of producing a pop record from Brian Eno, who told him “he really liked producing records, and he thought it might be fun for me to do it.”
  • RCA then approached Glass directly about producing a rock band. Glass said: “Well, if I like the music, I might.”
  • Glass selected Polyrock himself: “I like them a lot. They play new wave, they’re young guys, they have a lot of energy.”
  • He noted the project took “a good month of work, which for me is a lot of time,” and that he was not looking to produce more rock records afterward.

(Interview Magazine / Wentz excerpt, Dec 2023; Columbia University Press)

The signing direction is therefore confirmed: RCA approached Glass, not the reverse. Glass had come to the label’s attention through his high profile following Einstein on the Beach (1976, toured internationally) and a general moment when the minimalist/downtown scene was receiving mainstream press attention. Glass chose Polyrock from among whatever options RCA presented — the band was not signed and then assigned a producer. The contract was with Glass (and Munkacsi) as the production entity; the band came as part of that arrangement.

The Glass/Munkacsi production entity. Post-Punk Monk’s 2016 review of Above The Fruited Plain states: “By that time Philip Glass no longer had RCA cash to rope him in, so this one was produced by Billy Robertson of the band.” (source) The phrase “RCA cash to rope him in” is consistent with Glass’s own account — RCA was paying Glass as producer/talent, and his departure from the arrangement ended the major-label run.

Glass’s label trajectory at the time. Glass had self-released on his own Chatham Square label through the mid-1970s; Einstein on the Beach appeared on Tomato Records in 1979. The Polyrock RCA albums (1980–81) were a parallel pop/production venture before Glass signed an exclusive composer’s contract with CBS Masterworks in 1981. Glass had no direct prior recording relationship with RCA Victor for his own work. (Glass label timeline: Encyclopedia.com)

RCA’s broader experimental/new wave window, ca. 1979–1982. Around the same period RCA was also releasing Daryl Hall’s Robert Fripp-produced Sacred Songs (held since 1977, released March 1980) and Klaus Nomi’s debut (1981). This suggests RCA had an ad hoc appetite for art-credibility acts at this moment. (Sacred Songs context: Wikipedia; Klaus Nomi context: Wikipedia)

Contemporary cultural framing. A December 1980 Christian Science Monitor article documented Glass and Munkacsi as Polyrock’s producers and situated the project within a broader crossover moment when “serious composers were directly engaging pop audiences” — citing minimalism and rock’s shared “regular pulse, a high energy level, a lot of repetition, and a beat,” with Steve Reich performing at the Bottom Line as a parallel example. (CSMonitor, 15 Dec 1980)

Band’s perspective on RCA. In a 1981 FFanzeen interview, Billy Robertson expressed frustration with RCA’s marketing — specifically the “Polyvinyl, Polyrock of the Future” campaign — saying “We really hated it, but I’m not going to turn around and say ‘RCA stinks.’ They just got a little away from us.” Robertson gave no account of how the deal was made. (FFanzeen, 1981, republished 2017)

Promo radio special. RCA issued a promotional 12” radio special (RCA Special Radio Series, Vol. III, cat. DJL1-3831, 1980) featuring Polyrock tracks interspersed with Glass and Munkacsi interview segments, produced by Thirsty Ear Productions — consistent with RCA treating the Glass/Munkacsi name as the primary marketing hook. (Amazon listing)

Summary. RCA solicited Glass as a name-producer following Einstein on the Beach’s cultural impact. Brian Eno had encouraged Glass to try producing rock records; when RCA came to him, Glass agreed conditionally and chose Polyrock from the options presented. The band gained RCA as a label as a consequence of that Glass/Munkacsi production deal, not through independent A&R outreach.

2026-09-26 update: A primary source was located — Philip Glass’s ca.-1981 WKCR radio interview with Brooke Wentz, published as an excerpt in Interview Magazine (Dec 2023) from Transfigured New York (Columbia University Press, 2023). Glass’s own account confirms: RCA approached him (not the reverse), Eno had encouraged him to try producing rock records, and Glass selected Polyrock himself from whatever options RCA presented. The “RCA Victor signing” section above has been revised from inferential to confirmed-by-primary-source.

What remains unconfirmed: Which specific RCA A&R executive initiated contact with Glass; whether Munkacsi had any independent prior relationship with RCA; what other bands RCA may have offered Glass before he chose Polyrock.

2026-10-19 update — Glass memoir Words Without Music (Liveright, 2015).

Checked whether the memoir mentions Polyrock or the RCA deal. Conclusion: almost certainly not covered.

The memoir’s table of contents (Part I: Baltimore through Nadia Boulanger / India; Part II: Return to New York through Einstein on the Beach; Part III: Opera / Music and Film / Candy Jernigan / The Cocteau Trilogy) contains no chapter or section addressing rock production or Polyrock. The Broad Street Review (broadstreetreview.com) notes that Glass uses 282 of 396 pages to reach Einstein on the Beach (1975–76), leaving roughly 114 pages for everything after — a span covering his entire opera cycle, multiple film scores, and personal relationships. No review (Kirkus, Slate, Music & Literature, Broad Street Review, Austin Chronicle, Dallas Morning News) mentions Polyrock, RCA pop production, or Kurt Munkacsi. The memoir’s thematic structure focuses on Glass’s compositional and operatic career, not production-for-hire work.

No dedicated interviews with Kurt Munkacsi discussing the Polyrock sessions were located in any source.

Sources checked for memoir content: Broad Street Review; Kirkus Reviews; Music & Literature; Slate; table of contents via web search, bookey.app summary; Philip Glass official site.
