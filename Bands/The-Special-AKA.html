The Special AKA

Continuation of The Specials led by Jerry Dammers. Discogs artist ID: 197436. Active 1982–1985.

Overview

After Terry Hall, Neville Staple, and Lynval Golding departed in 1981, Jerry Dammers reconstituted the band as The Special AKA with a largely new lineup. Released In The Studio (1984) on Two-Tone/Chrysalis — the album took years to complete due to Dammers’ perfectionism and label tensions. “Nelson Mandela” (1984) became the anti-apartheid anthem, reaching UK #9. The band dissolved in 1985.

In The Studio — Production History

Recording sessions began in late 1981/early 1982 following the original Specials lineup fracture, but the album did not appear until June 15, 1984 — a gap of roughly two and a half years. Multiple interlocking causes account for the delay.

Dammers’ perfectionism. Dammers was, by all accounts, an extraordinarily exacting producer who refused to accept takes he considered imperfect. Rhoda Dakar recalled: “My memories are of going home in tears after singing one line of a lyric over and over for about five hours. I went home sobbing in a cab most nights.” (marcoonthebass.blogspot.com; en.apoplife.nl) Beyond sessions themselves, Dammers would spend long nights alone in the studio after the band had left, endlessly reworking mixes while studio bills accumulated. (findyl.co.uk) He treated the project as an ideals assignment where “money and career planning weren’t even considered. The message was all important.” (en.apoplife.nl)

Geographic fragmentation. Some band members were based in Coventry while others remained in London, making assembling the full lineup for sessions a persistent logistical obstacle. (marcoonthebass.blogspot.com)

Lineup instability. Personnel turned over repeatedly during the recording period. Bassist Horace Panter departed early, reportedly alienated by the oppressive studio atmosphere. Stan Campbell joined in 1983 as lead vocalist with expectations of commercial breakthrough that the grinding sessions denied; he became increasingly difficult, threatening to quit almost daily, and ultimately left immediately after recording “Free Nelson Mandela” and filming its video — before the song’s release or chart success. He had to be coerced back for a single Top of the Pops performance in April 1984, then departed permanently. (marcoonthebass.blogspot.com; marcoonthebass.blogspot.com)

The Chrysalis deal and financial implosion. Two-Tone’s original arrangement with Chrysalis, brokered by manager Rick Rogers (a former associate of The Damned), traded away financial protections in exchange for total creative control — leaving Dammers personally exposed for the mounting studio costs. (findyl.co.uk) For the deal’s fuller structure and primary-source documentation, see the dedicated section below. As bills mounted, Chrysalis released the compilation This Are Two Tone (1983) in a bid to generate cash flow while the album remained unfinished — a move that signals how anxious the label had become about the project. (marcoonthebass.blogspot.com) The final cost of the album is disputed: one source puts it at over £200,000 (marcoonthebass.blogspot.com), while another states £500,000 — described as the amount budgeted for four complete albums (en.apoplife.nl); see conflict note below. John Bradbury offered sardonic perspective: “After Jerry had finished recording, that’s probably all the money the record company had left.” (findyl.co.uk)

Aftermath. The album received strong critical reviews on release but sold poorly — six of its ten tracks were previously released singles, limiting its commercial appeal. Dammers later said: “After that, I was what they call ‘imprisoned’ to the record company for four years, because we had such a big debt.” (findyl.co.uk) The debt left Two-Tone paralysed; the label effectively ceased releasing records, and The Special AKA dissolved in 1985.

Rick Rogers / Chrysalis Deal — 2 Tone Creative-Control Arrangement

What the web sources establish (as of 2026-10-14)

Manager Rick Rogers (formerly associated with The Damned) brokered the 2 Tone/Chrysalis deal, with Chrysalis A&R man Roy Eldridge as the label-side contact who championed the arrangement. The deal was struck between May 1979 (release of debut single “Gangsters” on the band’s own Covertone imprint) and September 1979 (second single on the new Two-Tone/Chrysalis imprint), almost certainly in summer 1979. No exact signing date has been found in accessible web sources.

Core terms (secondary sources, web):

  • Chrysalis would fund up to 15 singles per year on the 2 Tone subsidiary imprint and commit to releasing at least 10 of those. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_Tone_Records, citing Reynolds below; search snippet from 2-tone.info gives “12 seven inches” — a variant figure, possibly from a different source.)
  • In exchange, Dammers/Rogers gave up standard financial protections, leaving the band personally exposed for recording costs. (findyl.co.uk)
  • 2 Tone operated as an independent imprint: Dammers retained full creative control and the right to sign other acts of his choosing. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Specials, citing Daniel Rachel below; classicpopmag.com quoting Dammers: “When we signed the label to Chrysalis, we retained artistic control. I just wanted to give like-minded bands a break.“)
  • Dammers acknowledged Chrysalis’s commercial advantage in the arrangement: “They got the talent spotting and A&R done for nothing.” (2-tone.info/the-specials/)

Conflict note — singles quota: Wikipedia’s 2 Tone Records article (via Reynolds, 2005) says “15 singles a year, release at least 10”; a 2-tone.info snippet says “12 seven inches a year.” These may reflect different quotes or different editions of the deal. Neither figure has been confirmed against a primary document.

Primary-source documentation

No interview or document giving exact deal terms has been found in freely accessible web sources. The two books that are cited in Wikipedia as sources for this deal are:

  1. Simon Reynolds, Rip It Up and Start Again: Postpunk 1978–1984 (Faber & Faber, 2005; Penguin, February 2006), Chapter 14 “Ghost Dance: 2-Tone and the ska resurrection.” Wikipedia’s 2 Tone Records article cites this (footnote 3) for the 15-singles/creative-control structure. Reynolds conducted extensive interviews for the book; the Chrysalis deal terms most likely derive from a Dammers interview within it. The book is the strongest existing documented secondary source for these terms.

  2. Daniel Rachel, Too Much Too Young: The 2 Tone Records Story (White Rabbit / Orion, 2023; updated paperback 2024). Wikipedia’s The Specials article cites this book (footnote 12) for the Chrysalis sub-label arrangement. Rachel’s book is an oral history built from new interviews with participants; it is the most recent and likely most detailed account. Neither the book preview nor reviews quote deal specifics.

Horace Panter, Ska’d for Life: A Personal Journey with The Specials (2007) is a further candidate; Panter was present during the signing period. Search results did not turn up deal-specific quotes from it.

Conflict note

  • Production cost: marcoonthebass.blogspot.com states “over £200,000”; en.apoplife.nl states £500,000 (“the amount reserved for four albums”). The two figures may not be contradictory (£200,000 = Chrysalis investment in the album; £500,000 = cumulative Two-Tone/Chrysalis exposure across the period) but no primary source has been found to resolve this. A Dammers interview giving a specific figure would settle it.
