Disco Inferno

Post-rock / experimental trio. UK (Essex). Discogs artist ID: 32826. Active 1989–1996.

One of the key UK post-rock groups of the early 1990s. Distinguished by Ian Crause’s technique of routing guitar through a sampler to produce both melodic texture and percussion, creating dense, lo-fi electronic compositions without conventional drum kits. Released records on ché Records, Cheree Records, Rough Trade, and One Little Indian. A 2011 EP compilation on One Little Indian introduced them to a new generation.

Biography

Formation (1989)

Disco Inferno formed in Essex in January 1989, initially as a four-piece. Keyboardist Daniel Gish was part of the early lineup alongside Ian Crause (guitar, vocals, sampler), Paul Willmott (bass), and Robert Whatley (drums/programming). Gish departed around 1991–92 to join Bark Psychosis; the remaining trio of Crause, Willmott, and Whatley became the core lineup.

ché Records period (1991–1992)

The band’s first releases came on the small independent ché Records (a division of Ché Trading Limited). Debut 7” Entertainment (1991) was produced by Charlie McIntosh, who continued as their principal producer through the ché years. Open Doors • Closed Windows (1991) was their first LP, followed by the Science 12” EP later that year — all three co-produced by McIntosh, with Science adding co-producer Richard Charlton. The design work across this run was handled by Cactus Design.

Science tracklist: “Waking Up” (5:58) / “Glancing Away” (5:15) / “Fallen Down The Wire” (4:38) / “No Edge, No End” (5:06). “Waking Up” was later covered by Piano Magic on their album Low Birth Weight (Rocket Girl, 1999), credited “music by Ian Crause / Paul Willmott / Robert Whatley.”

In Debt (1992, ché CHÉ 4 CD) was a retrospective compilation pulling tracks from across the ché period.

Cheree Records: transition (1992)

Summer’s Last Sound (1992) came out on Cheree Records (a sister label to ché under Cheree Records parent). The Cheree session shows the trio in full form: Crause credited for sample programming, guitar, and vocals; Willmott for bass; Whatley for drum triggers and bass patterns. McIntosh returned as co-producer. This EP marks the transition point where the sample-guitar approach was fully consolidated.

Rough Trade years (1993–1995)

Disco Inferno moved to Rough Trade for what became their most productive and celebrated run.

A Rock To Cling To / From The Devil To The Deep Blue Sky (1993, Rough Trade R2983) — debut Rough Trade release. A CD EP with two tracks, produced and engineered by Charlie McIntosh. The nine-minute “From The Devil To The Deep Blue Sky” became one of their most referenced pieces.

The Last Dance (1993, Rough Trade R3030) — 12” EP. The title track and “The Long Dance” produced by Michael Johnson and engineered by John A. Rivers; “D.I. Go Pop” and “Scattered Showers” produced by Charlie McIntosh.

D. I. Go Pop (1994, Rough Trade R3072) — their debut full-length album. Eight tracks, all written by Disco Inferno. Produced by Charlie McIntosh. Featured cover design by Fuel (9) and photography by David Spero (2). “Footprints In Snow” carries a text postscript credited to Eugene Desmond Silk.

Second Language (1994, Rough Trade R3153) — four-track CD EP. Produced by John Coxon, engineered by Mads Bjerke. Photograph by David Spero (2).

It’s A Kid’s World (1994, Rough Trade R3353) — three-track CD EP. Self-produced. Illustration by Clifton Hepburn.

Technicolour (1995, Rough Trade) — second and final studio album. Recorded and mixed by John Coxon and Mads Bjerke; produced by Disco Inferno. Nine tracks.

Dissolution and aftermath (1995–1996)

The band split acrimoniously in 1995, while or just after recording Technicolour; the album was released posthumously in 1996. Multiple factors converged: years of commercial failure despite critical acclaim, financial pressure, equipment theft (which had forced them into a more conventional live/studio setup), and a terminal rift between Crause and Willmott.

The creative tension had a specific shape. As Crause pushed further toward abstract, sampling-dominated composition, Willmott’s bass role was increasingly sidelined — and Crause proposed an unworkable arrangement for continuing: he would record alone, with Willmott and Whatley adding parts independently afterwards. Willmott later described Crause as having been unable to be in the same room with him. Crause, by most accounts, was both creatively and personally exhausted by this point.

Whatley was not a target of the acrimony: Crause and Whatley formed Floorshow together immediately after the split, recording an album also called The Vertical Axis. That album was shelved by a label (unconfirmed; Crause called the sessions “catastrophic”), and its tracks were later salvaged for the solo singles Elemental (2000, Tugboat Records) and Head Over Heels (2001, Acuarela), both featuring drummer Ritchie Thomas (Dif Juz, The Jesus and Mary Chain). A full solo album under the Vertical Axis title was released on Bandcamp in December 2013. Crause spent roughly nine years in Bolivia in the intervening period.

Paul Willmott and Robert Whatley both went on to Transformer (3). Willmott also played in Lisp.

Sources: The Quietus “A New 90s Part Two: Burning Up With Disco Inferno” (c. 2011, feat. Paul Willmott and Ian Crause); Wikipedia; WUOG interview with Ian Crause (April 2014).

Daniel Gish, who had left before the Rough Trade period, contributed keyboards to Bark Psychosis’s Hex (1994) and was a founding member of drum & bass project Boymerang with Graham Sutton.

Posthumous reissue (2011)

The 5 EPs (2011, One Little Indian TPLP1082CD) compiled material from the five Rough Trade EPs, remastered. Compiled by Ned Raggett and Brian Macdonald (5). Liner notes by David Howell (2); photography by Toby Glanville. Composition credits: Crause, Willmott, Whatley.

Web Research Notes

Sources: Wikipedia, Last.fm biography, The Quietus, FACT, Drowned in Sound, Spin, WUOG interview (Ian Crause, 2014), Words and Guitars, WhoSampled, Internet Archive.

Formation and Essex context

Formed January 1989 in Essex, England. No specific town within Essex has been confirmed in any source. The four original members (Crause, Willmott, Whatley, Gish) were teenagers at formation. The WUOG 2014 interview has Crause describing the environment as “a pretty suburban, curtain twitching, gossiping environment” during a period of neoliberal social shift in England — no more precise location given.

Their manager Michael Collins (credited on Rough Trade releases) had previously managed Wire during their 1970s incarnation. This is likely one route through which Wire became a key early influence.

Guitar-through-sampler technique

Crause purchased a Roland S-750 sampler with his savings and spent approximately six months programming toward the new sound before Summer’s Last Sound (1992). He MIDI’d his guitar; the technique involved triggering real-time samples from the guitar, with individual strings or frets triggering different sampled sounds, while Whatley did the same on drum pads. Willmott’s bass grounded the texture. Crause and Whatley later acquired MIDI-controlled instruments to replicate samples live.

Rather than sampling existing recordings in the conventional sense, the band recorded environmental sounds themselves — running water, wind, whistling birds, boots, car crashes, angry voices — and built from these. Crause described hearing The Young Gods and Public Enemy and wanting to make “backing tracks like moving pictures.” The MIDI guitar produced unexpected results that altered the plan: “the limitations changed things” (WUOG interview).

Crause’s own summary (2011 interview, quoted in search results): “We kind of got ours from Public Enemy, who were too black and The Young Gods, who sang in French, for fuck’s sake!”

FACT Magazine’s 2012 review of The 5 EPs characterised the band as having pioneered “literally playing samples live with each individual guitar string or drum pad triggering a different sound” — enabling them to function as a live band while fully integrating digital production.

Influences (confirmed cited)

  • Joy Division and Wire — early post-punk sound; Wire also a managerial connection via Michael Collins
  • My Bloody Valentine — production interest cited
  • The Young Gods — sampling approach, key influence on Crause’s pivot
  • Public Enemy / The Bomb Squad — hip-hop production and sampling cited explicitly
  • Crause also cited Ovid and Dante as primary influences on his thinking by the time of his 2014 WUOG interview: “books more than records influence the way I think now”

Critical reception: at the time vs. now

At the time, the band were out of step with Britpop and achieved minimal commercial success. D.I. Go Pop did not chart on release and was largely overlooked beyond some positive press notices. Technicolour received “muted enthusiasm or indifference” from critics.

Posthumous rehabilitation: One Little Indian’s 2004 reissue of D.I. Go Pop and Technicolour triggered a significant critical reassessment. Scott Plagenhoef’s Pitchfork review of the reissue scored D.I. Go Pop 9.3/10, calling it “nearly as urgent and key” as the EPs. Andrew Unterberger (Stylus Magazine) noted the reissues led to the album being “formally embraced by, in some cases, the critics who neglected it the first time around.” Pitchfork described the remasters as “the jewels in the crown of One Little Indian’s ‘Crossing the Pond’ reissue campaign.”

The 5 EPs (2011): Received near-unanimous acclaim on release. FACT gave it 10/10; Drowned in Sound gave 9/10 (calling them “forgotten revolutionaries” and saying their neglect was “Britpop’s forgotten crime”); Spin reviewed positively. The compilation reached a new generation via internet music forums that had been building around the 2004 reissues.

D.I. Go Pop Quietus anniversary piece (2014): An extended retrospective in The Quietus examined D.I. Go Pop as a key work in post-rock history.

It’s A Kid’s World — samples identified

The title track sampled:

  • Iggy Pop, “Lust for Life” — the drum pattern, slowed down (confirmed via WhoSampled)
  • Peter Howell / BBC Radiophonic Workshop, “Doctor Who” (1980 version)
  • Katsuhisa Hattori, “Dogtanian and the Three Muskehounds Theme” (1981)
  • Tony Kinsey, “Willo the Wisp Theme” (1981)

The juxtaposition of children’s TV nostalgia with cynical lyrics about growing up was deliberate.

Floorshow / The Vertical Axis

After the split, Crause formed Floorshow with Whatley. In the 2014 WUOG interview, Crause described the sessions for the original Vertical Axis (conceived ~15 years before the 2013 Bandcamp release) as “catastrophic” and referred to “a major label” — no specific label name has been confirmed in any public source. This remains an open question.

Tracks from Floorshow sessions were salvaged and used on two early solo singles: “Elemental” (Tugboat Records, late 2000) and “Head Over Heels” (Acuarela, 2001), both featuring drummer Ritchie Thomas (Dif Juz, The Jesus and Mary Chain).

Crause then moved to Bolivia and spent approximately nine years away from music. He returned in mid-2012 with “More Earthly Concerns” (released via blogs), then issued The Song of Phaethon EP (Bandcamp, November 2012) — described as a “long-form” piece inspired by Greek mythology and British involvement in the Second Gulf War. Several further solo tracks appeared on Bandcamp in early 2013. His full solo album using the Vertical Axis title was released on Bandcamp in December 2013.

Note: “Elemental” was a single, not an album — the vault’s biography section is correct. There was no solo full-length album in 2000; the Bandcamp Vertical Axis (2013) was Crause’s first long-player.

BBC sessions

Two documented sessions:

  1. “Mixing It” session — recorded 19 December 1994 at Maida Vale Studios, BBC Radio 3 programme Mixing It. Transmission date: 19/12/94. Tracks (all animal-named instrumentals): “Shark,” “Elephant,” “Tortoise,” “Shrew,” “Bird,” “Rats.” Produced by Philip Tagney. Commercially released 13 September 1999 on Tugboat Records. Total running time: 16:22.

  2. BBC session, 27 March 1995 — a second BBC session documented in the Internet Archive (uploaded 2024). Tracks: “It’s A Kid’s World,” “Technicolour,” “When The Story Breaks,” “Don’t You Know.” The specific BBC programme for this session has not been confirmed in any available source.

91/92 reissue (Rocket Girl, 2017)

The 91/92 title is the reissue name for what was originally In Debt (1992, ché Records). Released by Rocket Girl Records, 5 May 2017, as:

  • Limited double LP + single CD (limited pressing of 500 copies)
  • CD in gatefold cardboard sleeve with 8-page booklet

Added one previously unreleased track: “In The Cold”. Sleeve notes by Neil Kulkarni. The booklet includes interview excerpts. New artwork. (The Discogs entry notes Paul Willmott contributed to liner notes as interview; Kulkarni wrote the essay.)
