Shellac

Chicago post-punk / noise rock trio. Active 1992–2024. Discogs ID: 103197.

Steve Albini’s principal band following Big Black and Rapeman. All records on Touch And Go. Known for deliberately uncommercial practices — no management, no music videos, flat-fee touring. Steve Albini died May 7, 2024; To All Trains (2024) was released on May 17, 2024 — ten days after his death, making it a posthumous release. The album was recorded across four sessions (November 2017, October 2019, September 2021, March 2022) at Electrical Audio, Chicago, and mastered in 2023; it was fully completed during Albini’s lifetime. Albini gave at least one interview about the album (Kreative Kontrol podcast, April 23, 2024) knowing it was forthcoming.

Positions on Streaming and Sync Licensing

Streaming

Albini’s position on streaming evolved significantly over time and was never simple.

2014 — strong endorsement of internet distribution. In an April 2014 Quartz interview, Albini declared that internet-based music sharing was “the single best thing that has happened in my lifetime in music, after punk rock.” He argued that streaming services (naming Spotify, Beats Music, and Bandcamp) had essentially solved the problems he outlined in his 1993 essay “The Problem With Music”: record labels were “essentially irrelevant,” bands could reach audiences “with no corporate participation,” and the old publishing industry had collapsed (“the one I am most pleased to see collapse is the publishing racket”). On per-stream compensation rates he was dismissive of critics: “it’s like complaining that cars are going faster than horses.” He also expanded this into the November 2014 keynote at the Face the Music conference in Melbourne, arguing “the internet has facilitated the most direct and efficient, compact relationship ever between band and audience.” Louderthanwar summary / Stereogum / Rocknerd transcript summary

2015 — copyright as “expired concept.” Albini characterised copyright itself as an “expired concept,” stating: “That old copyright model of the person who wrote something down owns it and anyone else who wants to use it or see it has to pay him, I think that model has expired.” He also rejected the term “piracy” for downloading. This prompted guitarist Marc Ribot to publish an open letter calling Albini a “lousy hypocrite” and challenging him to release his catalogue under Creative Commons if he genuinely held this position; the exchange played out publicly via Stereogum and Vice in mid-2015. Stereogum — Ribot letter / Salon

2020 — cooling on Spotify specifically. In a March 2020 LEO Weekly interview, Albini said he didn’t have Spotify on his phone and didn’t know much about it, adding: “I think it’s probably doomed.” He expressed concern about the practicalities of policing every corporate association but acknowledged his personal distaste for certain platforms. LEO Weekly

2022 — removed Shellac and Big Black from Spotify. Albini pulled both bands’ catalogues from Spotify, stating: “Spotify is a terrible company, and I don’t want to be part of their business.” His objections were dual: the platform’s meagre per-stream payouts to artists, and its retention of Joe Rogan’s podcast amid COVID-misinformation controversy. (He commented on Rogan separately: “If a goddamn fucking Juggalo can manage it, a nine-figure podcaster can step the fuck up without whining.“) He was careful to add: “I don’t fault the bands who have their music on Spotify by choice.” NME — Joe Rogan / Consequence — return

2024 — return to Spotify, approved by Albini before his death. Around the release of To All Trains, both Shellac and Big Black catalogues returned to Spotify and other streaming services. According to reporting citing a Wire interview with the band, Albini was fully aware of and had approved the return prior to his death on May 7, 2024. The decision was therefore not made posthumously by the estate. Consequence / Brooklyn Vegan

Sync Licensing (music in TV, film, advertising)

No documented Shellac-specific policy on sync licensing has been found through web sources. Albini did not appear to give interviews where he explicitly stated a blanket refusal of sync deals for Shellac’s music. What is documented:

  • In a 2018 interview (source not fully pinned), Albini described sync licensing and music-for-advertising as legitimate income streams for bands in the modern industry: “Most bands make their living performing live now, or occasionally lining their music up with other projects like doing film stuff, or sync stuff, or licensing their music for advertising.” This is a descriptive/neutral observation, not an endorsement of Shellac doing it.
  • Shellac’s stated anti-promotion philosophy (“a visceral reaction to advertising and promotion”; “I didn’t want to trick anybody into buying our records”) would be philosophically at odds with commercial sync deals, but no interview has been found where Albini explicitly extended this to third-party licensing of Shellac’s catalogue.
  • No instances of Shellac music appearing in films, TV shows, or advertisements have surfaced in searches. This is consistent with the band’s anti-commercial posture but cannot be confirmed as an enforced policy from available sources.

Open question: The Wire’s oral history of Shellac (published as a memorial following Albini’s death, Wire issue 484, interview conducted April 2024 by Emily Pothast) may contain statements on licensing. Full text behind paywall; not confirmed. The Wire
