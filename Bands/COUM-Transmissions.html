COUM Transmissions

Discogs ID: 270562 UK performance art collective active 1969–1976, Hull and London. Precursor to Throbbing Gristle. Core members: Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti; Peter Christopherson also participated.

Overview

COUM Transmissions was a performance art and mail art collective founded by Genesis P-Orridge in Hull. The group’s 1976 show “Prostitution” at the ICA, London — featuring Cosey Fanni Tutti’s pornographic magazine work and used tampons in display cases — caused a parliamentary debate and press scandal, with Conservative MP Nicholas Fairbairn calling them “wreckers of civilisation.” COUM dissolved into Throbbing Gristle, with the industrial band framed as a continuation of the collective’s confrontational aesthetic by other means.

”Prostitution” Exhibition (ICA London, 1976)

Venue: Institute of Contemporary Arts (ICA), The Mall, London Dates: Opening night 18 October 1976; exhibition ran 19–26 October 1976 (artdesigncafe.com)

Content

The show consisted of:

  • Explicit photographs of lesbians and erotic nude photographs of Cosey Fanni Tutti drawn from her commercial pornographic modelling work (some photographs were subsequently removed under pressure and placed in a restricted box)
  • Assemblages incorporating rusty knives, syringes, bloodied hair, used sanitary towels, and blood bottles
  • Vaseline jars and other bodily-residue props
  • Photographic documentation of earlier COUM performances in Milan and Paris
  • Press clippings displayed and updated daily as new coverage arrived, so the media reaction became part of the exhibition itself
  • Transvestite guards and costumed performers mingling with gallery visitors
  • A stripper who replaced the conventional director’s opening speech
  • Beer replaced the usual wine at the opening

Sources: artdesigncafe.com; archive.ica.art — Prostitution Revisited; Wikipedia — COUM Transmissions (as of 2026-07-04)

Opening Night and Throbbing Gristle Launch

The opening night (18 October 1976) doubled as the official public debut of Throbbing Gristle. A punk band — Chelsea, performing under the name “LSD” — also played. The event marked the symbolic handover from COUM’s performance art practice to industrial music. (archive.ica.art)

Political and Press Reaction

Scottish Conservative MP Sir Nicholas Fairbairn attended the opening night and raised the matter in the House of Commons, calling Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti “wreckers of civilisation” and demanding an explanation from Arts Minister Harold Lever. He also reportedly described the show as “Now we are getting the lid off the maggot factory.” (artdesigncafe.com; Wikipedia)

The tabloid press was broadly hostile. The Daily Express ran: “State aid for Cosey’s travelling sex troupe.” Other headlines included “Cosey’s Sex Romp Pictures are Banned” and “Peddling Porn at the Taxpayer’s Expense.” Critics in the popular press called the work “sick, sadistic, obscene, filthy rubbish, pornography.” A counter-note: Marina Vaizey’s Sunday Times review was titled “Much Ado about Nothing at the ICA.” (artdesigncafe.com)

Scotland Yard’s Obscene Publications Squad investigated but neither COUM nor the ICA faced charges. The Arts Council subsequently came under pressure; the ICA’s Arts Council grant was reported to have been withdrawn the following year. (Wikipedia — COUM Transmissions)

Despite the controversy, ICA membership increased sharply in the wake of the show. (archive.ica.art)

Arts Council Funding — Web Research Notes

Multiple sources confirm the Arts Council of Great Britain came under direct pressure as an immediate consequence of the Prostitution exhibition, but the precise mechanism and target of any funding withdrawal is harder to establish from available sources:

Pressure on the ICA (confirmed): Arts Council chairman Lord Patrick Gibson pressured ICA director Ted Little to close the show; Little refused on the grounds that the Arts Council should not censor its clients’ exhibitions. (artdesigncafe.com) The Quietus records that “the aftermath of ‘Prostitution’ saw questions asked in parliament, the Arts Council reviewed its funding of the ICA whose chairman resigned.” (thequietus.com) Whether the ICA grant was actually cut or only reviewed and threatened is unconfirmed by this source.

Cosey Fanni Tutti’s memoir account: A summary of Tutti’s 2017 memoir Art Sex Music describes the Prostitution show as having “almost lost the ICA its funding” — suggesting the withdrawal was threatened but not necessarily completed. (rollingstone.com)

COUM’s own grant: COUM had received approximately £2,000 in state funding during 1975–76 (including small British Council grants for overseas shows and a modest Arts Council contribution of roughly £200 towards Prostitution itself). The documentary Other, Like Me (2020) describes the aftermath as including “the permanent withdrawal of UK Arts Council funding” from COUM. (upstatefilms.org synopsis) This aligns with the general understanding that COUM received no further public arts funding after 1976, but the documentary synopsis is a secondary/publicity description rather than a primary source.

Direct causation vs. policy change: No source found in this research explicitly frames the withdrawal as a formal policy change independent of the Prostitution controversy. The consensus across reputable sources (Quietus, memoir summary, documentary synopsis) is that the Arts Council’s response — whether threat or actual withdrawal — was a direct institutional reaction to the scandal triggered by Prostitution specifically, not a separate subsequent policy shift. The pressure was applied during or immediately after the October 1976 run, with Lord Gibson acting as the conduit between parliamentary/press outrage and the Arts Council’s institutional response.

Source quality caveat: No primary Arts Council documents, board minutes, or dated grant-decision letters have been located in open web sources. The claim that COUM’s specific grant was “withdrawn” (as opposed to simply not renewed) rests on secondary accounts in the documentary and in AI-aggregated pages. The stronger documentary evidence is for the threat to ICA funding and the Arts Council chairman’s direct intervention. A definitive answer likely requires the Arts Council archive at the Victoria and Albert Museum, or direct consultation of the ICA’s administrative records from 1976–77.

Conflict note: Fairbairn’s exact phrase varies by source — Wikipedia uses “wreckers of civilisation” (British spelling) as the parliamentary formulation; artdesigncafe.com uses “wreckers of Western civilisation.” The core phrase is consistent across all sources.

Lasting Significance

“Prostitution” is consistently cited as one of the most controversial exhibitions in ICA history and in British contemporary art broadly. It crystallised debates about public arts funding, bodily representation, and obscenity that ran through late-1970s British culture. As the launch platform for Throbbing Gristle, it is also the origin point of what became known as industrial music — COUM’s confrontational methodology carried forward into a sonic and commercial form. (Wikipedia; archive.ica.art)
