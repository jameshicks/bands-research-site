From Discogs

The self-proclaimed “world’s worst orchestra” was founded by Gavin Bryars in 1970 when he was lecturing at the Portsmouth School of Art.

Bryars wanted to engage the masses with classical music, and sought a way to liberate the form from the pomposity of its audience. His idea was to form an orchestra of the people. Anyone could join, regardless of skill.

The orchestra comprised known musicians playing instruments they had no knowledge of, virtuoso players and people who had never played an instrument in their lives. Their oeuvré would encompass the popular classics and rock songs - music that could be played by ear. No sight-reading skills were therefore required. The virtuoso players kept everything vaguely within the realms of what might be called a tune, with the other players reaching for (and missing) notes nearby. The result was a fascinating atonal mess of a noise, one which was considered profound by several of Bryars classical contemporaries.

The hilarious results of this experiment led to the Portsmouth Sinfonia’s records being pitched at the comedy market which earned them a cult following, enough for them to be selling out the Royal Albert Hall by 1974.

Although it ceased performing in 1982, the Portsmouth Sinfonia never formally disbanded. At the time of writing (March 2006) there are whispers that more concerts might just be forthcoming…

— Discogs artist profile, ID 442169

Overview

Founded in 1970 by composer Gavin Bryars while lecturing at the Portsmouth School of Art, the Portsmouth Sinfonia was a conceptual art project disguised as an orchestra: anyone could join, regardless of ability or instrument knowledge. Virtuoso players shared the stage with complete novices, all tackling well-known classical and rock pieces by ear, producing what admirers and audiences experienced as a cheerfully atonal spectacle. The stunt earned genuine cultural traction — they sold out the Royal Albert Hall in 1974 and attracted collaborators including Brian Eno, Andy Mackay, Phil Collins, and Phil Manzanera. The bulk of their catalogue appeared on Transatlantic Records across the 1970s; they stopped performing in 1982 without formally disbanding.

Eno, Mackay, and Manzanera — mechanism of involvement

Brian Eno. Eno studied at Ipswich Art School and Winchester School of Art (1966–1969), then moved to London in 1969. Wikipedia’s Brian Eno article states he “became involved with the Scratch Orchestra and the Portsmouth Sinfonia” after that move, but gives no mechanism. No web source found (interviews, biographies, journalism) explains how Eno came to join — whether Gavin Bryars invited him directly, whether it was through the overlapping Scratch Orchestra network, or whether Eno approached the Sinfonia himself. The connection between Eno and Bryars is well-attested afterwards (Bryars was the first artist signed to Eno’s Obscure Records label in 1975, and Bryars co-arranged the Pachelbel variations on Eno’s Discreet Music), but no source traces the initial introduction. [https://handwiki.org/wiki/Biography:Brian_Eno; https://wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Eno, as of 2026-08-23]

Andy Mackay. Mackay studied music and English literature at the University of Reading. While there, he formed a performance-art group called Sunshine (with future Roxy publicist Simon Puxley) and, crucially, “struck up a friendship with Winchester art student Brian Eno” — Mackay’s group performed at an art college in Winchester that Eno attended, and they followed this with collaborative performances. [Wikipedia: Andy Mackay, as of 2026-08-23; https://consequence.net/2008/09/icons-of-rock-andy-mackay/] This friendship predates the Sinfonia (Eno was at Winchester 1966–1969; the Sinfonia was founded May 1970). Mackay later brought Eno into Roxy Music in January 1971. However, no source found explains whether Mackay’s Sinfonia credits represent his playing in the orchestra or co-appearing with the Sinfonia on Eno’s own albums. The personnel lists for the two main Sinfonia-branded releases (Plays the Popular Classics, 1974; Hallelujah — At the Royal Albert Hall, 1974) do not include Mackay by name. His likely route to the Sinfonia’s shared-credits, if confirmed, is as a session guest on the Eno albums (e.g. Taking Tiger Mountain (By Strategy), 1974, where he plays brass on track 3 alongside Portsmouth Sinfonia strings on track 7). [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taking_Tiger_Mountain_(By_Strategy)]

Phil Manzanera. Manzanera attended Dulwich College (boarding school) and did not go to university. He joined Roxy Music in 1972 — the same band as Eno and Mackay — and became one of Eno’s closest collaborators in that period and after. No source found places Manzanera in the Portsmouth Sinfonia ensemble itself. Like Mackay, his Discogs “shared releases” with the Sinfonia most likely reflect their co-appearance on Eno session albums (e.g. Taking Tiger Mountain, where Manzanera plays guitar throughout, and the Portsmouth Sinfonia contributes strings to one track). No art-school connection to Gavin Bryars or the Portsmouth School of Art was found for Manzanera.

Summary of findings. The Eno-Mackay friendship is well-established and predates the Sinfonia by at least one year, mediated by the Winchester/Reading art school avant-garde scene rather than Portsmouth. No source confirms or denies whether Bryars issued specific invitations to Eno or to any Roxy Music member. The Sinfonia’s open-door policy (anyone could join) may mean no “invitation” mechanism existed — Eno simply joined. Mackay and Manzanera’s credits appear to be co-session credits on Eno albums, not independent membership of the orchestra. No journalism, liner note transcription, or interview found (as of August 2026) addresses the specific invitation question for any of the three.

Source quality note. The Consequence.net article (2008) and Wikipedia (Andy Mackay) are the only named sources for the Mackay-Eno Winchester meeting. All other sources are secondary aggregations that repeat the same Wikipedia language about Eno “joining” the Sinfonia without mechanism.
