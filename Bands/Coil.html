Coil

Coil

Oct 19, 202612 min read

Coil

Discogs ID: 660
Active: 1982–2004 (dissolved after Balance’s death; posthumous releases continue)
Origin: London, England
Genres: Post-industrial, experimental electronic, dark ambient, drone, neofolk, noise, acid house, avant-garde

English experimental music group founded in 1982–1983 in London by Geoff Rushton (John Balance) as a solo side project to Psychic TV, then cemented as a full working partnership with Peter Christopherson (formerly of Throbbing Gristle and Psychic TV) from 1984 onward. Their work explored the occult, sexuality, alchemy, and drugs, influencing gothic rock, neofolk, and dark ambient. AllMusic called them “one of the most beloved, mythologized groups to emerge from the British post-industrial scene.”

John Balance (born Geoffrey Laurence Burton, 16 February 1962, Mansfield) died 13 November 2004 after a fall at his home in Weston-super-Mare. Peter Christopherson (born 27 February 1955, Leeds) died in his sleep in Bangkok, Thailand, 25 November 2010. Both deaths ended their respective work; Christopherson formally announced Coil would not continue after Balance’s death.

History

Formation (1982–1983)

Balance had been a teenage zine journalist under the name Stabmental, covering Throbbing Gristle and Cabaret Voltaire. He first met Christopherson at a Throbbing Gristle gig (recorded as Heathen Earth) in February 1980, and the two became partners. Following TG’s 1981 dissolution, Christopherson joined Genesis P-Orridge in forming Psychic TV (and Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth). Balance, who had also briefly participated in Brian Williams’ Lustmord project, joined Psychic TV and contributed to Force the Hand of Chance (single “Just Drifting”) and Dreams Less Sweet (1983).

Balance began using the Coil name in 1982 for solo work. The first Coil gig (as a duo with Christopherson) took place on 4 August 1983 in London at the Magenta Club, during a film screening by Cerith Wyn Evans and Derek Jarman. Before Christopherson could commit fully, Balance collaborated with John Gosling of Zos Kia for several 1983 performances. The early collaborative recordings with Zos Kia became the split album Transparent, released February 1984 by Nekrophile Records (Austria). Balance and Christopherson departed Psychic TV in January 1984 to make Coil a full-time concern.

How to Destroy Angels and Some Bizzare years (1984–1986)

Coil’s official recording debut, the EP How to Destroy Angels, was released 20 April 1984 (Good Friday) on the Belgian label L.A.Y.L.A.H. Antirecords (LAY05). Recorded at Britannia Row Studios on 19 February 1984, it used predominantly iron and steel instruments and was dedicated to Mars as god of spring and war. (Wikipedia)

Scatology (1984/1985, Force & Form / K.422, a Some Bizzare sublabel) was co-produced with JG Thirlwell (credited as Clint Ruin). Stephen Thrower, Alex Fergusson (Alternative TV), and Gavin Friday contributed. The album’s themes focused on alchemy as transformation of matter. Its profits-donating single “Panic / Tainted Love” (a cover) was among the first AIDS benefit music releases; the video, directed by Christopherson, was acquired by the Museum of Modern Art, New York. (Wikipedia)

Horse Rotorvator (1986, Force & Form / K.422) featured Stephen Thrower and was recorded at multiple studios with Danny Hyde, Rico Conning, Paul Samuelson, and Tony Harris. A cover of Leonard Cohen’s “Famous Blue Raincoat” was included (the only track not written by Coil). The album was influenced by AIDS-related deaths of friends; “Ostia (The Death of Pasolini)” addressed the death of filmmaker Pier Paolo Pasolini. After this release, Coil left Some Bizzare following a falling-out with its owner Stevo Pearce.

Threshold House years (1987–1992)

Gold Is the Metal (With the Broadest Shoulders) (1987) launched Threshold House, Coil’s own label — described in its liner notes as a “stopgap and breathing space” between Horse Rotorvator and Love’s Secret Domain. Stephen Thrower was a full-time member throughout this period (1987–1992).

Coil wrote the score for the first Hellraiser film but withdrew when they suspected their music would not be used; the soundtrack eventually went to Howard Shore. The Unreleased Themes for Hellraiser tape (1987) appeared on Solar Lodge Records. They claimed that inspiration for Pinhead was partly drawn from piercing magazines borrowed from the group. (Wikipedia)

Love’s Secret Domain (1991, Torso) was produced by Coil and Danny Hyde, with additional flamenco guitar by Juan Ramirez. Described with the abbreviation LSD, it blended acid house elements with post-industrial structures and became a template for later waves of electronic music. “Windowpane” and a Jack Dangers remix of “The Snow” were released as singles. Stolen and Contaminated Songs (1992, Threshold House) collected outtakes and demos from the LSD era.

Also in 1992, Threshold House released How to Destroy Angels (Remixes and Re-Recordings), featuring a Steven Stapleton / Nurse With Wound remix.

Soundtracks and side projects (1993–1998)

Coil contributed music to Derek Jarman’s film Blue (1993) and later released Themes for Derek Jarman’s Blue (1993, Threshold House). Their 1985 score for Jarman’s The Angelic Conversation was released on CD in 1994. In 1995, Coil and Lee Ranaldo created the original soundtrack for Todd Verow’s Frisk, and also recorded soundtracks for Gay Man’s Guide to Safer Sex and Sara Dale’s Sensual Massage.

This era also saw a wave of side-project aliases. The Eskaton handled a Coil farewell to acid house (Nasa-Arab / First Dark Ride, 1994). ELpH (Discogs ID: 58037) — the project’s name derived from “elf” — released drone and experimental work including Worship the Glitch (1995, as “ELpH vs. Coil”, Eskaton). Black Light District (Discogs ID: 138237) released A Thousand Lights in a Darkened Room (1996, Eskaton). Time Machines (Discogs ID: 236349) released their debut on Eskaton in 1998, focusing on drone and trance-induction. These projects also launched the Eskaton label imprint.

Drew McDowall began collaborating with Coil around 1990 and was officially inducted in 1995; he departed between 1999 and 2000.

The Transparent Zos Kia / Coil split was reissued in CD format in 1997 on Threshold House, in partnership with World Serpent.

Late Coil (1998–2004)

After compilations Unnatural History II, Windowpane & The Snow, and Unnatural History III, Coil began releasing a series of four singles timed to the equinoxes and solstices of 1998, collected later as Moon’s Milk (In Four Phases) (2002, Eskaton):

  • Spring Equinox: Moon’s Milk or Under an Unquiet Skull — featuring new member William Breeze on electric viola
  • Summer Solstice: Bee Stings — also features Breeze; includes “A Warning from the Sun (For Fritz)”
  • Autumn Equinox: Amethyst Deceivers — includes “Rosa Decidua” with vocals by Rose McDowall
  • Winter Solstice: North — McDowall vocals; partially credited to side project Rosa Mundi

Astral Disaster (1999, Prescription) was created with new member Thighpaulsandra, initially limited to 99 copies, later re-released in substantially different form. Musick To Play In The Dark Vol. 1 (1999, Chalice) and Vol. 2 (2000, Chalice) followed; credited performers were Balance, Christopherson, McDowall, and Thighpaulsandra on Vol. 1.

Coil performed their first concert in 16 years on 14 December 1999 at Volksbühne, Berlin (18 minutes, as “elph.zwölf”). They performed twice at the Royal Festival Hall in 2000 — the first in April as part of a Julian Cope-curated weekend (first appearance in “fluffy suits,” inspired by Sun Ra); the second in September sharing a bill with Jim Thirlwell as Foetus (JG Thirlwell).

Queens of the Circulating Library (2000, Eskaton) — a single-track full-length drone album — is the only Coil release made without Christopherson’s assistance; production credited entirely to Thighpaulsandra. Constant Shallowness Leads to Evil (2000, Eskaton) was a noise-driven album reminiscent of Christopherson’s Throbbing Gristle work.

Late-period live members included Ossian Brown (from around 2000), Tom Edwards of Spiritualized (marimba), Michael J. York (bagpipes, duduk), and Cliff Stapleton (hurdy-gurdy). Stage performers Massimo & Pierce (Black Sun Productions) read the poetic introduction to “Ostia” during live shows but did not contribute musically.

The Remote Viewer (2002, Threshold House) and Black Antlers (2004, Threshold House) were followed by Coil’s final studio album, The Ape of Naples (2005, Threshold House), released posthumously on 2 December 2005. Recorded material included contributions from Thighpaulsandra (keyboards), Tom Edwards (marimba), Ossian Brown (synth), Michael J. York (pipes, duduk), and Cliff Stapleton (hurdy-gurdy), produced by Christopherson.

Coil’s final live performance was on 23 October 2004 at DEAF (Dublin Electronic Arts Festival), Dublin City Hall.

The Ape of Naples / The New Backwards (2007, Important Records) and a standalone Thai pressing of The New Backwards (2008, Threshold House) continued posthumous releases. A 16-DVD boxset Colour Sound Oblivion was released 2010. Christopherson continued releasing archival and remastered Coil material from Bangkok until his death.

Artistry and Approach

Balance explicitly described Coil’s music as “magickal music” — ritual practice rather than conventional songcraft. He divided the Coil catalog into a “solar” first half and “moon musick” second half. The group incorporated rare instruments including the Moog synthesizer, ANS synthesizer, Mellotron, Optigan, marimba, hurdy-gurdy, and bagpipes. Compositional techniques included the cut-up technique, ritual drug use, sleep deprivation, lucid dreaming, tidal shifts, John Dee-like scrying, technical glitches, chaos theory, and SETI synchronization. (Wikipedia)

Coil often packaged releases as limited editions with sigil-like autographs, stains of blood, or other occult-inflected elements, framing records as artifacts rather than products. Balance described himself as a “Born Again Pagan.” Christopherson rejected formal religious dogma: “We don’t follow any particular religious dogma […] The thing we follow is our own noses.” (Wikipedia)

Stated non-musical influences included William Burroughs (personal friends), Aleister Crowley, Brion Gysin, and Austin Osman Spare (they owned original Spare artwork). Musical interests ranged from Harry Partch, La Monte Young, Karlheinz Stockhausen, and Arvo Pärt to krautrock groups (Cluster, Can, Kraftwerk, Tangerine Dream), Captain Beefheart, Leonard Cohen, The Velvet Underground, and Butthole Surfers. (Wikipedia)

Connections

  • Throbbing Gristle — Christopherson was a founding member; the industrial lineage is direct
  • Psychic TV — both Balance and Christopherson were members before Coil; Genesis P-Orridge connection
  • Zos Kia — earliest Coil recordings as a split with John Gosling’s project
  • Current 93 — close scene allies; “England’s Hidden Reverse” cluster (documented in David Keenan’s 2003 book of that name)
  • Nurse With Wound — Steven Stapleton remixed Coil; shared avant-garde underground
  • Death in June — Douglas Pearce in same esoteric underground; Balance contributed to DIJ releases
  • Lustmord — Balance was briefly a member of Brian Williams’ project before Coil
  • Sickness of Snakes — pre-Coil project by Balance, Christopherson, and Boyd Rice
  • Black Light District — Coil alias; same trio as ELpH / Time Machines / The Eskaton
  • Time Machines — Coil drone alias
  • Electric Sewer Age — Christopherson/Danny Hyde late recordings, Bangkok (2013 posthumous release)
  • Brion Gysin — personal friendship; cut-up technique influence
  • Soleilmoon Recordings — distributed several Coil titles (including The Consequences of Raising Hell / Hellraiser cassette, SOL 4, 1988)
