Overview

An English indie rock band from Liverpool, formed in 1984 around the songwriting core of Lee Mavers (vocals, guitar) and John Power (bass). Their sound mixed indie rock with acoustic textures and skiffle influences, and their catalogue — concentrated on Go! Discs and London Records — runs from 1987 into the 2000s, with the bulk of activity in the 1990s. Steve Lillywhite and Mark Wallis were their primary production collaborators, accounting for the lion’s share of credited work across the band’s recorded output.

The La’s (1990): Why Mavers Disowned the Album

The disowning was a combination of both factors — Mavers’ specific technical objections to Lillywhite’s production and Go! Discs’ decision to force release over the band’s active objection — but the roots of the dispute run deeper than either alone.

Recording sessions timeline

The album consumed roughly three years and an estimated £1 million across at least six or seven producers and numerous abandoned sessions before Go! Discs released it in October 1990. The known sequence:

  • 1987 — early sessions produced the “Way Out” single (produced by Gavin MacKillop) and initial recording attempts.
  • 1987–1989 — sessions with John Porter (The Smiths), John Leckie (Chipping Norton Studios), Bob Andrews, and Mike Hedges. The Hedges sessions were the most productive: Hedges alone claims to have recorded 35 songs, well over an album’s worth of material. The master tapes from the Hedges sessions later went missing. Both Leckie and Hedges gave interviews that were complimentary about the band’s songs, but Mavers rejected the results regardless.
  • December 1989 – February 1990 — final sessions at Eden Studios, London, with Steve Lillywhite (then known for U2, Simple Minds, The Pogues). This was Go! Discs’ final attempt to see the project over the line.
  • October 1990 — Go! Discs released the Lillywhite-produced version without Mavers’ approval.

Sources: Wikipedia — The La’s (album); Classic Pop Magazine; guitar.com

Mavers’ specific objections to the released version

Mavers’ stated criticisms were both procedural and technical:

  1. Guide vocals: His most specific technical complaint was that Lillywhite used his guide (scratch) vocal takes on the released version rather than finished performances — i.e., placeholder vocals recorded for reference during tracking ended up on the album. (Classic Pop Magazine; guitar.com)

  2. The album was assembled without him: Mavers claimed the label “got it together from a load of backing tapes and mixed it up themselves and put it out” without his involvement or approval. He and the band walked out of the sessions before completion; Lillywhite finished the mixing without them. (Classic Pop Magazine)

  3. The sound did not match his vision: The band felt Lillywhite’s commercial production style — his CV included major-label pop-rock records — did not suit the rootsy, unadorned sound they wanted: acoustic guitars, bass, drums, a directness drawing on The Byrds, Arthur Lee’s Love, and British Invasion influences. Mavers described the released album as “like a snake with a broken back… the worst, a pile of shit” and called it “non-musical” and “non-rhythmical.” (guitar.com; Paste Magazine)

  4. The album was unfinished in his view: In Q magazine Mavers said: “We walked out on it while we were doing it. We hated it because we weren’t getting our sound across, so we turned our back on it.” (guitar.com)

Lillywhite’s account

Lillywhite’s interviews (MusicRadar) give the clearest picture of what made the sessions unworkable. He said: “I knew the songs were absolute diamonds, but getting them on tape wasn’t so easy. We’d record six songs that were fantastic, but if there was one thing wrong on the seventh, [Lee] would be convinced that everything else was terrible and we’d have to start everything all over again.” He also described Mavers’ internal blueprint for the record as “like an acid trip that kept coming back to him, and it would mess him up.” Despite this, Lillywhite praised Mavers highly: “I would put Lee right up there with any of the singer-songwriters I’ve ever worked with.” (uDiscoverMusic; Paste Magazine)

Label pressure: Go! Discs’ role

By late 1989 Go! Discs had exhausted its patience and its budget — the sessions had run to approximately £1 million with no completed album. The label hired Lillywhite as a final, non-negotiable attempt to finish the record. When Mavers and the band abandoned the Eden Studios sessions before completion, Go! Discs allowed Lillywhite to finish mixing without them and released the result in October 1990. The band was not only excluded from the final mix but reportedly unhappy with the artwork too. Rather than promote the record, band members spent interviews actively disparaging it and discouraging fans from buying it. (Classic Pop Magazine; Stereogum; Paste Magazine)

The Mike Hedges version

A Hedges-produced version exists and was included in the 2008 deluxe reissue alongside the Lillywhite version. Mavers is reported to have preferred this version, though he has been critical of both. Hedges, reflecting on Mavers’ perfectionism, said: “His standards were so high that you’re never going to reach them. At some point you have to say, ‘That’s it, I’m finished!’ and move on.” The fate of the original Hedges master tapes — reportedly covering 35 songs — is unclear; sources state they went missing. (Classic Pop Magazine; uDiscoverMusic)

Assessment

The dispute was a combination of both factors, but not equally weighted. Label pressure was the proximate cause of the release: Go! Discs forced the album out after years of cost overruns and Mavers’ serial rejection of completed sessions. But the specific objections Mavers articulated — guide vocals left in, mixing done without the band, a sound that didn’t match his internal vision — point to a perfectionism that had already defeated multiple other producers before Lillywhite arrived. Lillywhite’s pop production credentials gave Mavers a convenient frame for the objection, but the same pattern had played out with Leckie, Hedges, Porter, and Andrews before him. The label pressure and Mavers’ perfectionism were mutually reinforcing: the more sessions failed, the more pressure Go! Discs applied; the more pressure applied, the more Mavers felt the result was being taken out of his hands.

Post-Debut: The Elusive Second Album

No second La’s studio album was ever recorded, released, or officially announced as completed. What the historical record shows is a series of genuinely attempted sessions that each collapsed — primarily due to the same perfectionism that doomed the debut sessions — combined with contract expiry, label instability, and Mavers’ extended withdrawal from the music industry.

1990–1998: Contract limbo and the Rat Scabies sessions

After the October 1990 release, the band effectively ceased functioning as a recording unit. Go! Discs collapsed; its catalogue was absorbed by PolyGram, which inherited Mavers’ recording contract. That contract expired in 1998 without a second album being made. (Wikipedia — The La’s, as of 2026-10-18)

Around 1996, Mavers did make a concrete attempt at recording. He began sessions at The Arch studio in Kew, London — a studio owned by Rat Scabies, ex-drummer of The Damned. He was joined by ex-The Stairs frontman Edgar Jones, Lee Garnett, and Neil Mavers. Those sessions ended when damp rendered the studio unusable. Mavers and others continued to rehearse and record informally in Liverpool for approximately a year afterwards, but nothing emerged officially. (Wikipedia — Lee Mavers, as of 2026-10-18; Wikipedia — The La’s, as of 2026-10-18)

In 1998, Mavers also worked with an unsigned Liverpool band called The Crescent, producing a rehearsal tape that circulated as a bootleg. This was not a La’s project. (Wikipedia — Lee Mavers, as of 2026-10-18)

2005–2006: Reunion and new songs live

The band reformed in June 2005 with a partially new lineup (including Jay Lewis on guitar and Nick Miniski on drums) and played UK, Irish, and festival dates. During these performances the setlist included several new, unreleased compositions — titles that circulated among fans as candidates for a second album include “I Am the Key”, “Fishing Net”, “Raindance”, and “The Human Race”. In August 2006, John Power stated publicly that Mavers was “tinkering with something that’s majestic,” but cautioned it “can’t be rushed.” The only official release from this entire period was BBC In Session (2006), a compilation of BBC radio recordings from the band’s original run. No new studio material was released. (Wikipedia — Lee Mavers, as of 2026-10-18)

2009: The Babyshambles connection

In 2009 there was a reported plan to record a second album using members of Babyshambles as the backing band. Drew McConnell (Babyshambles bassist) confirmed he had been working with Mavers and stated publicly: “At the end of that tour we’re going to go in and record the second record, which is all written already.” (NME, 28 May 2009) He and Mavers had been gigging together for several months and were auditioning for a guitarist and drummer. However, this lineup did not materialise: live appearances were cancelled and no recording sessions took place. (Wikipedia — The La’s, as of 2026-10-18)

2011 and after: Stripped-back performances

In June 2011, Mavers and guitarist Gary Murphy performed a series of acoustic shows across the UK and Europe (including Rock en Seine in France). These were stripped-back, two-person sets rather than full-band recording or touring efforts. No further substantive second-album activity has been documented publicly as of 2026. (Wikipedia — The La’s, as of 2026-10-18)

Summary

The evidence points to several genuine but brief attempts — most notably the 1996 Kew studio sessions and the 2009 Babyshambles-backed plan — both of which collapsed before any recordings were completed. Mavers’ publicly stated intention, from the mid-2000s onward, was to first re-record the debut to his own satisfaction before moving on to new material; this sequencing appears to have prevented the second album from ever reaching a recording stage. The pattern throughout mirrors the debut album’s own history: sessions begin, an obstacle or dissatisfaction arises, the project is abandoned.
