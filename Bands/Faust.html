Faust

Discogs IDs: 26950 (1971–2004 + general), 988787 (Faust (6) — post-2004 Irmler faction), 988814 (Faust (7) — post-2004 Peron/Diermaier faction) Active: 1971–present (with gaps; reformed 1990s) Origin: Hamburg / Wümme, Germany Genres: Krautrock, experimental rock, sound collage, avant-garde Labels: Polydor (1971–72), Virgin (1973), Recommended Records (1979–88), ReR Megacorp (from 1988), Table Of The Elements (1994), Klangbad (1996–), Staubgold (2002–), Bureau B (2021–)

Krautrock / experimental rock group formed in Hamburg in 1970–71. Among the key first-generation krautrock acts alongside Can, Neu!, Cluster, and Amon Düül II. Pioneers of the studio-as-instrument approach and of collaged sound; their early records on Polydor and Virgin were commercial failures but became enormously influential on subsequent ambient, industrial, and post-rock music. Jean-Hervé Peron and Werner Diermaier are long-term members. The band also played on early Slapp Happy recordings during the Wümme period. Named influences include Radiohead, Throbbing Gristle, Cabaret Voltaire, Stereolab, Sonic Youth, Mark E. Smith, Nurse With Wound, and Madlib (Wikipedia).

History

Formation (1970–71)

Faust formed in 1970 in Hamburg under the aegis of producer and former music journalist Uwe Nettelbeck. Two pre-existing groups rarely seen live were merged: Nukleus (comprising Rudolf Sosna, Jean-Hervé Peron, and Gunther Wüsthoff) and Campylognatus Citelli (Werner Diermaier, Hans Joachim Irmler, and Arnulf Meifert). Whether the group approached Nettelbeck or Polydor tasked him with finding a band is disputed. Either way, Polydor signed them and the group relocated to a former schoolhouse at Wümme, outside Hamburg, where they lived, rehearsed, and recorded for three years. Engineer Kurt Graupner (Discogs ID: 331286) worked alongside Nettelbeck on the early sessions. (Wikipedia)

Polydor Era / Wümme Period (1971–72)

The self-titled debut Faust (Polydor, 1971) was released on clear vinyl in a clear sleeve with an X-ray of a human fist silkscreened on the outer sleeve — a striking visual statement matched by the album’s dissonant, collage-driven sound. It sold poorly but earned critical acclaim. Meifert was sacked shortly after the debut’s release. The second album, So Far (Polydor, 1972), was slightly more accessible and made extensive use of the colour black in its sleeve design (inner sheets carrying different illustrations per song). Both albums failed commercially, and Polydor dropped the band.

Also at Wümme in 1972, Diermaier, Peron, and Sosna collaborated with American violinist Tony Conrad on what became Outside The Dream Syndicate (Caroline Records, 1973) — one of the few available examples of drone-based minimalism at the time of release. The sessions were produced by Nettelbeck and engineered by Graupner.

During the Wümme period, Faust members (Diermaier, Peron, Wüsthoff) also played on Slapp Happy’s first two albums, Sort Of (1972) and Acnalbasac Noom (1973), both produced by Nettelbeck. Peter Blegvad of Slapp Happy played with Faust at Wümme and subsequently toured with them in the UK; Uli Trepte (ex-Guru Guru, Neu!) also joined that tour.

Virgin Era (1973)

Signing to Richard Branson’s newly founded Virgin Records, Faust became one of the first acts on the label. The Faust Tapes (1973) was a cut-and-paste album assembled from private Wümme recordings not originally intended for release; it was issued at the price of a single (48 pence in the UK). The sleeve featured a Bridget Riley op-art painting (“Crest”). It reportedly sold over 100,000 copies but was ineligible for UK chart placement due to its low price. Faust IV (Virgin, 1973) followed — recorded at Virgin’s Manor studio in England, allegedly on night shifts while Mike Oldfield tracked Tubular Bells during the day. Virgin subsequently dropped the band. In 1975, Irmler organised a clandestine session at Giorgio Moroder’s Musicland Studios in Munich, supposedly financed by an unaware Virgin; when the studio demanded payment Virgin refused, and Sosna, Peron, and Irmler were briefly arrested before their mothers settled the bills. The recorded material resurfaced decades later as Punkt. (Bureau B, 2022). (Wikipedia)

Departure of Nettelbeck (1973–74)

Nettelbeck and engineer Graupner departed around the same time as Virgin dropped the band, but the break with Nettelbeck had a distinct internal cause separate from the commercial failure of Faust IV.

Multiple sources point to a gradual shift in Nettelbeck’s role — from enabling collaborator to directive producer — which collided with Faust’s core no-compromise, no-outside-interference ethos.

Péron (Freq interview, 29 May 2000):

“The pressure of the record industry was stronger and stronger, and they wanted to have…more accessible sound.”

Péron explains that this pressure forced Nettelbeck into an increasingly formal production role: “He was so much the producer that he was becoming too heavy on our music. He was starting to say ‘This is not good; you should do this’.” This directly contradicted the band’s founding principle — “we didn’t take any orders, we didn’t make any compromises.” — source: freq.org.uk/interviews/an-interview-with-jean-herve-peron/ (interview date 29 May 2000)

Irmler (quoted in extract from Neu Klang: The Definitive History of Krautrock by Christoph Dallach, published The Quietus, 30 April 2024):

“It was always a given for us that no one else was allowed a say in our music.”

Irmler states that Branson and Nettelbeck “tried it” — i.e., asserted influence — which “led to the break-up.” He specifically says “I fell out with Uwe over it.” His defiance extended to the label: “I refused to speak anything but Swabian to Branson.” — source: thequietus.com/culture/books/faust-virgin-records-neu-klang/

Diermaier (Perfect Sound Forever interview, furious.com):

“We broke up the Faust projekt because of different meanings with Richard Branson.”

Diermaier frames the collapse as primarily a Branson/Virgin disagreement rather than specifically a Nettelbeck conflict — consistent with the picture that Nettelbeck was being pulled toward Branson’s commercial expectations, making the band-vs-label conflict and the band-vs-Nettelbeck conflict effectively one event. — source: furious.com/perfect/faustzappi.html

Summary picture: Nettelbeck had always functioned as a boundary between the band and the music industry. As Virgin’s commercial expectations intensified through the Faust IV recording period, Nettelbeck shifted from advocate to intermediary and then to directive producer — relaying or echoing label pressure. The band rejected this, Irmler and Nettelbeck fell out directly, and when Virgin simultaneously dropped the band, Nettelbeck and Graupner’s involvement ended with it. No source suggests Nettelbeck departed first or independently; the break appears simultaneous with the Virgin dismissal, caused by the same underlying tension. Nettelbeck left no known published account of his own perspective on the split (he died January 2007 in Bordeaux).

Dissolution and 1980s Archival Period (1975–93)

Faust dissolved around 1975. Reissues and archival releases through Chris Cutler’s Recommended Records maintained interest through the 1980s: Extracts From Faust Party 3 (Recommended Records, 1979), Munic & Elsewhere (Recommended Records, 1986), Seventy One Minutes Of… and The Last LP (both ReR Megacorp, 1988). The group’s whereabouts during this period were largely unknown; the Recommended catalogues referred to their “disappearance.”

Reformation (1990–95)

In 1990 and 1992 Irmler, Diermaier, and Peron reunited for performances. In 1994 Faust toured the US for the first time — Peron and Diermaier assisted by Steven Wray Lobdell, with members of Sonic Youth as the opening act; Irmler did not participate in the US leg. The reunion was documented on The Faust Concerts Vol. 1 and Vol. II (both Table Of The Elements, 1994) and the album Rien (Table Of The Elements, 1994). Keiji Haino appeared on some 1994 San Francisco dates. Rudolf Sosna briefly rejoined but his chronic alcoholism cut the reunion short “after four or five exhausting days”; he died on 10 November 1996. (Wikipedia)

Klangbad Era (1996–2003)

Irmler took a more active post-reunion role, producing records and releasing them on his Klangbad label (co-founded with Cornelia Paul, 1996). The studio album You Know Faust (Klangbad, 1996) was followed by Edinburgh 1997 (Klangbad, live) and Ravvivando (Klangbad, 1999). In June 1997 Peron left the group; from mid-1997 to 2004, Faust operated as Diermaier, Irmler, Steven Wray Lobdell, Lars Paukstat, and Michael Stoll. A live collaboration with Diermaier, Peron, and Ulan Bator members (Amaury Cambuzat, Olivier Manchion) under the name “Collectif Met(z)” in November 1996 anticipated the later faction.

In 2004 Faust collaborated with New Jersey avant-garde hip-hop duo Dälek for Derbe Respect, Alder (Klangbad / Staubgold, 2004) — the last release under the unified Faust name before the split. (Wikipedia)

2004 Split

Background and timeline

Between 1997 and 2004, Jean-Hervé Peron was absent from Faust following an earlier falling-out with the rest of the band. During this period the lineup was Werner Diermaier, Hans Joachim Irmler, Steven Wray Lobdell, Lars Paukstat, and Michael Stoll. The group recorded and toured as this five-piece.

In 2004 the configuration broke again: Diermaier fell out with Irmler and reached back out to Peron. Both men were simultaneously thinking of reconnecting: according to multiple interview accounts, Diermaier and Peron called each other at almost the same time. They met in early 2005 at Diermaier’s basement studio and began recording immediately, producing material that became the DVD Trial and Error 2005 (released 2007 on Fuenfundvierzig Label). Diermaier also invited Amaury Cambuzat and Olivier Manchion from the French band Ulan Bator into the lineup. Their debut as the reconstituted band was at the Art-Errorist Avantgarde Festival in Schiphorst, Germany.

Cause of the split

The split had two distinct layers — a pre-1997 rupture between Peron and the band, and then the 2004 rupture between Diermaier and Irmler — and sources treat them differently.

Diermaier on the 2004 break with Irmler (Perfect Sound Forever interview):

“In 2004, I had trouble with Jochen Irmler. Jean-Hervé and me got together again.”

Diermaier does not elaborate further on the nature of the trouble in any available interview. He does draw an artistic distinction between the two working relationships: “With Jochen Irmler, we improvise better; with Jean-Hervé, we play more conceptual.” — source: furious.com

Irmler on Peron’s place in the group (Freq interview, October 1998 — describing the pre-1997 departure, but the formulation recurs in later accounts):

“If a single person decides individually how everyone does things, a group can carry on like this for a short time but not all the time.”

Irmler framed it as Peron making unilateral decisions rather than operating as part of a collective, and said Peron “decided to leave, so we agreed it’s best for the group that he did.” — source: freq.org.uk (interview conducted 28 Oct 1998; note this describes the 1997 split, not 2004, but characterizes the fundamental tension)

Peron on both factions (It’s Psychedelic Baby Magazine interview, 2011):

“We had to separate because our personalities clashed and our attitudes towards art diverged. So now, we have two Faust’s. Isn’t that better than just one?”

He frames the split positively, as a natural bifurcation: “faUSt, Faust… like amoebae or worms, faUSt and Faust generate by splitting.” Both incarnations, he notes, continued to “do recordings and perform live; continuing to explore both past and future.” — source: psychedelicbabymag.com

In a later interview (Little Village), Peron declines to dwell on the division: “now it’s just the two of us doing one entity of faUSt, and another one doing another entity of Faust. We are on absolute neutral terms. We don’t talk to each other, we splendidly ignore each other.” — source: littlevillagemag.com

In another interview (Japan Times, 2008), Peron “politely declines to discuss” the split at all. — source: japantimes.co.jp

Who left whom

Based on available sources:

  • 1997: Peron departed (or was pushed out) after disputes with the rest of the band about individual vs. collective decision-making. Irmler’s phrasing suggests Peron made unilateral calls the group couldn’t sustain.
  • 2004: Diermaier had an unspecified falling-out with Irmler and chose to reunite with Peron rather than remain. Neither Diermaier nor Irmler has publicly specified who initiated the break. Irmler retained the Klangbad studio, the Klangbad label, and the rehearsing/recording infrastructure in Scheer — suggesting he remained while Diermaier and Peron departed to form the new entity.

Name and capitalization

No formal legal dispute over the “Faust” name has been documented in any published source. Both factions continued to operate under variants of the name simultaneously. Peron and Diermaier adopted the stylized “FaUSt” (sometimes written “faUSt”) as a deliberate distinguishing mark.

Peron’s explanation (Louder Sound / Classic Rock, 2017):

“It’s written as faUSt because it’s us — Zappi and me. There’s another Faust in the south of Germany, based on Joachim Irmler, but we don’t have contact and I want to make sure that people know which version of Faust they’re getting.”

— source: loudersound.com

Irmler’s faction retained “Faust” without modification; his side has been described as “Faust, in the south of Germany” (Scheer, Baden-Württemberg). No trademark filing, court action, or cease-and-desist has been reported.

Infrastructure and resources post-split

  • Irmler faction: Retained the Faust Studio in Scheer, the Klangbad label, and continuing members Lobdell, Paukstat, Stoll. Organized the Klangbad Festival 2004–2011. Released Kleine Welt (Live) (2008, Ektro) and Faust Is Last (2010, Klangbad), the latter billed as their closing statement.
  • Peron/Diermaier faction: No fixed studio base; operated nomadically across France and the UK with rotating collaborators. Initial releases on Art-Errorist; then Bureau B from 2009.

Irmler’s public account — Quietus 2010 interview (web research, 2026-10-18)

The Quietus 2010 interview with Irmler (“Faust And Last And Always: Germany’s Most Radical Rock Group Talk,” June 2010, timed to Faust Is Last) was successfully accessed at thequietus.com/interviews/faust-interview-with-hans-joachim-irmler-for-album-faust-is-last/ (previously returned 403 on earlier access; accessible as of 2026-10-18). Also accessible at the article-numbered URL thequietus.com/articles/04451-faust-interview-with-hans-joachim-irmler-for-album-faust-is-last.

What Irmler says about two Fausts existing:

Irmler frames the factionalism against the band’s founding concept:

“Any of us can be Faust you know? Our idea was that all six original members could be Faust but there should never be two Fausts at the same time.”

He acknowledges that the Diermaier/Peron/Cambuzat faction “broke the rules, in a way,” but his tone is resigned rather than hostile: “It took a little while for me to get used to it but now I think… ‘Why not?!‘” He frames this as consistent with the band’s anarchic ethos.

What the interview does NOT contain: Irmler does not name 2004 or any specific year for the split; he does not say who initiated the break; he does not describe the circumstances of Diermaier’s departure or characterize it as a personal falling-out. The interview is focused on Faust Is Last (2010) and the band’s legacy rather than the internal dispute. This is the fullest available statement from Irmler’s side, and it is notable for its lack of grievance or specific accusation.

Summary picture: Irmler’s public account of the 2004 split is thin. His fullest statement is the Quietus 2010 piece, which addresses the factional situation abstractly (the “two Fausts” concept vs. the founding ideal) without naming dates, causes, or individuals. He has not publicly aired a grievance against Diermaier or described the 2004 events in detail in any interview found. The most specific public account of the 2004 break remains Diermaier’s, in the Perfect Sound Forever interview: “In 2004, I had trouble with Jochen Irmler.”
