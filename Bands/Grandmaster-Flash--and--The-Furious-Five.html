Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five

Pioneering hip-hop collective formed in the South Bronx, New York City, 1978. Comprised one DJ — Grandmaster Flash (Joseph Saddler) — and five MCs. First hip-hop group inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (2007). Discogs artist ID: 20590.

Also related: Grandmaster Melle Mel & The Furious Five (Discogs ID: 17552) — a splinter lineup that continued during and after Flash’s departure.

Biography

Formation and Pre-Enjoy Period (1977–1979)

Joseph Saddler — Grandmaster Flash — developed his DJ technique in the South Bronx, refining the “clock theory” method of cueing breakbeats and the “Quick Mix Theory” of extending breaks by cutting between two copies of a record. He assembled the five MCs from the South Bronx DJ/MC circuit. The group performed at block parties and park jams before any commercial recording.

The Furious Five as a separate Discogs entity (ID: 70351) reflects the MCs’ billing on some releases without Flash.

Enjoy Records Period (1979–1980)

Before the Sugar Hill deal, the group recorded two singles for Enjoy Records (Discogs ID: 10149), produced by label owner Bobby Robinson:

Contemporary Enjoy Records hip-hop acts included Treacherous Three and The Fearless Four.

Sugar Hill Records Period (1980–1983)

The group signed to Sugar Hill Records (hip-hop) (Discogs ID: 4776), the label run by Sylvia Robinson and her production company Sylvia Inc. The Sugar Hill period produced their major commercial and critical work.

“The Message” (1982)

Released on Sugar Hill Records (cat. SHL 117). Widely cited as the first major “conscious rap” record — a grim portrait of inner-city life that broke sharply from party-rap conventions. Credits confirmed via Discogs release 50594:

  • Producer: Sylvia Inc. (Sylvia Robinson)
  • Written-By: Clifton “Jiggs” Chase, Ed Fletcher (Duke Bootee), Melvin Glover (Melle Mel), Sylvia Robinson
  • Note: Ed Fletcher (credited as “E. Fletcher”) = Duke Bootee (Discogs ID 21918, real name Edward G. Fletcher). The song is primarily credited as the work of Duke Bootee and Melle Mel, with Sylvia Robinson’s involvement in the production.
  • The group is the primary artist but the writing and initial conception came largely from Duke Bootee, who was a Sugar Hill house writer.

68 pressings of the UK release alone; 53 of another UK pressing. One of the most-pressed hip-hop singles in the DB.

“White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It)” (1983)

Primary artists on French pressing (Discogs 231323): Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five featuring Melle Mel and Duke Bootee. The song is built around a re-performed version of Liquid Liquid’s “Cavern” bassline. The resulting legal action brought by 99 Records won a $660,000 judgment that was never collected — Sugar Hill was already on its way to insolvency. See 99 Records for details.

Melle Mel and Duke Bootee are credited as featured artists on the primary line of some pressings, reflecting the transitional period when Flash and Mel were operating semi-separately.

Split and Parallel Lineups (1983)

Relations between Flash and Melle Mel broke down in 1983. Two simultaneous lineups resulted:

  • Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five — Flash’s faction
  • Grandmaster Melle Mel & The Furious Five (Discogs ID: 17552) — Mel’s faction, which continued on Sugar Hill; released the Beat Street soundtrack contributions (1984), “Step Off” (1984), “We Don’t Work For Free” (1984), “Pump Me Up” (1985)

Melle Mel’s lineup included Edward Morris (Scorpio), Keith Wiggins (Cowboy), and Melvin Glover, plus King Lou (3) (Discogs ID: 2410706).

The self-titled LP (1983, Germany, Discogs 67120) appears to be a German pressing/compilation-style release with minimal credits in the DB.

Reunion and Elektra Period (1987–1988)

A reunion brought Flash and Mel back together for one album:

On The Strength (1988, Elektra) — Discogs release 86709

Full credits:

  • Produced and arranged by Grandmaster Flash & The Furious Five
  • Grandmaster Flash: Turntables, Drum Programming, Mixed By
  • Melle Mel: Vocals
  • Cowboy: Vocals
  • Kid Creole (Kidd Creole): Vocals
  • Rahiem: Vocals
  • Scorpio (3): Vocals
  • Music consultant: Afrika Bambaataa
  • Mix engineers: Kevin (Blaze) Hedge, Mark Partis
  • Studios: Oasis, Secret Sound, Skyline, Unique, Waterfront

Singles from this period include “Fly Girl / Cold In Effect” (1988) and “Magic Carpet Ride” (1988). The album failed commercially; the group disbanded permanently after.

Sugar Hill Bankruptcy (1985)

Sugar Hill Records (hip-hop) declared bankruptcy in 1985. The label’s catalog was later acquired by Rhino Records (its Discogs parent label listed as Rhino Records (2), ID: 33244). The financial collapse was partly driven by unpaid royalties to artists and the White Lines lawsuit verdict.

Post-Reunion and Legacy

  • 1989: The Greatest Hits (Sugar Hill Records reissue)
  • 1991, 1993, 1999: various reissue/compilation editions on Unidisc, Sequel Records, Rhino
  • 1995: Credited on Duran Duran’s “White Lines” cover (Parlophone)
  • 2006: The Definitive Groove Collection (Rhino / Sugar Hill)
  • 2007: First hip-hop act inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame
  • Also in 2007: Appeared on various compilations; Grandmaster Flash continued solo DJ work through Elektra and Strut
