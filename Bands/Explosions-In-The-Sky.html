Explosions In The Sky

Discogs ID: 190408
Active: 1999–present
Origin: Austin, Texas, USA
Genres: Post-rock, instrumental rock
Labels: Sad Loud America → Temporary Residence Limited → Bella Union (UK/Europe)

American instrumental post-rock quartet formed in Austin, Texas in 1999. Originally played under the name Breaker Morant before changing to their current name that same year. All four original members have remained throughout the band’s existence.

Friday Night Lights — Origin of the Score Commission

How the gig came about

The contact was initiated entirely by Brian Reitzell, not by director Peter Berg. Reitzell had just finished the Lost in Translation soundtrack and was researching music for Friday Night Lights when he came across an interview with the band and listened to their 2003 album The Earth Is Not A Cold Dead Place. He concluded it was precisely what a West Texas football film should sound like — expansive, emotional, the right terrain.

Reitzell reached out to the band directly via email, pitching the project in straightforward terms: he was working on a new movie and wanted to know if they were interested in doing music for it. (Quoted via Wikipedia, sourced originally from Texas Monthly, see below.)

Studio resistance: When Reitzell brought the band to studio executives, they pushed back — working with a full band rather than a conventional composer seemed unworkable, and instrumental rock in a mainstream Hollywood film was practically unheard of at the time. Reitzell assembled a pitch mix of their music; it changed the executives’ minds. They flew Reitzell to Austin to meet the band in person over Mexican food. (Texas Monthly, paywalled; details aggregated in search results — treat with some caution pending direct article access.)

The band’s reaction: Munaf Rayani later recalled being apprehensive: “We were pretty nervous because we thought a film about football in West Texas had the real potential to somehow be like Varsity Blues.” Their personal connection to the subject matter — all members are from or raised in West Texas and were familiar with H.G. Bissinger’s book — ultimately overcame the hesitation. (Source: Texas Monthly ibid.; also aggregated at multiple music press outlets.)

Peter Berg’s role: Berg does not appear to have initiated the contact. Reitzell was the gatekeeper. A detail that circulated from the production: Berg reportedly pointed to a sunset on set one day and said “Look — it’s explosions in the sky,” which may have reinforced the fit he already sensed. (Source: Wikipedia, Friday Night Lights (film soundtrack), as of 2025-06-28.)

Production: The band composed most of the score during June and August 2004, recorded in Burbank, California. Despite access to rare studio equipment, they kept to their standard songwriting approach. “Your Hand In Mine” from The Earth Is Not A Cold Dead Place was adapted for the film. Reitzell kept them creatively isolated from the studio machinery so they could work as they would have in their own practice. (Source: Wikipedia, Friday Night Lights (film soundtrack), as of 2025-06-28.)

Industry significance: At the time, having a rock band rather than an individual composer score a major studio film was highly unusual. Drummer Hrasky later reflected: “I still think there was just this weird thing where they didn’t understand what to do with a band.” The band was on tour when the film opened in October 2004. The work is widely credited with reshaping how music is used in sports films and broadcasts. (Source: Spitfire Audio, “Explosions in the Sky on their new album and the enduring power of wordless music”.)

Common misconception: The band did not write the theme for the subsequent TV series Friday Night Lights. That theme is an original composition by W.G. Snuffy Walden. (Source: Wikipedia, Explosions in the Sky, as of 2025-06-28.)

Notes on sources

The Texas Monthly article (“How Explosions in the Sky Became the Sound of Film and Television”) is behind a paywall and could not be fetched directly during this research pass. Several details above (Mexican food meeting in Austin, studio executive resistance, Rayani “Varsity Blues” quote) originate there and have been aggregated across multiple music press outlets with consistent wording — assessed as reliable but not independently verified from the primary text. A future research pass with paywall access should confirm exact quotes and any additional detail.

Early Austin Venues (1999–2003)

The following venues have been confirmed through press sources and archived live recordings. The period covers from formation through to just after signing with Temporary Residence Limited (the band first appeared on TRL with Those Who Tell The Truth in 2001).

KVRX Student Radio, Austin — First ever performance, July 4, 1999. Played under the name Breaker Morant on KVRX’s Local Live series. The session yielded their first recording (“Remember Me As A Time Of Day,” later on How Strange, Innocence), and it was while packing up after the set that fireworks overhead prompted the name change to Explosions In The Sky. Drummer Hrasky: “I don’t even know how they knew us. Nobody knew who we were. We hadn’t even really played any shows yet.” (Austin Chronicle, “Catastrophe and the Cure,” 2011-07-01; setlist confirmed at setlist.fm)

Emo’s, Austin — First proper show, January 23, 2000. A bootlegged recording from an Emo’s show later reached Temporary Residence Limited and was a factor in the label signing them. Multiple archived recordings of Emo’s shows exist: June 20, 2000 (Internet Archive) and August 30, 2001 (Internet Archive). (Austin Chronicle ibid.)

Mercury, Austin — Multiple shows documented 2002–2003, including February 7, 2002 (Internet Archive), April 13, 2002 (Internet Archive), and a SXSW 2003 show on March 12, 2003 (setlist.fm). The SXSW set included early versions of The Earth Is Not A Cold Dead Place material.

The Parish, Austin — Show documented October 29, 2003, after The Earth Is Not A Cold Dead Place had been released (Internet Archive). This is a post-signing but still early-career Austin appearance.

Waterloo Records, Austin — Not a performance venue, but the site where drummer Chris Hrasky posted his “drummer wanted” flyer, leading directly to the band’s formation. (Austin Chronicle ibid.)

Note on sourcing: The January 23, 2000 date for the first Emo’s show, and the detail that a bootleg from an Emo’s show reached TRL, originate from the Austin Chronicle 2011 longform piece (“Catastrophe and the Cure”). The Chronicle article is the most substantial primary press source on the band’s early Austin years and was fetched directly. The archive.org recordings independently corroborate the Emo’s and Mercury venue associations.

Notes (web) — Formation date and “Breaker Morant” name origin

Formation date/month in 1999

No primary source (interview, press piece, band statement) giving the exact formation date has been found or is accessible online. What the web does establish:

  • Multiple search-result aggregations (drawing on AllMusic and Stereogum biographical text) place the four members’ first meeting in early 1999, with the Stereogum 20th-anniversary piece describing the scene as: Hrasky having recently moved to Austin posted flyers; James, Rayani, and Smith “put up flyers around town and linked up with Chris Hrasky.” That Stereogum piece (fetched directly) says only “the late ’90s” for when the West Texas trio moved to Austin; it does not specify month. (Stereogum, “How Strange, Innocence Turns 20”, 2020)
  • A search aggregator summary (not independently verified to a primary article) describes “In February 1999, three longtime friends from West Texas went to a pizza place in Austin, Texas, for a prearranged meeting with a young man from Illinois.” This detail is consistent with multiple sources placing the debut at KVRX on July 4, 1999 — meaning roughly five months elapsed between first meeting and first performance. Source of the “February 1999” claim could not be confirmed from any primary article or interview accessed during this research pass; it likely originates from the Austin Chronicle 2003 longform piece (“Born on the Fourth of July”), which returned a 403 error. Treat as unverified pending direct access to that article.
  • The July 4, 1999 KVRX show (and accompanying name change) remains the only precisely dated early milestone, confirmed by Austin Chronicle 2011 and setlist.fm.

”Breaker Morant” name origin

No band member quote or primary interview explaining why they chose the name “Breaker Morant” has been located. What the web offers:

  • Multiple aggregator summaries (Wikipedia mirrors, biography aggregators) state the band “started making music together under the name Breaker Morant, after the Australian movie.” The referent would be Breaker Morant (1980, dir. Bruce Beresford), a war-drama about the 1902 court martial of Australian Lt. Harry “Breaker” Morant and others during the Boer War — a critically acclaimed film (7.8 on IMDb, ten Australian Film Institute Awards). This claim is plausible given the members’ ages (college-age in 1999, film accessible on VHS/cable) but no primary interview or original-text source containing this explanation has been accessed. The claim circulates in Wikipedia-mirror sites and aggregators without a traceable first source.
  • The Austin Chronicle 2011 longform piece (“Catastrophe and the Cure”) describes the KVRX show and name change in detail but — per the fetched text — does not appear to address why “Breaker Morant” was chosen in the first place (the article returned 403 on direct fetch; summary derived from search-result excerpts).
  • The Austin Chronicle 2003 piece (“Born on the Fourth of July”) returned 403 and could not be read. That article, given its title and early date, is the single most likely source for both the formation month and the Breaker Morant naming story.

Assessment: The “named after the Australian movie” claim is the leading hypothesis and is widely repeated, but it rests on aggregator text, not a quoted band member. Until the 2003 Austin Chronicle piece or a band interview can be read directly, this should be flagged as unverified.

Research pass conducted 2026-08-20. Austin Chronicle 403 blocked both the 2011 and 2003 pieces. AllMusic, ProgArchives, Riff Magazine, Seven Days VT, and List.co.uk all returned 403 or rendered without article body text.
