Lola (group)

Discogs ID: 61264

NYC-based house and disco project led by Bob Blank and Lola Blank, active from 1987 through at least 2020. The group entity on Discogs encompasses productions credited to both Blanks as a unit, with contributions from Arthur Russell and Kenny Blank on key releases. Material appeared on Jump Street Records, Syncopate (2), Vinylmania, and the self-operated Lola Records imprint (Stamford, Connecticut). Not to be confused with other artists named “Lola” on Discogs.

Overview

The Lola group represents the collaborative recording identity of Bob and Lola Blank as a front-facing artist rather than a production credit. Their debut single “Wax The Van” (1987) was recorded and produced at Blank Tape Studios with Arthur Russell contributing keyboards and co-writing, and Kenny Blank (Bob’s son, later known as actor/director Kenn Michael) on keyboards. The single received broad international distribution: Jump Street Records in the US and Syncopate (2) (an EMI sublabel) for UK and European territories, making it one of the wider-distributed outputs from the Blank circle.

“I Need More” (1988, Vinylmania) continued the partnership with Arthur Russell — his last documented studio contribution before his death in 1992. Fred Zarr (keyboards) and David Shaw were additional collaborators on that session. “Work It” (1989) appeared on Lola Records, a Connecticut-based house label connected to Bob Blank’s relocation from New York, and brought Kenny Blank in as co-producer and remixer alongside his parents.

The “Wax The Van” master was reissued on Groovin Recordings (Italy) in 2018, indicating ongoing collector interest in the original sides.

Blank Productions (Discogs artist ID 752944, also a group entity) lists Bob Blank, Lola Blank, and Kenny Blank as members, and has one documented production credit — “Changing Up” (1989, Sunshine Music LTD) — suggesting Blank Productions is a closely related but distinct production umbrella for the family unit, used on outside commissions rather than their own artist releases.

Notes (web)

Arthur Russell / Blank collaboration — formal vs. informal

No source identifies a formal publishing or recording contract between Arthur Russell and the Blanks for “Wax The Van” specifically. The available evidence points strongly to an informal, relationship-based arrangement consistent with Russell’s broader working method at Blank Tape Studios.

Bob Blank’s Tape Op interview (issue 167, tapeop.com/interviews/167/bob-blank) describes the Russell–Blank working relationship as casual and flexible: Russell would visit Blank’s Connecticut studio “by the day,” with no fixed booking procedure. Sessions were variously financed by grant money Russell controlled (“‘I have this grant. Here’s the money. Tell me when to stop.’”) or traded for goods (Russell once offered Blank “a beat-up 1951 Ford” in exchange for studio time). Blank would sometimes leave Russell to work alone, other times collaborate for hours depending on what Russell needed at the moment.

The same interview is the primary source confirming Lola Blank’s (“Lola Love’s”) connection to “Wax The Van”: Blank states directly that “my wife, Lola Love, had the record ‘Wax the Van’ and started performing at The [Paradise] Garage,” where Blank witnessed “thousands of guys dancing, going nuts.” This is the only known first-person account of the track’s origins from a participant.

A DMY piece on Russell’s dance collaborations (dmy.co/10-best/digging-deep-arthur-russell-s-dance-collaborations, as of 2026-08-07) describes the session as Russell recorded “with singer Lola Blank, her husband (and excellent producer) Bob Blank and Kenny Blank,” but gives no contractual or arrangement detail.

A Ransom Note feature on Lola (“House Hunting #35 - Lola,” theransomnote.com/music/playlists/house-hunting-35-lola/, as of 2026-08-19) adds the most specific framing found to date: it states the track “began life as a composition by Arthur Russell,” with Bob Blank then enlisting Lola for vocals (described as “a family affair”), and notes that Kenny Blank — described as Bob’s seven-year-old stepson at the time — contributed interjections. This positions “Wax The Van” as a Russell-originated piece brought to the Blank studio for realization rather than a collaboration built from scratch with the Blanks. The same piece notes the track was “a favourite of Paradise Garage prophet Larry Levan” and was licensed to Chrysalis Records (UK/European distribution). Rush Hour’s product description (rushhour.nl) similarly reads: “written by Arthur Russell who also plays keys,” foregrounding Russell as the author rather than co-author.

Caveat on the Tape Op interview: A careful re-read of the Bob Blank Tape Op interview (issue 167) shows that Blank’s mention of “Wax the Van” and his mention of Arthur Russell’s studio sessions appear in separate passages. Blank says: “I went to a disco two times, and that was when my wife, Lola Love, had the record ‘Wax the Van’” — but does not in that context name Russell as a participant. The connection between Russell and the track is established by sleeve credits and the Ransom Note/DMY pieces, not by a direct first-person statement in the Tape Op interview. The Tape Op interview confirms the informal day-rate/grant-funded model for Russell’s Blank Tape sessions generally, which by analogy supports the same characterization for “Wax the Van,” but the direct link is not stated there.

Summary: The preponderance of evidence points to an informal, relationship-based session: Russell arrived at the Blank studio with a pre-existing composition, Bob Blank produced and brought in Lola for vocals as a family/circle arrangement, and the co-write credit on the sleeve reflects standard Blank studio practice. No source — the Tape Op interview, the Ransom Note piece, the Tim Lawrence biography (Bob Blank is cited as an interviewee, per Google Books word-frequency analysis, but relevant passages are not in the preview), the NYPL Russell papers finding aid (which lists “Wax the Van” among Russell’s recordings but gives no Blank session detail), or any other press — locates a separate formal publishing contract or advance for this specific track. The question cannot be definitively closed without access to either Lola Blank’s own account or the Lawrence biography’s treatment of the Blank sessions in the mid-to-late 1980s.
