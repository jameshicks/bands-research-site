Overview

The DFA — short for “Death From Above” — was a punk-funk production duo formed by James Murphy and Tim Goldsworthy in New York in the early 2000s, operating as the creative engine behind the DFA label that bears their initials. Working across electro, disco, indie rock and house, they shaped a danceable, post-punk-inflected sound that defined the early-2000s New York dance-rock moment. Their closest collaborators included Eric Broucek, Nancy Whang, The Rapture, and The Juan MacLean. Goldsworthy departed in 2009, leaving Murphy to steer the DFA enterprise forward.

Notes (web) — Goldsworthy departure

Timeline note: Discogs and the overview above say “2009,” but contemporaneous press places the actual break in early 2010. Resident Advisor’s report is dated February 2010 (https://ra.co/news/11652), and Goldsworthy’s own statements at the time suggest his involvement had wound down over the preceding two years (i.e., from roughly 2008). The formal legal split was not until 2012–13. See conflict note below.

What the contemporaneous sources say (Feb 2010):

Jonathan Galkin told Resident Advisor (Feb 2010): “Tim Goldsworthy moved away to somewhere in the United Kingdom, without informing anyone here at DFA. We have had no word from him since he left.” [https://ra.co/news/11652]

Goldsworthy disputed this in a statement to The Quietus (Feb 2010): “I haven’t left.” He said his relocation was driven by work being UK-based and by family considerations, not an intentional departure. He acknowledged he had had limited involvement with DFA over the previous two years but said he remained a partial owner and was willing to contribute production or remix work going forward. [https://thequietus.com/news/news-tim-goldsworthy-leaves-dfa-records/]

No contemporaneous source identified a single precipitating event or creative falling-out. The public framing from DFA’s side (Galkin) was simply that Goldsworthy left without notice; Goldsworthy’s framing was that relocation to the UK for work and family did not constitute “leaving.” No interview or press account from 2009–2010 gives an internal reason for the split (disagreement, creative differences, financial dispute). The financial allegations came later.

Subsequent legal escalation:

By 2012 Goldsworthy had ceased all involvement and relocated definitively to the UK. In March 2013 James Murphy and DFA Records filed a lawsuit against Goldsworthy seeking $93,899 in damages. The complaint alleged breach of contract, unjust enrichment, failure to perform services he was paid for, outstanding loans, improper use of the company credit card, and unauthorized bank account withdrawals. Goldsworthy allegedly rejected attempts at an amicable resolution. [https://exclaim.ca/music/article/james_murphy_files_lawsuit_against_dfa_co-founder_tim_goldsworthy]

The Rolling Stone and Billboard pieces on the lawsuit (2013) were not fetchable (tollgate redirect). The Exclaim piece is the main detailed source for the lawsuit.

LCD Soundsystem connection:

Wikipedia notes (as of 2026-11-08) that the Murphy–Goldsworthy friendship “is rumoured to be the subject of the song ‘How Do You Sleep?’ from LCD Soundsystem’s album American Dream (2017).” [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Goldsworthy]

What Goldsworthy did after leaving:

From late 2009 onward, based in the UK, he worked as a producer and remixer. Confirmed post-DFA credits: remixes for Goldfrapp, Massive Attack (contributed to Heligoland, 2010), and DJ Hell’s collaboration with Bryan Ferry; production work for Little Boots, Monarchy, and The Maccabees (Given to the Wild, 2012); remix work for UNKLE (“The Answer,” 2010). [Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Goldsworthy; RA: https://ra.co/news/11652]

Conflict — departure date:

Discogs profile and the page overview above say “Goldsworthy exited in 2009.” RA’s contemporaneous report (Feb 2010) and Goldsworthy’s own statements place the public break in early 2010, with diminishing involvement from roughly 2008. Wikipedia (DFA Records) does not give a year. The 2009 date in Discogs may reflect when his active production work for DFA stopped rather than a formal exit, which the sources suggest was never cleanly defined until the 2012–13 legal proceedings.
