Spontaneous Music Ensemble

Discogs ID: 207925 Also known as: S.M.E., SME, Collective, Spontaneous Music Orchestra Formed: c. 1965–1966, London, England Active: 1965–1994 (various lineups) Genre: Free Improvisation

British free improvisation collective founded by drummer John Stevens (2). One of the first British groups to develop a systematically non-jazz-idiomatic approach to collective improvisation — chamber-scale, interaction-focused, rooted in spontaneous response rather than harmonic or rhythmic frameworks. A formative environment for Evan Parker and Derek Bailey, who both appeared on foundational early recordings.

Biography

Origins and the Little Theatre Club (1965–1968)

The Spontaneous Music Ensemble grew out of the experimental jazz scene centred on the Little Theatre Club in London, which John Stevens (2) helped establish around 1965–1966. Stevens had trained as a drummer in the RAF and by the mid-1960s was deeply engaged with the outer limits of jazz — listening to Ornette Coleman, Albert Ayler, and the emerging European improvisation scene — while also beginning to develop distinctly non-idiomatic principles of his own.

The earliest documented lineup brought together musicians moving away from conventional jazz structures. Challenge (1966, Eyemark EMPL 1002) captures this transitional phase: the personnel include Trevor Watts on alto and soprano saxophone, Kenny Wheeler on flugelhorn, Paul Rutherford (2) on trombone, Jeff Clyne and Bruce Cale on bass, with Eddie Kramer producing. Track compositions are credited to Watts, Rutherford, and Stevens, reflecting a collective authorship model that would persist throughout the group’s history. The idiom at this stage retains jazz tonality and structure but pushes toward open, responsive playing.

By 1967 the group had stripped down toward duo and small-group interaction. Archival recordings released decades later on Emanem — Summer 1967 (1995, Emanem 4005) and Withdrawal (1966–7) (1997, Emanem 4020) — document the transition: just Stevens and Parker improvising together, or a slightly larger set including Derek Bailey and Barry Guy. These sessions mark the point at which the SME stopped working within jazz conventions and began developing what would become their signature chamber approach: very quiet, detail-focused, with no conventional pulse or harmonic anchor.

Karyōbin and the Island Records Period (1968–1969)

Karyōbin (1968, Island Records ILPS 9079 / Hexagram ILPS-60) is the SME’s most prominent early recording and one of the key documents of British free improvisation. Produced by Eddie Kramer (Jimi Hendrix’s engineer, an unusual choice reflecting the countercultural circuits both inhabited), the album features Stevens on drums, Evan Parker on soprano saxophone, Derek Bailey on electric guitar (credited as “Dennis Bailey”), Dave Holland on double bass, and Kenny Wheeler on trumpet and flugelhorn. Two long untitled parts fill the sides; there are no composed themes, no pulse, no soloistic hierarchy. The approach — each player listening and responding micro-moment by moment — is fully realised here. The album’s title refers to the mythological birds of paradise that never land, a metaphor for music without a resting point.

The Marmalade release John Stevens Spontaneous Music Ensemble (1969, Marmalade 608 008), produced by Giorgio Gomelsky, shows a very different face of the group: a large ensemble including Bailey, Wheeler, Trevor Watts, Johnny Dyani on bass, Peter Lemer on piano, vocalists Maggie Nicols, Pepi Lemer, and Carolann Nichols. The two sides (“Oliv I” / “Oliv II”) are text-scored group pieces with vocal parts — ambitious and eclectic, leaning into the same experimental-rock adjacency that brought Gomelsky into the picture.

Big Ensemble and Consolidation (1971–1973)

The Source — From And Towards (1971, Tangent Records TNGS 107) documents the SME at large-ensemble scale. An eleven-piece group includes Watts, Wheeler, Mick Pyne on piano, Bob Norden and Chris Pyne on trombone, Brian Smith on tenor and soprano, Marcio Mattos and Ron Mathewson on bass, Ray Warleigh on alto saxophone and flute, and Stevens composing and directing as well as playing drums. All compositions are credited to Stevens. The sleeve was co-designed by Stevens and Jak Kilby, who photographed many SME sessions throughout this period.

Birds Of A Feather (1972, BYG Records BYG 529 023) — recorded in France and released on the French avant-garde label — strips back to a quintet: Stevens, Watts, Julie Tippetts (vocals and guitar), Ron Herman on bass, produced by Giorgio Gomelsky. Tippetts’s voice is integrated as an improvisational instrument on equal footing. All compositions credited to Stevens.

So, What Do You Think? (1973, Tangent Records TGS 118) returns to a quartet format — Stevens, Watts, Bailey, Wheeler, and Dave Holland on cello and bass — for a continuous 47-minute improvisation across two long LP sides. This is a relatively lean, stripped-down recording; liner notes by critic Max Harrison.

Bobby Bradford Collaboration and Emanem Period (1974–1977)

Bobby Bradford With John Stevens And The Spontaneous Music Ensemble (1974, Freedom / Intercord) brought American cornetist Bobby Bradford into the SME orbit, demonstrating the group’s continuing engagement with American free jazz lineages while developing their own distinct idiom.

Face To Face (1975, Emanem 303) marks the beginning of the SME’s association with Emanem, the label run by Martin Davidson that would become the primary document of the group’s later work. The album is a duo between Stevens and Watts, produced and recorded by Davidson and Mandy Davidson.

Biosystem (1977, Incus INCUS 24) is the SME’s sole release on Incus Records, Derek Bailey’s label. The lineup here is a chamber quartet: Stevens on percussion and cornet, Evan Parker as producer (not performer), Roger Smith on guitar, Nigel Coombes on violin, and Colin Wood on cello. All tracks co-composed by Stevens, Smith, Coombes, and Wood. This chamber-string configuration points toward the more intimate, electroacoustic-adjacent direction the group would develop in the 1980s.

Later Lineups and Continuity (1982–1994)

Throughout the 1980s Stevens kept the SME concept alive through shifting personnel configurations, often in duo or trio settings. Eighty-Five Minutes Part 1 and Part 2 (both 1986, Emanem) document Australian performances. The group had an extended association with Kent Carter (bass) during this period, as well as Roger Smith and Nigel Coombes as recurring partners.

A New Distance (1995, Acta 8) is the last SME album released while Stevens was alive, recorded at Conway Hall, London. The lineup is: Stevens on drum kit and pocket trumpet, Roger Smith on Spanish guitar, and John Butcher on tenor and soprano saxophone. A spare, chamber-scale trio. Liner notes by Steve Beresford. Stevens died in September 1994; the album appeared posthumously. The Acta label was run by Ian Vickers.

The group’s posthumous documentation continued on Emanem: New Surfacing (2013, Emanem) collects further archival material.

Karyōbin lineup vs Withdrawal (1966–7) lineup

Withdrawal (recorded 1966–7, released 1997) documents an even earlier and more intimate configuration: Stevens, Watts, Parker, Bailey (amplified guitar), and Barry Guy (double bass and piano). This is the lean chamber core from which the SME’s methodology crystallised.

Connections

  • John Stevens (2) — founder and constant; also led Spontaneous Music Orchestra, Amalgam (2), Detail (4), and solo projects
  • Evan Parker — early member; later became leading figure of European free improv; produced Biosystem
  • Derek Bailey — early member; went on to found Incus Records and Company (2)
  • Trevor Watts — co-founder; later led Trevor Watts & The Celebration Band
  • Emanem — primary documentation label, run by Martin Davidson
  • Incus Records — released Biosystem (1977); Bailey’s label, also issued SME-adjacent recordings
  • Company (2) — Derek Bailey’s parallel free improv collective, overlapping personnel
  • The Music Improvisation Company — parallel early British free improv group, overlapping membership (Parker, Bailey)
