Psychedelic German band formed in 1969. Originally from Bodenwerder (in Lower Saxony) ; they relocated to Berlin in 1972. During their existence, they only released one album (Golem) but in 1996 more unreleased recordings were issued. Then, between 2011 and 2015, Rotorelief released 4 “unreleased albums from Sand, that should have seen the light of day after the band’s unique released album Golem, but Sand split at this time”. For instance, “the titles of Desert Navigation were composed by Sand in the period of Golem, or by Johannes Vester (also by his band Johannes Vester And His Vester Bester Tester Electric Folk Orchestra) on one hand, and Ludwig Papenberg on the other hand, both still in the spirit of Sand”.

Overview

Sand was a psychedelic German trio — Johannes Vester, Ludwig Papenberg, and Ulrich Papenberg — formed in 1969 in Bodenwerder, Lower Saxony, before relocating to Berlin in 1972. Working in Krautrock, experimental, and space-rock idioms, they released a single album (Golem) before splitting, despite having recorded enough material for several more records. Their archive was gradually unearthed over the following decades: a 1996 release appeared on Current 93’s United Durtro label, and between 2011 and 2015 Rotorelief issued four further archival albums described as records that “should have seen the light of day” after Golem, with a catalogue entry as recent as 2020.

How Sand came to be released on United Durtro (1996)

The chain of connection runs: Stapleton’s record collection → Tibet → Johannes Vester → United Durtro.

Steven Stapleton as the vector. Steven Stapleton owned a copy of Golem (1974) as part of his collection of obscure German experimental music. Sand does not appear on the Nurse with Wound list (confirmed by checking the Wikipedia article for the list, as of 2026-11-05 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurse_with_Wound_list); Stapleton simply collected the record on his own. The brainwashed.com review of the 2010 Rotorelief reissue of Golem names “David Tibet, Stephen Stapleton, and Julian Cope among its more outspoken champions” of the album. https://brainwashed.com/index.php?Itemid=855&catid=101&id=25192%3Asand-qgolemq&option=com_content&view=article

Tibet discovers Golem via Stapleton. David Tibet found Golem in Stapleton’s collection and became, by his own account in liner notes, obsessed with the album. The brainwashed.com review of the Rotorelief Golem reissue states: “It is easy to hear why this appealed to David Tibet so much when he found it in Stephen Stapleton’s record collection that fateful day.” https://brainwashed.com/index.php?Itemid=855&catid=101&id=25192%3Asand-qgolemq&option=com_content&view=article

Current 93 covers Sand before making contact. Tibet’s obsession with Golem led Current 93 to record a cover of Sand’s “May Rain” for the 1992 album Thunder Perfect Mind — without initially having contacted the band. An unreleased version from those sessions (May 1992) featured David Tibet (vocals), Michael Cashmore (guitar), David Kenny (bass), Rose McDowall (backing vocals), and Steven Stapleton (drones); this version was later released as the United Durtro promo single When The May Rain Comes (UDOR4), given away free with the first 1,000 copies of Ultrasonic Seraphim. https://www.brainwashed.com/common/htdocs/discog/udor4.php?site=c93

Tibet tracks down Vester (1995). Tibet tracked down Johannes Vester through Klaus Schulze’s manager Klaus Müller. According to Vester in a 2020 interview: “In 1995 David Tibet contacted me. He had covered ‘May Rain’ without our knowledge.” Tibet then visited Vester in Berlin, and “over three days we searched my archive of SAND recordings,” resulting in the double CD Ultrasonic Seraphim. Tibet and John Balance of Coil had apparently considered remaking the entire Golem album but concluded it was impossible. https://www.psychedelicbabymag.com/2020/05/sand-interview.html

Stapleton’s role in the 1996 release itself. Stapleton contributed directly to Ultrasonic Seraphim (United Durtro, UDOR2/3CD, 1996): he created the back-inlay painting “Listening To Sarah” (credited under his alias Babs Santini) and wrote liner notes alongside Tibet and Vester. Mastering was by Denis Blackham at Country Masters. Per the brainwashed.com release page (UDOR23): https://www.brainwashed.com/common/htdocs/discog/udor23.php

Summary of the connection chain:

  1. Stapleton collected Golem as an admirer of obscure German experimental music (not via the NWW list — Sand was not on it).
  2. Tibet found the record in Stapleton’s collection and became obsessed with it.
  3. Current 93 covered “May Rain” in 1992 (with Stapleton playing drones on an unreleased take).
  4. In 1995, Tibet located Vester via Klaus Schulze’s manager Klaus Müller and visited Berlin to trawl the archive.
  5. The result was Ultrasonic Seraphim (1996) on Tibet’s United Durtro, with Stapleton contributing artwork and liner notes.

Vester also noted that Stapleton later created paintings for Sand album covers (under the Babs Santini alias), which explains the ongoing shared-credit count with both Stapleton and NWW-adjacent collaborators such as Andrew Liles and Denis Blackham. https://www.psychedelicbabymag.com/2020/05/sand-interview.html

Why Sand split after Golem (c. 1974–1975)

The clearest account comes from Johannes Vester in his 2020 It’s Psychedelic Baby Magazine interview. Asked whether the band toured to support Golem, he replied flatly: “No. SAND broke up immediately after the album’s release.” No further single precipitating event is named; the dissolution appears to have been a convergence of several factors.

Disappointment with the Kunstkopf production. Golem was recorded using Klaus Schulze’s Artificial Head Stereo Sound (Kunstkopf) binaural technique — a demonstration product for Delta-Acustic’s headphone-focused catalogue. Vester: “We ourselves were quite disappointed with the technology, which actually only came into play through headphones. We didn’t really believe in the later success of the album.” The Brainwashed.com review of the 2010 reissue (citing the album’s reputation) confirms this production approach compromised dynamic range on conventional speakers — something that was “Sand’s lasting chagrin.” Despite this, Schulze himself was well-regarded personally: Vester described him as “a very relaxed guy at this time” who “picked us up from home every morning with his S-Class Mercedes and drove us to the studio.” https://www.psychedelicbabymag.com/2020/05/sand-interview.html; https://brainwashed.com/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=25192%3Asand-qgolemq&catid=101&Itemid=855

Ludwig Papenberg’s diverging direction. After the album’s release, Vester states: “Ludwig pursued musically completely different projects.” Ludwig (and his brother Ulrich) moved into intermedia / performance art: they collaborated with visual artist Klaus Ritterbusch in the artist group Amelith, which “dealt primarily with the medium of film.” Their 1974 work Amelith 2: Intermedia-Konzert — a multimedia installation combining four-channel tape playback with electronic live music — was presented at the ADA 2 (Aktionen der Avantgarde 2) festival in Berlin, funded by the Neuer Berliner Kunstverein. This dates the Amelith work to the same year as Golem, indicating Ludwig had already moved on. https://www.nbk.org/en/video-forum/ludwig_papenberg; https://www.psychedelicbabymag.com/2020/05/sand-interview.html

Label intent vs. band reality. Despite the split, Delta-Acustic intended to continue releasing Sand material: Vester notes that the label saw Golem as a relative success and wanted more records. But the band’s dissolution made this impossible at the time, leaving the recorded archive unreleased until David Tibet unearthed it in 1995 (see “How Sand came to be released on United Durtro” section above). The four Rotorelief archival releases (2011–2015) confirm the volume of unreleased material that existed. https://www.psychedelicbabymag.com/2020/05/sand-interview.html

Post-split trajectories. Ulrich Papenberg and Johannes Vester continued as solo artists but were unable to release records at the time. The members “lost sight of each other” for years. Vester was not contacted about Sand again until 1995, when Tibet tracked him down through Klaus Schulze’s manager Klaus Müller.

Summary. The split was not a dramatic rupture but a natural dissolution: disappointment with the binaural production format, Ludwig Papenberg’s move toward intermedia/film art, and the absence of any touring or promotional activity to sustain momentum. The band simply dispersed immediately after Golem appeared, leaving the unreleased archive sitting in Vester’s archive for two decades.
