From Discogs

The Go-Betweens were a post-punk band formed in Brisbane, Australia in 1977 by singer-songwriters and guitarists, Robert Forster and Grant McLennan. They were later joined by Lindy Morrison on drums, Robert Vickers on bass guitar and Amanda Brown on violin, oboe, guitar, and backing vocals, before disbanding in late 1989. Forster and McLennan reformed the band in 2000 with a new line-up, McLennan died on 6 May 2006 of a heart attack and The Go-Betweens disbanded again.

In 1988, “Streets of Your Town”, the first single from 16 Lovers Lane, became the band’s biggest chart hit in both Australia and the United Kingdom (UK). The follow-up single “Was There Anything I Could Do?” was a No. 16 hit on the Billboard Modern Rock Tracks chart in the United States. In May 2001 “Cattle and Cane”, from 1983’s Before Hollywood was selected by Australasian Performing Right Association (APRA) as one of the Top 30 Australian songs of all time. In 2008, 16 Lovers Lane was highlighted on Special Broadcasting Service (SBS) TV’s The Great Australian Albums series as a classic example of 1980s rock music.

— Discogs artist profile, ID 83077

Overview

The Go-Betweens were a Brisbane post-punk group built around the songwriting partnership of Robert Forster and Grant McLennan, who co-founded the band in 1977. Their classic line-up expanded to include drummer Lindy Morrison, bassist Robert Vickers, and multi-instrumentalist Amanda Brown (violin, oboe, guitar) before they disbanded in 1989; Forster and McLennan reformed with a new line-up in 2000, but McLennan’s death from a heart attack in May 2006 ended the band permanently. Their catalogue is weighted heavily toward the 1980s — twenty-four releases in that decade alone — with Rough Trade and Beggars Banquet anchoring their UK presence and Capitol handling their late-period American releases. “Streets of Your Town” (1988) was their biggest chart hit on both sides of the world, “Was There Anything I Could Do?” charted on the US Billboard Modern Rock Tracks, and “Cattle and Cane” was named one of APRA’s Top 30 Australian songs of all time.

Label History: Rough Trade to Beggars Banquet (1982–1989)

Rough Trade (1982–1983) — dropped for The Smiths

The Go-Betweens released three records on Rough Trade in 1982–83: the Hammer The Hammer single, Send Me a Lullaby, and Before Hollywood. The Rough Trade relationship ended not through artistic disagreement but financial pressure: the label was running short of money and chose to concentrate its resources on a newly signed act. Forster has given a consistent account across several retrospective interviews: “it was good luck that we got signed to Rough Trade and made ‘Cattle and Cane’ and Before Hollywood, but it was bad luck that we got dropped — the record label was running out of money and decided to put what money they had on The Smiths.” [source: Robert Forster interview, The Spinoff, 9 November 2016, https://thespinoff.co.nz/pop-culture/09-11-2016/i-wouldnt-have-written-this-book-if-he-hadnt-died-robert-forster-on-life-with-and-without-grant-mclennan]

No source has surfaced in which either Forster or McLennan describes the Rough Trade exit as artistically motivated or connected to a desire to reach a larger audience. The circumstances were contractual and financial, with Rough Trade effectively choosing The Smiths over The Go-Betweens at a moment when the label could not fund both. [source: encyclopedia.com Go-Betweens entry, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/go-betweens]

Sire (1984) — Seymour Stein, one album, dropped for low sales

After Rough Trade, the band was picked up by Seymour Stein at Sire Records, who signed them on the strength of word-of-mouth enthusiasm; Spring Hill Fair (1984) was the result. It was their only Sire release and the only album from their original tenure (1977–1989) to come out on a major label. Despite critical appreciation and successful European and North American touring, sales were poor, and Sire dropped the band. [source: encyclopedia.com Go-Betweens entry, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/go-betweens; The New Perfect Collection blog, https://thenewperfectcollection.com/2015/11/26/67-spring-hill-fair-go-betweens-1984/]

An anecdote circulates about Seymour Stein and Madonna: Stein had signed both acts around the same time, and the rapid rise of Madonna rendered Sire unable or unwilling to invest promotional resources in the Go-Betweens at the same scale. The exact sequence (and whether the band was dropped or left) is rendered ambiguous by overlapping retellings; a commenter on The New Perfect Collection blog claims Stein told the band he was prepared to drop Madonna for them, and that the band declined — though this story is noted by the same commenter as probably apocryphal. [source: The New Perfect Collection blog, comments section, https://thenewperfectcollection.com/2015/11/26/67-spring-hill-fair-go-betweens-1984/]

No source attributes the Sire exit to the band’s sound being insufficiently commercial; the evidence points to a straightforward commercial failure on the label’s own terms, compounded by Sire’s resources being consumed by Madonna’s simultaneous ascent.

True Tone (1985) and Elektra (1985) — signed but never recorded

After Sire, the band signed with True Tone Records in Australia and with Elektra in Europe and North America in 1985. Neither relationship produced any recorded output: the band never recorded for True Tone, and the Elektra deal collapsed when the label closed before the contract was formally signed. [source: encyclopedia.com Go-Betweens entry, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/go-betweens; Wikipedia on True Tone Records (Australian label), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Tone_Records_(Australian_label)]

True Tone went on to handle Australian distribution of Go-Betweens material released on Beggars Banquet — specifically Liberty Belle and the Black Diamond Express (1986) and The Able Label Singles (1987) — functioning in effect as a licensing and distribution partner rather than a recording label. True Tone itself later ceased operations after a dispute with EMI over distribution rights. [source: Wikipedia on True Tone Records (Australian label), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Tone_Records_(Australian_label)]

Note on the vault Discogs label table: The table in the Labels section shows “Truetone Records” with years 1986–1988 and 8 Discogs entries, which is consistent with this Australian distribution role rather than an independent recording relationship.

Beggars Banquet (1986–1989) — signed January 1986, most stable partnership

In January 1986 the band signed to Beggars Banquet, their most stable label relationship and the one under which they made their three most celebrated albums. Beggars released Liberty Belle and the Black Diamond Express (1986), Tallulah (1987), and 16 Lovers Lane (1988). The most direct Forster comment on the overall pattern of their label changes: “What always amazed me was there was always someone to pick us up… It wasn’t as if we would get dropped and no one would be interested. We would get signed again, which made you wonder why you got dropped in the first place.” [source: Robert Forster interview, The Spinoff, 9 November 2016, https://thespinoff.co.nz/pop-culture/09-11-2016/i-wouldnt-have-written-this-book-if-he-hadnt-died-robert-forster-on-life-with-and-without-grant-mclennan]

Beggars Banquet signing mechanism — web research result (October 2026): A trawl of seven secondary sources, including reviews of Grant & I (2016) in the Sydney Review of Books, The Afterword, God Is In The TV, and GetIntoThis, plus the Forster interviews in The Spinoff (2016), Arts Fuse, and paulmcdermott.ie, found no passage from the memoir — nor any interview with Forster — that names a specific person at Beggars Banquet who initiated the signing, identifies an agent or manager as intermediary, or describes whether the band approached the label or vice versa. The circumstantial picture across all sources is consistent: the Elektra deal collapsed mid-recording (the label closed before contracts were signed), leaving the band holding a finished album they had recorded at Beggars’ expense; “Elektra pays for the record and doesn’t even know it. We’ve got an album that’s ours, we can sell it to anyone” — and Beggars Banquet signed them shortly after. [source: Liberty Belle Wikipedia article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_Belle_and_the_Black_Diamond_Express; Louder Than War, https://louderthanwar.com/the-go-betweens-beggars-banquet-catalogue-reissues-review/] Whether Grant & I contains the specific personnel/mechanism detail in a passage not excerpted or summarised in any of the reviewed secondary sources cannot be determined without reading the memoir directly. The UNCUT album-by-album feature (January 2016, https://www.uncut.co.uk/features/robert-forster-album-by-album-72395/) was paywalled and could not be retrieved.

Forster also noted in a 1986 interview that the band was “very ambitious” at this point: “I want us to be successful, and the band at the moment is very ambitious.” [source: Robert Plummer, Louder Than War, review of Beggars Banquet catalogue reissues, https://louderthanwar.com/the-go-betweens-beggars-banquet-catalogue-reissues-review/]

Was there a deliberate shift toward a more mainstream sound?

The evidence suggests a genuine artistic evolution rather than a label-driven commercial pivot. On Liberty Belle, the band’s stated aim was to write “simpler music, but with a fuller sound” — this description comes from critical retrospective analysis of the album, not a directly attributed band quote, but it is consistent with the sound of the record: self-produced by the band and Richard Preston at Berry Street Studios, London, with string arrangements giving the album more orchestral body than their Rough Trade work. [source: Louder Than War, https://louderthanwar.com/the-go-betweens-beggars-banquet-catalogue-reissues-review/]

By Tallulah (1987), the band made a more explicit commercial push, hiring producer Craig Leon — who had produced the first Ramones album and early Blondie records — in an explicit attempt to write more commercially accessible music. Four of the ten tracks were released as singles. [source: Louder Than War, https://louderthanwar.com/the-go-betweens-beggars-banquet-catalogue-reissues-review/]

16 Lovers Lane (1988), produced by Mark Wallis, took the polished approach furthest — Forster later reflected that the album had been “overproduced and overly pop-oriented without the commensurate units shifted.” [source: LA Review of Books, Chris O’Leary, 19 February 2017, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/part-company-the-ballad-of-the-go-betweens/; Magnet Magazine, April 2019, https://magnetmagazine.com/2019/04/03/a-conversation-with-robert-forster/]

The trajectory — from scrappy Rough Trade post-punk to orchestrated Beggars Banquet pop — was therefore real and consciously pursued. However, no interview attributes it to label pressure or label directives. The label changes appear to have been driven by outside forces (Rough Trade’s finances, Sire’s sales disappointment, the Elektra implosion) rather than by the band seeking a particular kind of label deal. The sonic evolution tracked alongside the label moves but was not caused by them: it was an internal artistic project that accelerated during the Beggars Banquet years, reaching a commercial apex on 16 Lovers Lane that still failed to break through.

Lindy Morrison commented that “if we’d used drum machines and just had synthesisers do most of the stuff — I think we could have got a hit,” suggesting the band was aware of the gap between their direction and what the charts actually required, and chose not to close it fully. [source: Wikipedia, Liberty Belle and the Black Diamond Express article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_Belle_and_the_Black_Diamond_Express]

Forster himself, in liner notes for a 1999 compilation, offered this retrospective: “We were too good for the bloody charts.” [source: Louder Than War, https://louderthanwar.com/the-go-betweens-beggars-banquet-catalogue-reissues-review/]

Disbandment 1989

The Go-Betweens disbanded in December 1989, following the world tour in support of 16 Lovers Lane. Robert Forster’s own biography page on his official site summarises the moment in six words: “December: The Go-Betweens split professionally and romantically.” [source: robertforster.net biography, https://www.robertforster.net/rfbiography.html]

Multiple converging pressures are documented across retrospective interviews, the 2016 memoir Grant & I, and the 2017 documentary Right Here:

Romantic entanglement within the band. Forster and drummer Lindy Morrison had been a couple for much of the band’s life; by the time of 16 Lovers Lane their relationship was over. Meanwhile McLennan and multi-instrumentalist Amanda Brown were romantically involved. The end of those relationships — and their overlap with one another — created what Chris O’Leary, reviewing Forster’s memoir in the Los Angeles Review of Books, described as “Morrison and Forster had broken up, and Morrison and Brown became a new faction within an already fractious power structure.” [source: LA Review of Books, Chris O’Leary, 19 February 2017, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/part-company-the-ballad-of-the-go-betweens/] The Right Here documentary review on Access Reel confirms: “Robert Forster and Lindy Morrison were in a relationship for some of those years. Grant McLennan and multi-instrumentalist band-member Amanda Brown were in a relationship for some other years. This was part of the stress and conflict.” [source: Access Reel review of Right Here, https://www.accessreel.com/the-go-betweens-right-here/the-go-betweens-right-here-review/]

Financial exhaustion and the touring grind. The band never achieved the commercial breakthrough that would have given them breathing room. Forster, as reported in O’Leary’s review of Grant & I, described a trap they had fallen into: they “never generated the money to buy time for regeneration or time away from each other,” and constant touring and recording had become unsustainable. [source: LA Review of Books, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/part-company-the-ballad-of-the-go-betweens/] The Right Here documentary review states explicitly: “Their lack of monetary success was one of the elements that drove the first break up in 1989.” [source: Access Reel, https://www.accessreel.com/the-go-betweens-right-here/the-go-betweens-right-here-review/] The band had also, according to other sources, been running a US support slot with Lloyd Cole in the fall of 1989, completing the touring cycle that finally exhausted them. [source: encyclopedia.com Go-Betweens entry, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/go-betweens]

Forster’s alienation from 16 Lovers Lane. Magnet Magazine (2019) and the LA Review of Books both note that Forster felt the album had been “overproduced and overly pop-oriented without the commensurate units shifted” — i.e., the band had bent toward commercial pop without the commercial reward. This compounded the sense that the group’s direction had run into a dead end. [source: Magnet Magazine, April 2019, https://magnetmagazine.com/2019/04/03/a-conversation-with-robert-forster/; LA Review of Books, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/part-company-the-ballad-of-the-go-betweens/]

Studio tensions during 16 Lovers Lane. Wikipedia’s article on the album (not reproduced here) notes Morrison clashed with producer Mark Wallis over pre-production methods. This friction was a symptom of wider internal tensions rather than the cause of the split, but it formed part of the accumulated strain.

The immediate catalyst. O’Leary’s review of the memoir identifies a mundane trigger: “After a random incident — Morrison dressing down a new bassist during practice — McLennan told Forster he wanted to leave, and Forster realized that he did, too.” [source: LA Review of Books, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/part-company-the-ballad-of-the-go-betweens/] The decision was therefore mutual, arriving suddenly after years of accumulating pressure, rather than the result of a specific strategic review.

McLennan on the broader life-stage dimension. In a 1999 interview around the acoustic reunion performances, McLennan offered a more philosophical framing: “We started as teenagers in ‘78. Twelve years together is a long time — even the Beatles didn’t last that long. We hit our 30s and, though it’s a bit American to say this, we grew up and it was time to experiment, time to be an adult. You can’t be in a gang all your life. That’s why bands are so good when they’re young — they have that fire and dignity.” [source: encyclopedia.com Go-Betweens entry, citing 1999 McLennan interview with Jim Sullivan of the Boston Globe, https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/go-betweens]

Acrimony and aftermath. The split was described as “acrimonious” in Rolling Stone Australia’s coverage of Lindy Morrison; Morrison herself later said of her relationship with Forster that “there was too much water under the bridge.” [source: Rolling Stone Australia, https://au.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/lindy-morrison-im-just-over-it-2125/] Morrison and Brown were not part of the tentative post-split plans: according to Forster and McLennan’s own account, the two men had considered forming an acoustic duo together — and when McLennan mentioned this to Brown, she ended their relationship. [source: Wikipedia, The Go-Betweens, citing band member accounts] The other three members (Morrison, Brown, and bassist John Willsteed) were not included in these post-breakup discussions.

Source note. The deepest first-hand account of this period is Forster’s 2016 memoir Grant & I: Inside and Outside the Go-Betweens (Penguin Books Australia). No published excerpt or interview has surfaced in which Forster quotes his own specific words to McLennan, or McLennan’s to him, at the moment of the decision. The above relies on O’Leary’s reading of the memoir, retrospective interviews, and the documentary. The memoir’s relevant chapters remain the single most likely source for direct quotes.

Disbandment 2006

McLennan died suddenly of a heart attack at his Brisbane home on 6 May 2006. He was 48. Forster arrived at McLennan’s house that day as a guest for a gathering, and was told the news on arrival; McLennan may have died while Forster was briefly away at a nearby bookshop. [source: theafterword.co.uk review of Grant & I, https://theafterword.co.uk/grant-and-i-inside-and-outside-the-go-betweens/]

On the decision to end The Go-Betweens, Forster has framed McLennan’s death as itself bringing the band to a close, rather than describing a deliberate choice to retire the name. His own website press copy reads: “In May 2006 Grant McLennan, my song writing partner died, bringing to an end The Go-Betweens.” [source: robertforster.net, https://www.robertforster.net/rfevangelist.html]

In a 2007 Teletext interview — conducted in the period when he was completing the posthumous Intermission compilation — Forster described his immediate reaction and how he decided what to do next: “When Grant died, I froze. I didn’t know what to do about music. Everyone left me alone to decide in my own time and, at the end of 2006, I felt the best thing to do was to carry on as we’d planned; finish sorting Intermission.” [source: 2007 Teletext interview with Robert Forster, cited in Wikipedia’s Intermission article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermission_(Robert_Forster_%26_Grant_McLennan_album)] This quote addresses what he did in the immediate aftermath — completing a planned release — but does not explicitly discuss whether he weighed continuing the band’s name.

He also said he “wasn’t sure if I was ever going to make another record” but eventually produced The Evangelist (2008) as a solo album rather than a Go-Betweens record. The album incorporated several unfinished McLennan songs. [source: robertforster.net, https://www.robertforster.net/rfevangelist.html]

In a 2023 NME interview Forster described the final reformation era warmly: “I’m just happy we did it, in light of Grant’s passing… I think we were really getting better too. Where Grant was when he passed away, he was writing his best songs in years.” [source: NME, https://www.nme.com/features/robert-forster-interview-the-candle-and-the-flame-album-go-betweens-3390240]

Research note: No interview found (as of September 2026) in which Forster explicitly addresses whether he considered continuing The Go-Betweens under that name and decided against it. His public framing consistently treats the band as having ended with McLennan’s death — as self-evident rather than a deliberated choice. The 2016 memoir Grant & I: Inside and Outside the Go-Betweens covers this period but no published review or interview excerpt has surfaced Forster articulating a specific reason for not carrying on the name. This gap may be resolvable by reading the book’s final chapters directly.
