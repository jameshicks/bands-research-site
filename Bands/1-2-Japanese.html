1/2 Japanese

US lo-fi art-punk / noise band founded by brothers Jad Fair and David Fair. Discogs artist ID: 178047. (Discogs name: “1/2 Japanese”; vault filename uses “1-2” per Obsidian wiki-link conventions.)

Overview

Half Japanese was formed by brothers Jad Fair and David Fair around 1974 in Uniontown, Maryland — though their first documented release, the self-released cassette Not Fade Away, dates to 1974, and formal vinyl releases began with Calling All Girls (1977) on their own 50,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 Watts Records. Rooted in radical anti-virtuosity and total self-expression, the Fairs initially approached rock music with no formal training and a visual-art sensibility; David Fair’s 1981 essay “How To Play Guitar” — explaining that beginners should tune out of standard conventions and simply play — became a touchstone document of the American lo-fi and outsider music movements.

The band has never dissolved or gone on hiatus: from the late 1970s through 2025, Jad Fair has driven an unbroken output that spans noise-collage, lo-fi rock, art-pop, and children’s music. Over fifty years the lineup has been radically fluid, cycling through dozens of collaborators while maintaining Jad Fair’s vocals and lyrics as the constant core. The band’s international footprint expanded in the late 1980s–90s through connections with UK-based musicians (particularly Mick Hobbs, John Sluggett) and Swiss/French players (Gilles Rieder, Jean-Maurice Rossel).

Key label relationships: early records on their own Watts imprint; mid-period through Iridescence Records (1984–86), then their own Watts label again; a period on Alternative Tentacles (1997–2001); one release on Emperor Jones (1997); a sustained relationship with Fire Records from 1995 through at least 2025; and parallel releases on Joyful Noise Recordings in the 2010s.

The 1993 documentary Half Japanese: The Band That Would Be King (directed by Jeff Feuerzeig) brought significant critical attention to the band’s history; it was distributed by Vanguard and helped cement their cult standing.

Recording Notes

Don Zientara / Inner Ear Studios sessions (mid-1980s)

Don Zientara’s home studio, Inner Ear, operated out of his Arlington, VA basement from the late 1970s until a 1990 relocation to a larger space on South Oakland Street. All confirmed Half Japanese work with Zientara pre-dates that move.

Confirmed at Inner Ear:

  • Sing No Evil (1985) — confirmed recorded at Inner Ear Studio. Source: Bandcamp Daily “The Essential Punk Legacy of Inner Ear Studio” (https://daily.bandcamp.com/lists/inner-ear-studio-label-profile, accessed 2026-07-19), which names the album explicitly, quotes Zientara (“Half Japanese were also crazy…in a good way! They would experiment…a lot in the studio”) and quotes bassist Howard Wuelfing describing sessions as “very loud, very intense, very spontaneous” with material introduced and arranged on the spot.

Engineer credited but studio not independently confirmed by a named web source:

  • Charmed Life (1986, released 1988 on 50 Skidillion Watts) — Don Zientara credited as engineer and mixing. Wikipedia’s article on Charmed Life lists him in the Personnel section (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charmed_Life_(Half_Japanese_album), as of 2026-07-19) but does not name the recording location. Given that Inner Ear was Zientara’s only studio through 1989, it is presumed Inner Ear, but no web source directly states this for Charmed Life.

  • Our Solar System (1984, Iridescence) — Zientara has 15 shared Discogs credits with the band spanning this period. No web source located names a studio for this album; Wikipedia’s Our Solar System article (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Solar_System, as of 2026-07-19) lists only the Fair brothers as personnel, no engineer or location.

Context: The Zientara oral history book The Inner Ear of Don Zientara (Antonia Tricarico, Akashic Books, 2023) includes Jad and David Fair recounting their recording sessions — described as “stuffing the Zientara family basement with three drum kits and all of their equipment for a live recording session” — but no specific album titles from those sessions are named in available book descriptions or reviews. Source: No Echo review (https://www.noecho.net/reviews/inner-ear-of-don-zientara-book-review, accessed 2026-07-19); Goodreads listing (https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/120839331-the-inner-ear-of-don-zientara, accessed 2026-07-19).
