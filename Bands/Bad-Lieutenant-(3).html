Bad Lieutenant, the New Order Name, and the 2011 Reunion

Bad Lieutenant’s formation is inseparable from the dispute over the New Order name following Peter Hook’s 2007 departure. Hook announced in May 2007 that he and Sumner were no longer collaborating; New Order’s official site initially contradicted this. By 2008, Sumner had confirmed there were no plans for further New Order albums. (Wikipedia: New Order (band), as of 2026-07-15)

Sumner’s public framing in 2009 was that continuing as New Order without Hook was not possible — analogising the situation to Joy Division becoming New Order after Ian Curtis’s death. He told interviewers that without Hook they would not be New Order anymore, so changing the name and starting again as Bad Lieutenant was the right thing to do. He also cited Hook’s unwillingness to let the remaining members use the New Order name as a practical constraint. (WFUV / TAS interview, 2009; NME/Slicing Up Eyeballs reporting, 2009)

In the same period Sumner described his commitment to Bad Lieutenant as primary and long-term: “This is my bag and I want to see this through…to the end,” adding that he had set aside other collaborative work to concentrate on it. (WFUV / TAS interview, 2009) He also cited the improved working atmosphere compared to New Order’s later years as a motivation for the new configuration. (Search aggregation of 2009 interviews)

The 2011 reunion

The transition back to the New Order name came abruptly. In September 2011, Sumner, Stephen Morris, and Gillian Gilbert announced two charity concerts — billed under the New Order name — at Ancienne Belgique, Brussels (17 October) and La Bataclan, Paris (18 October), in aid of music video producer Michael Shamberg’s medical costs. The reformed lineup retained Phil Cunningham from Bad Lieutenant and brought in Bad Lieutenant bassist Tom Chapman (2) as Hook’s permanent replacement; Gilbert returned for the first time in a decade. (NME, September 2011; Slicing Up Eyeballs, October 2011)

Sumner later described the initial intention modestly: “We did two gigs for Michael. It was a brave new world for us, because we didn’t have Peter Hook in the band, and we didn’t know how people would react.” Stephen Morris echoed this, saying “It started a year ago when we were just gonna do two shows in December. And that was it really. It could easily have been just two shows.” (Consequence of Sound interview, November 2012) The positive response led to extended touring through Europe, Australia, Latin America, and North America across 2012.

No public statement from Sumner explains why he switched from “Bad Lieutenant is my permanent home” to resuming the New Order name. The most likely practical explanation, supported by contemporary reporting, is that the remaining three members (Sumner, Morris, Gilbert) formed a new company — New Order Limited — to control the trademark independent of Hook, resolving the name-rights obstacle that had necessitated Bad Lieutenant in the first place. (search aggregation, multiple sources)

Peter Hook’s response

Hook stated the 2011 announcement “has taken me completely by surprise” and that he did not understand the decision, adding that it “would have been courteous and professional to have spoken to me in advance.” He compared New Order without him to Queen without Freddie Mercury. Separately, Hook tweeted sarcastically about the situation: “It’s a shame that, I thought Bad Lieutenant weren’t bad!” — an acknowledgment that Bad Lieutenant had been the interim project. (Slicing Up Eyeballs, September 2011)

Bad Lieutenant’s role: bridge or obstacle?

The evidence supports reading Bad Lieutenant as a practical bridge rather than an obstacle: it kept Sumner and Cunningham creatively active and together during the name-rights impasse, and Tom Chapman’s membership in both bands made the personnel transition seamless when New Order re-formed around him. At no point did any party describe Bad Lieutenant as blocking a reunion — the obstacle was the Hook-Sumner split and the disputed name, not the existence of the side project. Bad Lieutenant entered indefinite hiatus at the point the New Order name became available to the remaining members; there is no public account of friction between the two trajectories.

Morris, speaking about Bad Lieutenant in June 2010 — roughly a year before the New Order reunion — characterised it in terms that echo the Joy Division → New Order transition: “Bad Lieutenant is to New Order what New Order was to Joy Division — an evolutionary next step.” (DRUM! Magazine, June 2010) This framing suggests Morris regarded Bad Lieutenant as a genuine successor project rather than a stopgap, which makes the 2011 reversion to the New Order name more abrupt in retrospect. Morris gave no interview prior to the reunion explaining a change of heart.

New Order Limited — corporate and trademark history

The following account is drawn from court documents (the High Court case Peter Hook v Bernard Sumner and Ors), contemporary press, and the Justia trademark registry. It documents the legal mechanism by which the remaining members secured the New Order name independent of Hook.

Vitalturn Company Limited was incorporated in 1992 as the vehicle holding all intellectual property associated with New Order (and Joy Division, and The Haçienda). All four original members — Sumner, Morris, Gillian Gilbert, and Hook — were equal 25% shareholders and directors. Revenues from any use of the New Order name flowed through Vitalturn and were split equally. (vLex, Peter Hook v Bernard Sumner and Ors)

2 September 2011: While Hook was abroad, the three remaining members (Sumner, Morris, Gilbert) executed two coordinated actions:

  1. They amended Vitalturn’s articles of association to allow directors to pass resolutions by majority written vote rather than unanimously — stripping Hook of his veto as a 25% shareholder.
  2. They caused Vitalturn to sign an exclusive worldwide trademark licence granting rights to perform, record, and merchandise under the New Order name, in perpetuity (with a 10-year minimum term blocking early termination by the licensor).

The licence was granted to a newly created vehicle, at that point named Top Robe Limited. Hook’s solicitor was notified by email on Monday 5 September; Hook himself learned of it when it had already been executed. The judge in the subsequent High Court case described the scheme as “preconceived and coordinated,” noting it was carried out at a time “when they knew he was abroad and so would not be able to participate.” (vLex, Peter Hook v Bernard Sumner and Ors)

7 September 2011: Top Robe Limited changed its name to New Order Ltd (also reported as “New Order Limited”). Two days later — 9 September 2011 — the two benefit concerts were publicly announced. (vLex, Peter Hook v Bernard Sumner and Ors; Post-Punk.com, settlement announcement)

The US trademark for NEW ORDER is registered to Vitalturn Company Limited (USPTO serial no. 79975029). (Justia Trademarks)

Over the following four years (2011–c.2015) New Order Ltd reported approximately £7.8 million in income. Hook, who retained his 25% stake in Vitalturn but received no corresponding share of income flowing through New Order Ltd, claimed losses of over £2.3 million by October 2016. He filed suit in 2015. (Rolling Stone, “New Order ‘Disappointed’ in Peter Hook Lawsuit”; Post-Punk.com)

The case settled with a “full and final” agreement; terms were not publicly disclosed. Hook subsequently stated he was “finally happy” and could “play New Order tracks the way he wants,” suggesting the settlement included rights clarifications on his side. (kulr8.com wire report)

Conflict note: The off-the-records.com summary of the legal situation characterises the reformed band as “not New Order either — strictly speaking, they’re a completely different band who just happen to be renting the New Order name from Vitalturn Ltd.” This framing is consistent with the court’s description of the licence arrangement and is not contradicted by Discogs or Wikipedia, but is this website’s editorial gloss rather than a finding of the court. (off-the-records.com)

What triggered the 2011 name decision — available evidence

Resolved (October 2012). Sumner gave an explicit on-the-record account of the catalyst in an interview with Billboard, reported by Uncut on 22 October 2012. (Uncut, 22 Oct 2012) When asked whether he would have carried on with New Order if Hook hadn’t toured Unknown Pleasures, Sumner said: “Twenty million dollar question, that is. I don’t know. But we did think, why should we hold back if he’s doing that? He opened the gateways of hell.” He elaborated: “I thought it was disrespectful to the rest of us. But I must admit that once he started doing it, we did think, ‘What are we doing holding back with New Order?’ So, in a way — if you’ll excuse the pun — he showed us the light.”

A second contemporary interview (The Telegraph, February 2012, text excerpted at cristinarocks.com) adds further specificity. Sumner said: “Were we cool with him touring Joy Division albums? No. God knows, if he hadn’t done that, whether we’d be doing this now.” And: “Truth be told, we thought ‘F--- it, if you’re going to do that, what are we holding back for?’ As Winston Churchill said: let him do his worst and we’ll do our best. He said he’d work his way through the New Order albums after the Joy Division stuff, so we thought we’d head him off at the pass, really.” (cristinarocks.com excerpt of The Telegraph, Feb 2012; direct Telegraph link is now behind a News Corp Australia block.)

Both quotes confirm Hook’s Peter Hook & The Light Joy Division touring as the stated catalyst — Sumner’s position shifted from “we can’t use the name” to “why are we holding back?” in direct response to Hook’s solo touring of the shared catalogue. The 2009 framing (“we can’t use it without Hook”) appears to have been overtaken by the calculation that the remaining members had as much claim to the name as Hook had to the catalogue.

The remaining unresolved piece is the legal prerequisite: these interviews explain the emotional/strategic catalyst but do not address how the corporate restructuring (the Vitalturn articles amendment of 2 September 2011) was arranged, or whether the legal mechanism was a precondition for the announcement or followed from it. The interviews are consistent with the corporate action being a tool to execute a decision already made, but no interview sourced to date confirms that framing.

The closest available evidence for other aspects of the catalyst is:

  1. Hook’s Peter Hook & The Light performances (from 2010 onward) — Hook was performing Joy Division and New Order albums in full with his own band. Contemporary reporting and Classic Pop’s retrospective both note that Sumner and Morris were “angered” that Hook had “spoiled Joy Division’s legacy” by doing so. (Classic Pop, “New Order: the later years”) This is now confirmed by Sumner’s own words as the primary catalyst.

  2. The Michael Shamberg benefit concerts — the stated occasion for the reunion was fundraising for ailing Factory Records video producer Michael Shamberg. Sumner and Morris both described the two October 2011 concerts as the entire plan at the time, with the extended reformation emerging from audience response. (Consequence of Sound, November 2012 — already cited in the “2011 reunion” section above.)

  3. The corporate mechanism as prerequisite — the September 2011 articles amendment and licence execution preceded the public announcement by a week, and logically preceded any decision to tour. This suggests the name-rights obstacle (which had necessitated Bad Lieutenant in 2008) was resolved first, enabling the announcement, rather than the Shamberg concerts spontaneously creating the impetus and the legal work following. Sumner did publicly acknowledge the legal work in a January 2013 Billboard interview: “We had to go and sit down with a lot of lawyers and make sure that we could do it legally without being challenged.” This confirms awareness of a legal prerequisite but does not clarify whether the corporate restructuring was Sumner’s initiative, Morris’s, or jointly orchestrated. (Billboard, January 2013 — the “lawyers” quote is attributed to this interview in contemporary search aggregation; direct article text currently behind a TollBit paywall redirect.)

  4. Hook’s counter-narrative — Hook offered an alternative explanation in a March 2014 interview, suggesting Sumner’s actual motivation was disillusionment with the small venues Bad Lieutenant played rather than any strategic response to Hook’s Joy Division touring: “I do think this is what spurred Bernard into getting New Order back — is that he toured with Bad Lieutenant and couldn’t stand the shitholes. I will bet you a pound to a thousand that that’s what it was.” Hook added that Sumner “must have been sat there, thinking ‘I can’t be doing with this — I’m stealing New Order off that bastard!‘” (Gigwise, “Peter Hook: ‘Tw*tto Bernard Sumner stole New Order from me’”, 3 March 2014) This is Hook’s speculation, not a statement from Sumner or Morris, and is contradicted by Sumner’s own on-record accounts cited above — but it is the only named counter-narrative and is worth noting as the dissenting view.

Hook’s review (Louder Than War, 2014) characterises the memoir’s treatment of “the taking of the New Order brand name and good will in 2011” as something Hook “view[s] as illegal”, and Hook told NME (2014) that the book was “solely aimed at justifying the taking of the New Order brand name and goodwill in 2011” — but neither piece quotes what Sumner actually wrote as his stated reasoning. The Billboard exclusive in which Hook dissected the book chapter by chapter is behind a TollBit paywall. (Louder Than War; NME, 2014; Billboard exclusive — paywalled)

What can be said: Hook’s public response to the book (NME, Louder Than War, 2014) confirms the book addresses the 2011 name decision, but Hook’s characterisation of it as “justifying” the action does not reveal Sumner’s own framing. Whether the book adds further specificity, recants, or elaborates on those interview accounts is not determinable without reading the text.
