Bombers

Discogs ID: 48727

Disco act. One of West End Records’ first major roster artists (42 releases, 1978–2013). Key singles include “The Mexican / Dance, Dance, Dance” (WES 12112, 1978) and “(Everybody) Get Dancin’” (WES 22115, 1979).

Bombers were a Montreal-based studio project, created by producers George Lagios and Pat Deserio — both veterans of Montreal’s disco/pop scene. Lagios had previously produced records for Michel Pagliaro; Deserio was known for his cosmic disco project Kebekelektrik. The group was recorded at Studio Tempo, Inc. in Montreal and originally released in Canada on the Telson label. West End picked up the material for US distribution/release. source: canuckistanmusic.com

Featured vocalists included Tamara Lorincz and Mack “BC” Jones. Buster (Cherry) Jones, later of Talking Heads, also appeared on the debut. Alongside Gino Soccio, Bombers are considered among the most significant disco/dance acts to emerge from Canada in the late 1970s. source: chosensperms.blogspot.com

West End Records — Early A&R Strategy and Bombers’ Place in It

Mel Cheren and Ed Kushins co-founded West End Records in 1976. Cheren’s stated A&R philosophy was openly instinct-driven: “If I liked a song, we would put it out.” He credited genuine musical passion — “I have always had this love for music” — rather than market calculation, explicitly distinguishing West End from labels that were “just to make money.” source: disco-disco.com source: pineshistory.org

Sonically, West End deliberately departed from the lush orchestration of competitors like Salsoul, favouring a stripped-down, groove-centric approach. The label’s identity was not pre-planned but emerged organically from its accumulated releases. Cheren himself acknowledged uncertainty about what exactly defined the West End sound. source: beatsbeyondborders.com

The label’s first release (1976) was a soundtrack (Sessomatto); its second was a split 12” by Jakki and Sounds of Inner City (WES 12101, mixed by Tom Moulton). The catalog’s first entry appears to have been Philly U.S.A.’s “Speak Well” (WES 12096, 1976). source: fortunecity/westendrec source: disco-disco.com

Bombers’ “The Mexican / Dance, Dance, Dance” (WES 12112, 1978) arrived roughly as the 17th entry in the WES 12xxx series, making them one of the label’s first dozen-odd 12” single signings — alongside Karen Young’s “Hot Shot” (WES 12111, 1978, which sold close to a million copies) and Bettye LaVette’s “Doin’ The Best That I Can” (WES 12113, 1978, remixed by Walter Gibbons). The fortunecity discography page notes that Bombers’ releases helped put “this label on the dance map.” source: fortunecity/westendrec

West End also worked closely with DJ-remixers — including Larry Levan, Tee Scott, and Walter Gibbons — whose club play and remixes shaped the label’s market reach. This DJ-first feedback loop (sign what works on the floor, support the remixers who prove it) was central to how West End built its early catalog, and Bombers fit that model: their tracks were floor-tested disco product whose distribution Cheren took on for the US market. source: beatsbeyondborders.com
