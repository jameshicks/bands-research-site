The Doors

Discogs ID: 56798 Origin: Los Angeles, California, USA Active: 1965–1973 (reunion/tribute appearances 1978, 1993, 1997, 2000, 2011–2013) Genres: Psychedelic Rock, Blues Rock, Acid Rock Labels: Elektra Records

Biography

Formation — UCLA and the London Fog (1965)

The Doors formed in Los Angeles in July 1965, emerging directly from a pre-existing group called Rick And The Ravens (Discogs artist ID: 759311), which had included Ray Manzarek and his brothers. The founding mythology holds that Jim Morrison and Ray Manzarek — both students at UCLA’s film school — encountered each other on a Venice Beach, and Morrison recited lyrics that would become “Moonlight Drive.” Manzarek immediately saw the creative potential and proposed forming a band.

The name “The Doors” derived from Aldous Huxley’s The Doors of Perception (itself drawing on William Blake). The core lineup crystallized quickly: Morrison on vocals, Manzarek on keyboards and organ (also covering bass lines on a keyboard bass), Robby Krieger on guitar, and John Densmore on drums. An early bassist, Patricia Sullivan (Patricia Hansen), departed by late 1965; the band permanently operated as a quartet with Manzarek covering low-end duties.

The band played its early residency at the London Fog on the Sunset Strip in 1966, capturing material later documented on London Fog 1966 (Bright Midnight Records, 2016). They moved to the more prestigious Whisky a Go Go, where a series of residency performances brought them to wider attention — and eventually attracted Jac Holzman, head of Elektra Records, who signed them in 1966.

Commercial Peak — Elektra and the First Albums (1967–1968)

The debut album The Doors (Elektra EKS-74007, 1967) was recorded at Sunset Sound Recorders and produced by Paul A. Rothchild, engineered by Bruce Botnick. The album’s breakthrough single, “Light My Fire” (a Krieger composition), reached number one on the US singles chart. “Break On Through (To The Other Side)” was the opening track and first single. The album’s eleven tracks already established the band’s full sonic range — from garage-blues (“Back Door Man,” a Howlin’ Wolf/Willie Dixon cover), to extended psychedelic suite (“The End,” 11:35), to their cover of Brecht/Weill’s “Alabama Song.” Session bassist Larry Knechtel (Discogs ID uncredited on this pressing) played bass on the album. No bassist appears in the credits of this specific pressing; Manzarek’s keyboard bass covered the majority of bass duties throughout the Morrison era.

Strange Days (Elektra EKS-74014, 1967) followed the same autumn, again produced by Rothchild and engineered by Botnick. Doug Lubahn (Discogs ID: 252840) played occasional bass. The cover photography by Joel Brodsky became one of the era’s most recognizable album images. Mastering was handled by Ray Hagerty.

Waiting for the Sun (Elektra EKS-74024, 1968) reached number one on the US albums chart. Multiple bassists contributed: Doug Lubahn, Kerry Magness (on “The Unknown Soldier”), and Leroy Vinnegar (acoustic bass on “Spanish Caravan”). The album’s “The Unknown Soldier” was accompanied by a controversial short film depicting an execution. Jac Holzman served as Production Supervisor.

Orchestral Excess and Commercial Strain — The Soft Parade (1969)

The Soft Parade (Elektra EKS-75005, 1969) was the band’s most elaborately produced album and the most commercially divisive. Rothchild brought in orchestral arranger Paul Harris for orchestral arrangements, and multiple session players were added: Doug Lubahn and Harvey Brooks on bass, Curtis Amy on saxophone, George Bohanon on trombone, Champ Webb on English horn, Jesse McReynolds on mandolin, Jimmy Buchanan on fiddle, and Reinol Andino on congas. Morrison and Krieger wrote separately for the first time, with individual songwriting credits on each track — Morrison on the title suite and several others, Krieger on “Tell All the People,” “Touch Me,” “Wishful Sinful,” and “Running Blue.” The album produced the hit “Touch Me” but critics noted its divergence from the rawer first albums.

Return to Form — Morrison Hotel (1970)

Morrison pushed for a return to the blues-rock stripped sound on Morrison Hotel (Elektra EKS-75007, 1970). The album split into two LP sides presented as separate “venues” — Hard Rock Cafe (Side A) and Morrison Hotel (Side B). Produced by Rothchild, engineered by Botnick. Bass duties were divided between Lonnie Mack (on “Roadhouse Blues” and “Maggie M’Gill”) and Ray Neapolitan (most other tracks). John Sebastian appeared on blues harmonica on “Roadhouse Blues” under the pseudonym G. Puglese (to avoid contractual issues — he was signed elsewhere). Marc Benno contributed rhythm guitar. Design by Gary Burden; photography by Henry Diltz.

A notable recording session for the album took place at a club called the Doors Workshop on Santa Monica Blvd — a rehearsal/recording space the band had set up — foreshadowing the more radical decision to record L.A. Woman there.

Morrison’s Decline and L.A. Woman (1971)

By 1970–1971 Morrison’s personal deterioration — heavy drinking, legal troubles stemming from his arrest at a Miami concert in 1969 (where he was charged with indecent exposure) — had strained the band’s working relationships. When sessions began for what became L.A. Woman, Rothchild withdrew, reportedly finding the material “a nothing album” and feeling the vibe had shifted away from studio craft. The band and Botnick recorded the album in a rudimentary setup at their own workshop space, with mobile equipment.

L.A. Woman (Elektra EKS-75011, 1971) was co-produced by The Doors and Bruce Botnick. Jerry Scheff (Discogs ID: 255687) played bass throughout; Marc Benno (Discogs ID: 255686) contributed rhythm guitar on several tracks. The album is widely considered the band’s strongest blues-based work — the ten-track set includes “Love Her Madly,” “Riders on the Storm,” and the sprawling title track. Morrison’s vocals were raw and commanding; it was one of his last recording sessions before his death.

Jim Morrison traveled to Paris shortly after completing the album. He died there on July 3, 1971. The official cause was listed as heart failure; he was 27. No autopsy was performed.

Post-Morrison Period — Other Voices and Full Circle (1971–1972)

After a period of mourning and deliberation, the surviving trio — Manzarek, Krieger, and Densmore — decided to continue as The Doors. They released two further studio albums, both co-produced with Bruce Botnick.

Other Voices (Elektra EKS-75017, 1971): All songwriting credited to Krieger, Manzarek, and Densmore — no Morrison material. Multiple bassists: Jack Conrad, Jerry Scheff, Ray Neapolitan, Willie Ruff, Wolfgang Melz. Emil Richards on marimba and percussion, Francisco Aguabella on percussion. The trio shared vocal duties. Mastered by Bob MacLeod.

Full Circle (Elektra EKS-75038, 1972): Produced solely by The Doors (Manzarek, Krieger, Densmore), engineered by Henry Lewy. Backing vocalists Venetta Fields, Clydie King, and Melissa Mackay were featured (“The Other Voices”). Charles Lloyd played tenor saxophone and flute on two tracks. Multiple bass players again — Chris Ethridge, Jack Conrad (also rhythm guitar), Charles Larkey, Leland Sklar. Bobbye Hall and Chico Batera contributed percussion.

Neither album found the commercial success of the Morrison-era catalog. The band dissolved in April 1973.

Posthumous — An American Prayer (1978)

In 1978 the surviving Doors produced an album from recordings Morrison had made of his own poetry readings between 1969 and 1971. An American Prayer (Elektra 5E-502, 1978) set new musical accompaniment — composed and recorded by Manzarek, Krieger, and Densmore — against Morrison’s spoken-word voice recordings. John Haeny (Discogs ID: 256659) was chief engineer and co-producer. Sound restoration by Thomas Stockham and Bob Ingebretsen. Additional engineer contributions from Frank Lisciandro, Babe Hill, Fritz Richmond, Paul Ferrara, and others. Bob Glaub and Jerry Scheff played bass. Reinol Andino contributed percussion. Bruce Botnick was credited specifically for the pre-1971 Doors songs he had originally engineered.

The album reached the US top 60 and has remained in the catalog. It is classified as both Rock and Non-Music (Spoken Word) on Discogs.

Rock and Roll Hall of Fame and Legacy

The Doors were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1993. Brief reunions occurred: with Eddie Vedder and other guest vocalists in 1993, with Ian Astbury on vocals in 2000 (touring as “The Doors of the 21st Century”), and in 2011–2013. Ray Manzarek died in 2013.

The Doors’ catalog has been continuously managed and reissued through Doors Music Company in partnership with Elektra/Rhino. Key archival releases include the Box Set (1997), Perception box set (2006, containing all six studio albums remastered), and the ongoing Bright Midnight Archives series of live recordings.
