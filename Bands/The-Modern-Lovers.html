The Modern Lovers

Boston-area proto-punk band, formed 1970, effectively active 1970–1974. Led by Jonathan Richman. Discogs artist ID: 252664.

Overview

Formed by Jonathan Richman in Boston around 1970. Key early lineup included Jerry Harrison (later of Talking Heads) and Ernie Brooks (later of The Necessaries). Recorded influential demos in 1972 with John Cale producing. Those recordings were eventually released as their debut album in 1976 (Beserkley). Influential on the Velvet Underground lineage and proto-punk; “Roadrunner” and “Someone I Care About” are signature tracks.

Ernie Brooks — The Cale Sessions and Dissolution (Web Sources)

Two published interviews capture Ernie Brooks’s account of the 1972–73 John Cale sessions and the band’s 1974 collapse:

Please Kill Me, October 2017 (interview by Legs McNeil): https://pleasekillme.com/modern-lovers-bassist-ernie-brooks/

Brooks describes the 1973 Warner Bros. sessions as increasingly dysfunctional. During work on “Someone I Care About,” John Cale reportedly pushed Jonathan Richman: “You gotta sound mean; you gotta sound like you wanna kill somebody!” — but Richman replied, “I don’t want to hurt anybody — I wanna make a nice, happy-sounding record.” Brooks frames this as the central collision: Cale wanted “angst and violence in the sound” consistent with the band’s live set, but Richman was already shifting away from loud, electric rock. Brooks notes Cale was “drinking a lot” during this period and reads his frustration sympathetically: “it must have been a terrible thing for Cale, because he was the producer of this potentially great record he wanted to make — that Jonathan wouldn’t let him make.”

Brooks recounts trying to negotiate with Richman directly: “You can do whatever you want afterwards. Let’s just make this record.” Richman refused, on grounds that quiet music with “magic in it” would be heard regardless of volume. When Warner Bros. asked for live performances of the recorded material, Richman told a label executive: “We’ll play the songs for the record, but we won’t do any of these songs when we play live” — the position that ended the label relationship. Brooks is measured rather than bitter: “I never said, ‘Fuck you, Jonathan!’ I never said, ‘I’m not talking to you…’” and “he was way ahead of us, but we couldn’t follow him.” He also recalls that after “Roadrunner” drew strong audience reaction, Richman declared: “People like that song too much; I don’t think we should do it anymore.”

FFanzeen No. 7, 1981 (interview by John Morace; archived online): https://ffanzeen.blogspot.com/2010/04/ernie-brooks-lover-looks-back-at-roots.html

An earlier account, published roughly seven years after the dissolution, covering similar ground with a slightly different emphasis. On Cale: “he was an A&R man, and a producer for Warner’s. But that didn’t last long.” On Richman’s acoustic turn: “Jonathan wanted to go in a more acoustic direction. He started thinking that electricity was too weird” and “He didn’t want any guitar. He didn’t want anything besides voice because that was the thing that was — real.” The other band members’ position: “we wanted to keep it a real rock’n’roll band.” On the practical pressures from the label: “When you have to deal with the record company…you really have to be together or you can be destroyed very quickly.”

John Cale’s account — framing the collapse:

Three sources carry Cale’s own words, though the fullest is still behind a paywall.

The Uncut interview (URL: https://www.uncut.co.uk/features/john-cale-on-jonathan-richman-he-created-his-own-special-reality-127473/) remains inaccessible (403 on two attempts, dates unknown). Its headline and search-snippet material yield: “There was very little that was orthodox about Jonathan. Like his views on life, his views on music and art were much more from a childlike and dream-filled perspective, which allowed him to create his own special reality.” This is appreciative rather than caustic in tone — Cale is framing Richman’s worldview as coherent on its own terms, not as a catastrophic failure.

In a Stereogum career-spanning interview (https://stereogum.com/2268312/john-cale-career-spanning-interview/interviews/weve-got-a-file-on-you), Cale says of Richman and the band: “Jonathan is very child-like! The Modern Lovers had a whimsical approach to playing rock music. It was refreshing to be around. He had an infectious curiosity.” No mention of the sessions collapsing; the register is fond retrospection.

A third statement, sourced in The Point Magazine essay “No Going Back: Jonathan Richman, modern lover” (https://thepointmag.com/criticism/no-going-back-jonathan-richman-modern-lover/) and attributed there to “an interview several years after their sessions together” (publication not named in the Point piece), carries a sharper register: “They were not aggressive. They were weak. There was a definitive weakness about the music. This weakness kept on developing and developing until it was still just a weakness.” This is the only Cale quote that directly addresses the musical direction as a failure, and it is the one that aligns most closely with the “nothing usable” characterisation.

Comparison of framings — Cale vs. Brooks:

The two accounts are compatible but differently weighted. Cale (at least in the “weakness” quote) frames Richman’s evolution as deterioration — the music losing its aggression and becoming technically inadequate for the record he was trying to make. Brooks frames exactly the same events as divergence: Richman moving toward something the others couldn’t follow, with Brooks explicitly saying “he was way ahead of us.” Cale’s “weakness” language implies the change was a loss of quality; Brooks’s language implies a change of direction that left the band behind. Both agree on the proximate cause (Richman refusing to record the band’s electric sound), but Cale’s framing is more evaluative (“weak”) while Brooks’s is more structural (“he wanted something different; we couldn’t give it to him”). The Uncut headline quote — “He created his own special reality” — sits closer to the Brooks register than the “weakness” register, suggesting Cale may have softened his account in later years.

Note: the Point Magazine essay does not cite the specific Cale interview by name. The quote may originate in an early 1970s or late 1970s print interview; the Uncut piece (date unknown) is the most likely candidate for its more recent iteration.

1976 Beserkley Release — Authorization and Member Involvement (Web Sources)

The question of whether Harrison, Brooks, Robinson, and Felice were consulted about or approved the 1976 Beserkley debut release was searched across multiple sources (Oct 2026); no published interview or historical account directly addresses it. The following is what can be established:

How Beserkley obtained the material. Matthew King Kaufman was an ex-A&M promoter who had helped organize the 1972 A&M demo sessions before founding Beserkley Records in 1973. He acquired the rights to the 1972 recordings from the labels (A&M and Warner Bros.) — Wikipedia’s Matthew King Kaufman article states he was “acquiring the rights to the 1972 A&M Modern Lovers recordings, which were issued as an album in 1976.” (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_King_Kaufman, as of 2026-10-17.) A secondary source (mmone.org/modern-lovers, mmone.org/modern-lovers) states “Beserkley subsequently licensed Cale’s tapes.” The authorization was therefore a label-to-label (or label-to-Kaufman) rights transaction, not a band-member approval process. Individual member consent was not a legal requirement — the recordings belonged to Warner Bros. and A&M.

Jerry Harrison’s “Hospital” tape credit. The track “Hospital” (a 1971 Intermedia Sound session, predating the Cale/Fowley sessions) was credited on the LP as “donated by Jerry Harrison,” indicating Harrison at minimum cooperated with Beserkley’s release for this one pre-label-era recording he personally held. The Tape Op interview (tapeop.com/interviews/153/jerry-harrison) and the Ultimate Classic Rock profile (ultimateclassicrock.com/jerry-harrison-joining-talking-heads/) do not contain any statement from Harrison about donating the tapes or being consulted about the overall album release. See dedicated subsection below for Oct 2026 research pass.

Ernie Brooks, David Robinson, John Felice — no record found. The two published Ernie Brooks interviews (Please Kill Me 2017, FFanzeen 1981) cover the band’s formation through dissolution but neither mentions the 1976 Beserkley release, the tapes being issued, or whether Brooks was consulted. No interview with David Robinson or John Felice discussing the 1976 release was located across 11 searches and fetches.

Jonathan Richman’s position. Richman explicitly did not consider the Beserkley demo compilation his debut — he preferred Jonathan Richman and the Modern Lovers (released three months earlier). Wikipedia notes this. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Modern_Lovers_(album), as of 2026-10-17.) This suggests Richman had at most limited enthusiasm for Kaufman’s compilation, but it does not speak to whether he was formally consulted.

Structural conclusion. The release appears to have been Kaufman’s initiative, executed through label-rights acquisition rather than band-member authorization. Harrison’s tape donation suggests some cooperation on his part. Whether Brooks, Robinson, Felice, or Richman were informed in advance or gave explicit approval is not documented in any accessible source. The absence of reported legal challenges or public complaints from band members is circumstantially consistent with either acquiescence or simply no leverage — since the recordings were label property, members had no veto.

Jerry Harrison — “Hospital” Tape Donation (Web Sources, Oct 2026)

What the LP establishes. The 1976 Beserkley debut LP credits the track “Hospital” as “donated by Jerry Harrison.” Wikipedia’s article on the album confirms the factual basis: Harrison “possessed the original 1971 session tapes” from the Intermedia Sound (Boston) sessions arranged by Stuart Love of Warner Bros. Records. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Modern_Lovers_(album), as of 2026-10-17.) The credit implies Harrison actively provided this tape to Kaufman; the remaining tracks came from the label-to-label rights acquisition (A&M / Warner Bros. to Beserkley).

Why the Intermedia tape was not acquired via the label transaction. The Wikipedia article on the album indicates Warner Bros. did not hold the Intermedia tapes in their library — Harrison’s copy was therefore the only accessible source. This structural fact explains why a separate “donated by” credit was necessary for this track alone, but it is a reconstruction from the LP’s internal evidence, not a statement Harrison has made.

Sources checked for Harrison’s first-person account (Oct 2026).

  • WTF with Marc Maron ep. 1351 (July 25, 2022): A transcript is available at podscripts.co (podscripts.co/podcasts/wtf-with-marc-maron-podcast/episode-1351-jerry-harrison). Harrison mentions Kaufman in the context of the Beserkley release — “they sold it to Beserkley, this guy Matthew Kaufman” — but the transcript contains no mention of “Hospital,” the tape donation, why or when he gave the tape, or whether he was consulted about the full album release. The episode focuses on band formation, the Cale sessions, Talking Heads origins, and Harrison–Richman friendship. Transcript searched in full; no relevant passage found.

  • Tape Op No. 153 (“Mindfulness in the Recording Studio”): (tapeop.com/interviews/153/jerry-harrison) Mentions the Intermedia Sound context — “That’s where the recording of ‘Hospital’ took place” — and notes that Allan Mason and Kaufman were associates, but contains no statement from Harrison about donating the tape, his motivations, timing, or consultation about the album release.

  • Bob Lefsetz Podcast (Harrison episode): (iheart.com/podcast/1119-the-bob-lefsetz-podcast-30806836/episode/jerry-harrison-107860584/) No mention of Hospital tape, Beserkley album, or tape donation.

  • Ultimate Classic Rock, Broadway World 2025, Glide Magazine: All three checked in prior research passes; none contain a statement by Harrison about the tape donation.

Conclusion. No accessible interview contains Harrison’s first-person account of why or when he gave the “Hospital” tape to Kaufman, or whether he was asked about the full album release. The LP credit is the only primary evidence. The structural reason the tape was in Harrison’s hands (Warner Bros. did not retain it) is inferable but not documented in Harrison’s own words in any accessible source as of Oct 2026.

Jerry Harrison — Band or Richman’s Project? (Web Sources)

Multiple interviews yield a consistent picture: Harrison regarded the Modern Lovers as a genuine band — one he was proud of and whose dissolution hurt him — but his language consistently places Richman as the sole creative centre. The distinction is between a band he joined and believed in and a band where creative parity existed; Harrison’s accounts support the former, not the latter.

Formation framing. In a Tape Op interview (tapeop.com/interviews/153/jerry-harrison), Harrison describes the band’s origin as accidental and socially driven: “Jonathan came into my apartment with this group of people, raving about the Velvet Underground and The Stooges” — he was making a documentary film and wanted Richman to do the soundtrack, then “Suddenly, both Ernie and I ended up joining the band.” The phrasing “ended up joining” positions it as Richman’s pre-existing vehicle that absorbed Harrison and Ernie Brooks, not a collective they co-founded.

Creative ownership. In a Glide Magazine interview (glidemagazine.com/280543/jerry-harrison-talks-talking-heads-history-making-performing-of-remain-in-light-interview/), Harrison says: “I first of all was trying to learn how to play some parts that Jonathan had played and he had like moved on to different parts.” This implies Richman pre-wrote the keyboard and guitar parts that Harrison was then tasked with executing — consistent with a songwriter-centric operation rather than collective arrangement.

Richman as the direction-setter. On the Cale sessions and dissolution, Tape Op again: “It was frustrating, because we were still trying to record what would be the first record. Kim Fowley produced some tracks after John Cale got so frustrated — Jonathan’s world philosophy had changed.” The pivot is entirely attributed to Richman’s philosophical evolution; Harrison frames himself and the others as having to react to it.

The band’s collective pride. At the same time, Harrison consistently uses “we” when describing the band’s output and significance. In a Broadway World interview from April 2025 (broadwayworld.com/columbus/article/Interview-Jerry-Harrison-of-STOP-MAKING-SENSE-at-Southern-Theatre-20250422), the dissolution is called devastating — “people considered us to be a unique, important band” — and in a search-result snippet (attributed to an unconfirmed broader interview): “One of the biggest things I’ve had to overcome was the disappointment when the Modern Lovers fell out.” In the Glide piece: “Certainly, The Modern Lovers, when we came out and made the record in 1972, stood as sort of an alternative to Emerson Lake & Palmer and Yes.” He calls it a band they made together; Richman is designated “front man” in press coverage Harrison doesn’t contest.

Synthesis. Harrison’s retrospective framing is not “I was hired help for Jonathan’s solo project.” He treats 1971–74 as a real band era — a distinct entity he belonged to — and is clearly proud of it. But he does not claim co-authorship of the artistic vision: Richman showed him a way of hearing music; the parts were Richman’s; the philosophy that ended the band was Richman’s. The Modern Lovers was a band in the sociological sense (a tight unit with shared commitment) but effectively Richman’s project in the creative sense. This is structurally consistent with Ernie Brooks’s account: Brooks also uses “we” and mourns the collapse, yet describes Richman as the immovable will the others organized themselves around.

Note on source gaps. The WTF with Marc Maron podcast episode 1351 (July 2022) is confirmed to discuss Harrison’s Modern Lovers years and his friendship with Richman, but no full transcript was accessible at time of research; the episode audio may contain more explicit characterization than the sources above. Broadway World 2025 interview is the most direct on the “band” label; Tape Op is the most detailed on the creative dynamic.
