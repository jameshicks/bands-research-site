ESG

Art-funk / dance-punk / no wave. South Bronx, New York City. 1978–present (intermittent).

ESG = Emerald Sapphire & Gold. Discogs ID: 16534. Name variations: E.S.G., E⋆S⋆G, ESG II.

Overview

An all-family ensemble from the South Bronx built around the Scroggins sisters — Renee (lead vocals, guitar), Valerie (drums), Marie (congas), and Deborah (bass) — with friend Tito Libran on congas. Their music is centered on interlocking polyrhythms, skeletal bass lines, and the sisters’ collective vocal arrangements, with Renee’s lead vocals hovering between chant and funk. Barely any guitar on the early records; the percussion and bass carry everything.

Discovered when Ed Bahlman of 99 Records encountered them at a talent show at the Music Building (West 30th Street, Manhattan), where he was serving as a judge. Their mother Helen had been entering the sisters into talent competitions to develop their musicianship. ESG did not win the contest, but Bahlman approached them anyway; Renee Scroggins has recalled: “He called me. I said, ‘Oh, my God, we won the contest!’ And he said, ‘Well, no…’ But he became our unofficial manager.” [source: staticmag.org; corroborated by Fordham BAAHP oral history of Renee Scroggins, research.library.fordham.edu/baahp_oralhist/226/] Their first paying gig after this was at Mechanics’ Hall for an event called “Popfront.” Their debut EP (1981) was produced by Martin Hannett for the studio tracks — one of his only US sessions — and by Bahlman for the live side. Factory Records released it in the UK as FAC 34; Tony Wilson had seen them open for A Certain Ratio and brought them to Manchester.

Their music was subsequently sampled by Public Enemy, Wu-Tang Clan, Beastie Boys, TLC, Big Daddy Kane, and many others. The 1992 EP Sample Credits Don’t Pay Our Bills addressed this directly. By the 2000s, Soul Jazz Records reissued and documented their catalogue, and the next generation of the family — Renee’s daughters Chistelle and Nicole — joined the band.

Formation

The Scroggins sisters grew up in the South Bronx (Renee born in the Moore Projects, Jackson Avenue and 149th Street). Their mother Helen Scroggins, concerned about neighbourhood deterioration and street danger, provided instruments as a Christmas gift and encouraged the sisters toward music after watching the Don Kershner rock concert and PBS’s “Soul” program. She also held weekly home music reviews and entered the sisters into talent competitions. [source: Fordham BAAHP oral history of Renee Scroggins, research.library.fordham.edu/baahp_oralhist/226/] Renee played guitar; Valerie took up drums; Marie and Deborah followed. Renee had studied violin in the orchestra at Junior High School 145. The sisters incorporated Latin rhythmic influences from musicians they heard in St. Mary’s Park. Friend Tito Libran added congas.

Split 12” with Level 42 (1981)

Moody / Starchild (Rated “X” Records, 1981) — a split 12” pairing ESG’s “Moody” with Level 42’s “Starchild.” An unexpected pairing of South Bronx percussion-funk with UK jazz-funk; both tracks had been circulating in dance music contexts.

Notes (web, 2026-10-15): The release was a DJ-format promo pressing rather than an artistic collaboration between the two bands. ESG’s side is a re-edit of “Moody” by Danny Krivit (the Manhattan DJ known for gray-market re-edits used as DJ tools from the early 1980s). The Level 42 side carries “a new edit” of “Starchild” — described in retailer notes as a “Paradise Garage favourite,” indicating both tracks were paired because they circulated in the same New York club ecosystem. [source: phonicarecords.com product listing, https://www.phonicarecords.com/index.php/product/esg-level-42-moody-starchild-rated-x-records/32869]

The label Rated “X” Records is not identified on any retrievable source: no founder, owner, or affiliation documented online. The release appears consistent with the pattern of gray-market DJ-edit 12”s pressed in New York in 1981–82 for club and DJ use, not licensed commercial singles — the format Danny Krivit’s early re-edits typically took. No interview or contemporary article found that documents who initiated the release or why these two tracks specifically were paired. Searched: Phonica listing, Danny Krivit official site (releases, bio, 500 Edits article), Red Bull Music Academy interview, Wikipedia “Starchild (song),” clone.nl listing (403); no source names a commissioning party or explains the label’s origins.

Sample Credits Don’t Pay Our Bills! (Nega Fulô Records / Nuphonic, 1992)

12” EP responding directly to the widespread uncleared use of their recordings. The title is the statement. Tracks by TLC, Wu-Tang Clan, Public Enemy, and many others drew from ESG recordings without payment; this release documented and protested that. Released on Nega Fulô Records (NEGA 789) / Nuphonic (NUX 104). Discogs release ID: 326681.

Sampling Legacy

ESG’s “UFO,” “Moody,” and related tracks became among the most-sampled records in hip-hop. Documented users include Public Enemy, Wu-Tang Clan, Beastie Boys, TLC, Big Daddy Kane, and indie acts. The 1992 EP Sample Credits Don’t Pay Our Bills was their explicit response. This is distinct from Liquid Liquid’s legal action against Sugar Hill Records — ESG’s situation involved widespread uncleared sampling with no resulting lawsuit.

I said, ‘Oh, my God, we won the contest!’ And he said, ‘Well, no…’ But he became our unofficial manager.” [source: https://staticmag.org/lizzy-jack/esg-interview/]

The Encyclopedia.com article and Fordham BAAHP summary both note the first paying gig that followed was at Mechanics’ Hall (also “Mechanic Hall”), for an event called “Popfront”, opening for “Bound and Gagged.” [sources: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/esg; https://research.library.fordham.edu/baahp_oralhist/226/]

Conflict flagged: Current Overview paragraph said “South Bronx community center” — updated to “Music Building (West 30th Street, Manhattan)” based on the BAAHP oral history. The BAAHP page appears to be a summary rather than a verbatim transcript; the BAAHP entry describes the talent show setting but does not name the year. Multiple other sources consistently cite 1979 for the first gig at Mechanics’ Hall, suggesting the discovery was late 1978 or 1979.
