Rites Of Spring

Discogs ID: 286239 Active: 1984–1986 (dissolved January 1987) Origin: Washington, DC, USA Label: Dischord Records Name variation: R.O.S.

DC post-hardcore band widely cited as a founding influence on emo. Active for roughly two years and only a dozen or so live performances, they released one LP and one 7” EP before dissolving in early 1987. Their combination of hardcore intensity with emotionally confessional, poetic lyrics — delivered by Guy Picciotto with physical abandon — generated the “emotional hardcore” tag that was later shortened to “emo.”

Formation

Rites Of Spring grew out of Insurrection, a DC hardcore band that featured Brendan Canty (drums), Mike Fellows (bass), and Guy Picciotto (guitar). After Insurrection dissolved, the three continued playing together and guitarist Eddie Janney joined; Picciotto took on vocal duties as well. The band formed in 1984 and quickly became part of what would be called “Revolution Summer” — a loose 1985 DC scene shift away from macho hardcore toward more emotionally open, melodically adventurous post-punk. Other bands of this moment included Embrace (2), Dag Nasty, Beefeater (2), and Soulside.

Despite the influence they accrued, the band played live rarely — approximately a dozen shows across their entire existence — and was known for the intensity of those performances, which reportedly moved audience members to tears.

Dissolution and Aftermath

Mike Fellows departed in January 1987, which ended Rites Of Spring. The remaining three members — Picciotto, Janney, and Canty — immediately formed One Last Wish with Michael Hampton (3) (who had produced the LP). One Last Wish recorded a full LP in 1986 but played only a handful of shows before dissolving; the recordings were finally released as 1986 (1999, Dischord / Peterbilt Records). Janney moved to bass in that band.

Following One Last Wish, Picciotto, Janney, Fellows, and Canty regrouped as Happy Go Licky (1987–1988), pursuing a noisier, less structured sound distinct from Rites Of Spring. Happy Go Licky released a self-titled record on Peterbilt Records (1988).

By the time Happy Go Licky wound down, Ian MacKaye had invited Picciotto to join the band that would become Fugazi (formed 1987). Picciotto and Canty became two of Fugazi’s four permanent members for the band’s entire run (1987–2003).

Post-Rites trajectories

Guy Picciotto — Fugazi (1987–2003), also producer/engineer work (PJ Harvey, Blonde Redhead). Runs Peterbilt Records.

Brendan Canty — Fugazi (1987–2003), The Messthetics (with Joe Lally, 2017–present), Deathfix (2013).

Eddie Janney — One Last Wish (bass), Happy Go Licky, then smaller DC bands including Skewbald / Grand Union and Rozzlyn Rangers. Also produced Embrace (2)‘s LP (1987) and Shudder To Think’s early work. His woodcut artwork appears on both the LP and End On End CD.

Mike Fellows — Happy Go Licky (1988), then shifted away from the DC scene. Session and session-adjacent work with Silver Jews (American Water, 1998; Bright Flight, 2001; Ease Down The Road, 2001; Tennessee, 2001). Released solo material as Miighty Flashlight (MIIGHTΨ ΦLAΣHLIGHT, 2002, Jade Tree). Later recording engineer and mixer credits (No Quarter releases, Half A Million).

Recording Infrastructure

All studio recordings were made at Inner Ear Studios in Arlington, VA, engineered by Don Zientara. This was standard for Dischord bands of the period — Zientara and Inner Ear were the house engineer/studio for virtually the entire Dischord catalog. Ian MacKaye (of Minor Threat and, later, Fugazi) produced the LP alongside Michael Hampton (3); the EP was co-produced by MacKaye and the band.

Influence and Context

Rites Of Spring are typically named as the first or primary influence on the genre that became “emo” (originally “emotional hardcore”), a term applied retroactively. Contemporaneous DC bands often grouped with them in this framing include Embrace (2) (MacKaye’s post-Minor Threat band), Gray Matter, and later Jawbreaker and Sunny Day Real Estate.

Critically, the “Revolution Summer” framing — articulated loosely among DC hardcore participants in 1985 — was a conscious attempt to move past the perceived limitations and macho posturing of first-wave hardcore. Rites Of Spring, alongside Embrace and other Dischord-adjacent acts, embodied this shift.

Fugazi, the band that directly succeeded these players, extended the sonic and ethical approach for another 16 years and remains one of the most influential post-hardcore acts.
