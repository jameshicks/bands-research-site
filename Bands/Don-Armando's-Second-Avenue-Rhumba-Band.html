Don Armando’s Second Avenue Rhumba Band

Latin-inflected disco act from New York, active 1979–1980. A spin-off of Dr. Buzzard’s Original Savannah Band, built around the band’s percussionist Don Armando Bonilla and produced by Andy Hernandez (Coati Mundi), with executive production by August Darnell and Michael Zilkha of ZE Records. Their 1979 debut single “Deputy of Love” reportedly reached number one on the US disco charts and remains one of ZE Records’ most celebrated early releases. The band released one studio album and two singles before dissolving.

Overview

Don Armando’s Second Avenue Rhumba Band was the brainchild of Don Armando Bonilla Jr. (Discogs ID: 426318), percussionist and vocalist in Dr. Buzzard’s Original Savannah Band. When the Savannah Band wound down at the end of the 1970s, Bonilla assembled the Rhumba Band as a vehicle for his own Latin-tinged disco vision.

The project was embedded in the same creative circle that would become Kid Creole And The Coconuts: Andy Hernandez produced and arranged the LP while also playing vibraphone in Dr. Buzzard’s; August Darnell served as executive producer and would shortly launch Kid Creole; and Fonda Rae — who became a featured vocalist here — went on to sing on the first Kid Creole album Off The Coast Of Me (1980). The 12” single “I’m An Indian, Too / Deputy of Love” was released on both ZE Records and Buddah Records (cat. ZEA 12-003), giving it broader distribution than typical ZE releases of the period. The album followed on ZE (ZEA33-005).

The only original material after the album was the 1980 7” single “Winter Love / How To Handle A Woman” (ZEA 45-305), produced by Andy Hernandez. There were no further releases under this name. Discogs records 26 pressings of the debut 12” single and 8 pressings of the LP across various international territories, indicating ongoing demand through the 1980s and beyond. A Russian “Deputy of Love” pressing appeared in 2005.

ZE Records Context

Don Armando’s Second Avenue Rhumba Band was among the earliest ZE Records releases, sitting in the label’s foundational 1979 batch alongside The Contortions, James White & The Blacks, Aural Exciters, and Lizzy Mercier Descloux. ZE at this point was distributed via Buddah Records in the US (the label “ZEA 12-003” and Buddah share catalog space on the debut 12”). The Latin-Caribbean inflection of the Don Armando record directly prefigured what would become the Kid Creole And The Coconuts sound.
