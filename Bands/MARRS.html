Note: filename uses MARRS due to filesystem constraints; canonical name is M|A|R|R|S.

One-off collaboration between Colourbox and A.R. Kane, plus CJ Mackintosh and Dave Dorrell. The name is an acronym of the five core members: Martyn Young (Colourbox), Alex Ayuli (A.R. Kane), Rudy Tambala (A.R. Kane), Russell Smith (A.R. Kane-adjacent bassist), Steven Young (Colourbox).

“Pump Up the Volume” (AD 707 / BAD 707, 1987) reached UK #1. One of the earliest UK chart hits built around sample collage. The Colourbox Discogs profile notes that “litigation surrounding the samples led to the end of the band.”

The Sample Dispute

“Pump Up the Volume” was built from approximately 26 sampled records — the entire A-side was a collage of drum breaks, vocal snippets, and musical fragments assembled by John Fryer on a Studer 24-track at Blackwing Studios. This was 1987, before any framework for sample clearance had been established in the UK.

The Stock Aitken Waterman injunction

When the single reached #2 on the UK charts, Pete Waterman (of Stock Aitken Waterman) placed an injunction against the record over an unlicensed sample from their track “Roadblock.” The disputed element was described as seven seconds of an anonymous background voice saying a single word — no melody, no instrumentation. Waterman wrote an open letter to the music press calling such sampling “wholesale theft.” Distribution halted for five days while negotiations took place.

Resolution: the SAW sample was stripped from all overseas pressings. The version containing it had already shipped to some territories (Australia notably received it before the injunction hit). The record resumed distribution and reached #1 for two weeks in October 1987.

The cascade

John Fryer said afterward: “[SAW] decided to sue, and at that point a number of American artists thought, ‘Well, if they’re suing, we’re going to sue as well.‘” James Brown’s lawyers had reportedly been waiting specifically for the US release. To preempt further action, the US version (licensed to 4th & Broadway) was substantially re-edited: the James Brown “watch me” sample and several other restricted vocal snippets were removed and replaced with George Kranz’s “Din Daa Daa” and “Oh Yeah” by Choice MCs, among other alternative sources.

The fallout

The litigation compounded pre-existing tensions. Colourbox and A.R. Kane had recorded in entirely separate sessions, exchanging tracks remotely rather than working together — the collaboration was already fraught. A.R. Kane later said in interviews that their contributions to the A-side had been “all but removed” from the final mix. The legal disputes and the bitterness over credits “really screwed up any remnants of friendship between 4AD and A.R. Kane.” Colourbox never recorded again. MARRS was a one-off in name and in fact.

Industry context

The irony noted at the time: SAW’s own Rick Astley record was simultaneously using the Colonel Abrams bassline without clearance. The MARRS episode — a landmark hit derailed by litigation within weeks of release — became one of the early cases that accelerated the music industry’s push toward formalized sample clearance practices. “Where there’s a hit — there’s a writ” became an industry adage.

Open Questions

  • [~] Whether SAW dispute was formally settled or simply resolved by edit/removal — Partially narrowed; not fully resolved. Every named authoritative source (Sound on Sound “Classic Tracks” [soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-marrs-pump-volume], Wikipedia “Pump Up the Volume” article [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pump_Up_the_Volume_(song), as of 2026-06-27], en-academic mirror of the PUTV Wikipedia article) describes the same resolution: five days of negotiations resulted in an undertaking that overseas releases would not include the “Roadblock” sample. None of these sources mention a financial payment, royalty transfer, or signed settlement agreement. Two claims appear in secondary/tertiary sources without primary-source citation: (a) that Waterman said it was “a matter of principle rather than profit” and “promised to donate all royalties from the court case to charity” — present in the en-academic Stock Aitken Waterman article (itself a Wikipedia mirror) and search aggregator summaries, but not in the Wikipedia “Pump Up the Volume” article and without an attributed interview or press clipping; (b) that “SAW won, taking all the royalties from the hit single” and M|A|R|R|S “reportedly ended up without a penny” — this appears only in the uncited personal blog Songs to the Siren (songstothesiren.com/song/PumpUpTheVolume.html) and cannot be corroborated. Working conclusion: The documented resolution is sample removal from overseas pressings following five days of negotiation; the injunction was lifted on that basis. Whether any money changed hands — and whether Waterman’s reported “charity” pledge reflected actual payment terms — is unconfirmed by primary sources. Pete Waterman’s memoir I Wish I Was Me (2000) has not been consulted; it is the most likely primary source for his own account of the terms. Ivo Watts-Russell or Martyn Young interviews would be the other avenue.
