Management

Anya Phillips (February 1955, Taipei County, Taiwan – 19 June 1981): Met James Chance at an X Magazine benefit in March 1978; became his girlfriend and manager. Co-founded the Mudd Club (1978) with Steve Maas and Diego Cortez. Her brother is the Chinese pop singer Fei Xiang. Designed clothing without formal training — Debbie Harry wore Phillips-designed pieces on Blondie’s Plastic Letters (1978). Diagnosed with cancer 1979; Chance organised a benefit at Bond’s (May 1981) featuring Debbie Harry, Nile Rodgers, Bernard Edwards, Kid Creole and the Coconuts, Fab 5 Freddy. She died of brain cancer on 19 June 1981, aged 26. The New York Times ran a belated obituary only in November 2020.

History

James Chance’s Background

Born James Siegfried, 20 April 1953, Milwaukee, Wisconsin. Grew up in Brookfield, WI. Began on piano (taught by nuns at Catholic elementary school); took up alto saxophone at 18. Attended Michigan State University briefly; enrolled at the Wisconsin Conservatory of Music but found it too conservative and dropped out. Moved to NYC in late 1975/early 1976 following the dissolution of his Milwaukee band and the death of its singer.

NYC arrival: Studied briefly under free jazz saxophonist David Murray (the direct link to the loft jazz movement). First band: Flaming Youth (1976, instrumental quartet). Briefly co-founded Teenage Jesus and the Jerks with Lydia Lunch in 1976 — they met at CBGB, he moved into her apartment, played saxophone on early TJTJ recordings.

Formation (Late 1977)

First documented show: 4 December 1977. Chance recruited players including Jody Harris and George Scott III from the downtown scene, Pat Place (who had been playing guitar for approximately three weeks), and Adele Bertei (from Cleveland, who had never played keyboards — Chance had her treat the organ rhythmically, not melodically).

The music was a deliberately unstable synthesis: James Brown’s funk discipline as the rhythmic chassis, Ornette Coleman’s harmolodic freedom as the saxophone philosophy, punk’s nihilism as the attitude.

No New York and the Artists Space Festival (Spring 1978)

Early 1978: Artists Space gallery hosted a five-night underground festival organised by Michael Zwack and Robert Longo. Brian Eno was already in NYC to produce Talking Heads’ More Songs About Buildings and Food; he attended the festival (Diego Cortez brokered the connection; Adele Bertei, working as Eno’s assistant, claims she took him herself). Eno proposed documenting the scene.

Recorded: June 1978, Big Apple Studio, NYC. Each band given one night; no overdubs, entirely live, minimal production. Released on Antilles Records (AN-7067, 1978). The Contortions occupy Side A.

Contortions tracks:

  1. “Dish It Out” (3:17)
  2. “Flip Your Face” (3:13)
  3. “Jaded” (3:49)
  4. “I Can’t Stand Myself” (4:52) — James Brown

Pat Place’s Departure (Spring 1978)

After the Paris gig — show money spent on drugs rather than distributed to the band. Place left in solidarity. Chance later acknowledged the period was dominated by heroin. She went on to found Bush Tetras.

ZE Records and Buy / Off White (1979)

Michael Zilkha (ZE Records co-founder) offered $10,000 to record a disco/funk album with creative latitude. This produced two albums released almost simultaneously, under different names but with largely the same personnel:

  • Buy (as the Contortions, ZE Records, 1979) — the “art” record; hard no wave funk
  • Off White (as James White and the Blacks, ZE-Buddah, 1979) — more overtly disco-funk; features Lydia Lunch as “Stella Rico”

Dissolution (Autumn 1979)

Internal tensions — financial disputes, drug problems, Phillips’s management pressures — led to the original band breaking up in autumn 1979.

Joseph Bowie (trombone, in the Blacks lineup) took the Black musicians from “the Blacks” and formed Defunkt (debuted 1979) — an influential jazz-punk-funk hybrid.

Deaths

  • George Scott III: 5 August 1980, heroin overdose, aged 26. His death also ended 8-Eyed Spy (Lydia Lunch’s group).
  • Anya Phillips: 19 June 1981, brain cancer, aged 26.
  • James Chance: 18 June 2024, gastrointestinal disease, aged 71.

Live Performance Style

Chance deliberately confronted passive audiences — frustrated by the detached spectatorship he associated with both jazz audiences and downtown art crowds, he walked into the audience to attack people physically: punching, slapping, kicking. His most notorious victim was rock critic Robert Christgau, whom he punched during a performance.

The logic was perversely consistent: James Brown had commanded audiences to respond, to dance, to give back energy. Chance’s violence was a demand for participation by other means — if the audience wouldn’t move, he’d make them. Over time, as audiences came to expect it, the tactic lost its shock value.

John Lurie (Lounge Lizards): “He’d get into a fight with people in the audience, and you’d have to go defend him.”

Legacy

The Contortions established the template — punk aggression + funk rhythms + free jazz dissonance — that directly influenced:

  • ESG, Liquid Liquid (immediate NYC peers — the dance-punk axis)
  • Big Boys (cited Buy as inspiration)
  • Defunkt (grew directly out of the Blacks lineup)
  • Gang of Four (parallel development in Leeds; Hugo Burnham noted the sonic affinity with Bush Tetras)
  • The Rapture, LCD Soundsystem, Liars, Yeah Yeah Yeahs (2000s NYC dance-punk revival — all drawing on the Contortions/ESG template)

James Murphy (LCD Soundsystem) has cited the no wave era repeatedly as foundational.
