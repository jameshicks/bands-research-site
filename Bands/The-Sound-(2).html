The Sound (2)

Post-punk band from South London, UK. Discogs ID: 171148. Active 1979–1988.

Overview

The Sound were one of the most committed and underappreciated bands of the British post-punk era. Formed in South London in 1979 around the songwriting of Adrian Borland, the band produced a run of albums from 1980 to 1987 that tracked a consistent and deeply personal vision: expansive guitar textures, searching introspection, and emotional directness that separated them from both the austerity of their post-punk peers and the glossier new wave mainstream. Despite earning consistent critical respect in the UK press and an enduring cult following — particularly in the Netherlands, where they regularly toured and recorded — they never achieved significant commercial success. Their work has been extensively reissued since the 1990s and their reputation has grown substantially in retrospect.

The band’s core identity rested almost entirely on Borland, who wrote the vast majority of their material and drove the band’s creative direction throughout their existence. His death in April 1999 — a suicide, four years after the Sound had already dissolved — drew renewed attention to the body of work he had created.

Formation and Early Years (1979–1980)

Adrian Borland (born Adrian Kelvin Borland) had been active in the South London music scene before The Sound, playing in The Outsiders (2) and The Witch Trials in the late 1970s. The Sound formed in 1979 with Borland on vocals and guitar, Bi Marshall (born Benita Biltoo) on keyboards, Graham Bailey (also known as Graham Green) on bass, and Michael Dudley on drums.

Their debut release was the Physical World E.P. (1979) on the small South London imprint Tortch Records, a three-track 7” EP (cat. tor 003) featuring “Cold Beat,” “Physical World,” and “Unwritten Law.” All writing was credited to Borland. The limited run established the band’s sound: guitar-forward post-punk with melodic intensity.

Also in 1979, Borland and Graham Bailey released material as Second Layer, a more experimentally angular project, also on Tortch (and briefly Fresh Records (14)). Second Layer released three records between 1979 and 1981, including the album World Of Rubber (1981, Cherry Red).

Korova Period (1980–1983)

The Sound signed to Korova, the Warner-affiliated UK imprint that also released Echo & The Bunnymen and The Teardrop Explodes. Their debut full-length, Jeopardy (1980), was co-produced by the band and Nick Robbins and released through WEA for the Benelux territory (cat. WEA 58255). The album’s lineup featured Borland, Bi Marshall (keyboards), Graham Green (bass — the same person as Graham Bailey), and Michael Dudley (drums). A session contributor, Adrian Janes, also received co-writing credits on two tracks — specifically the lyrics to “Words Fail Me” and “Night Versus Day.” Janes had been a member of The Outsiders (2) alongside Borland and Bob Lawrence, and though he was not part of The Sound, occasionally co-wrote for the new band.

The album’s tracklist included what became one of the band’s signature songs, “I Can’t Escape Myself,” as well as “Heartland,” “Jeopardy,” “Resistance,” and “Unwritten Law” — tracks that laid out the anxious, searching quality that would define their work. The production was spare and live-sounding, with Robbins and the band splitting production credit.

The Heyday single (1980, Korova KOW 10) preceded the album’s UK release. Produced by Nick Robbins and the band, it was another early statement of their post-punk melodic approach.

Bi Marshall left the band in December 1980, fired after an argument with Borland in the wake of a major UK tour supporting Echo & The Bunnymen. Her replacement was Colvin Mayers (known as Max Mayers, Discogs also lists a separate entry as Duncan Doilet), who had a strong keyboard and guitar presence and became a central figure in the band’s second phase.

From The Lions Mouth (1981, Korova KODE 5) was the Sound’s second album and is widely regarded as their strongest work. Co-produced with Hugh Jones — a Welsh producer who worked extensively in post-punk and new wave (also known for his work with Echo & The Bunnymen) — and with mixing by Julian Mendelsohn, the album had a fuller, more textured sound than the debut. The lineup was Borland (guitar, vocals), Mayers (keyboards), Green/Bailey (bass), and Dudley (drums). Key tracks include “Winning,” “Sense Of Purpose,” “Contact The Fact,” “Judgement,” “Fatal Flaw,” “Possession,” “The Fire,” “Silent Air,” and the closer “New Dark Age.” Songwriting was largely collaborative across the four members, with Borland as the primary creative driver. A live recording from this period, Live Instinct (1981), documented the band in performance.

The Sense Of Purpose (What Are We Going To Do) single appeared in 1981.

All Fall Down (1982) marked a slight change in approach. Co-produced by Nick Robbins and the band, with engineering by Flood (Mark Ellis, then early in his career), Craig Milliner, and Steve Prestage. The album brought in Wasp synthesizers, drum machines, and metal percussion alongside Dudley’s live drums, creating a harder-edged and more rhythmically complex texture. The lineup remained Borland, Mayers, Green/Bailey, and Dudley.

In 1983, a compilation release appeared: This Cover Keeps Reality Unreal on Cherry Red.

Statik Period (1984–1987)

After leaving Korova, the Sound signed to Statik Records, the UK independent run by Laurie Dunn out of West London. Statik was distributed by Virgin throughout most of this period.

The Shock Of Daylight EP (1984, Statik Records, Netherlands pressing) was a pivotal release — six tracks produced by Pat Collier. The EP brought in brass arrangements by Tim Smith and Sarah Smith (2), engineered by Alan Douglas (2). A bonus track on side B had drums and bass recorded by Nick Robbins and Graham Simmonds, with Mike Bigwood on tape operations. Borland continued to write all lyrics; his name appears both as “Borland” and “A. Borland” across the album’s credits.

Heads And Hearts (1985, Statik / Megadisc) was produced by Wally Brill, with engineering by Gavin MacKillop, Richard Manwaring, Simon Smart, and assistants Ben Kape, Nick Collins, and Steve “Barney” Chase. The sleeve was designed by 23 Envelope. The lineup was Borland (vocals, guitar), Mayers (keyboards, guitar), Green/Bailey (bass), and Dudley (drums).

In The Hothouse (1985, Megadisc) was a live album capturing the band at that stage of their career.

The Counting The Days single appeared in two versions — 1984 and 1986 — both on Statik, both produced by Pat Collier. The 1984 version and One Thousand Reasons (1984, also Statik) rounded out their mid-period singles.

Thunder Up (1987, Play It Again Sam Records, Belgium) was the final studio album. Co-produced by Nick Robbins and the band, with sleeve by Yellow Now. The lineup was the stable core four: Borland (vocals, guitar, lyrics), Mayers (keyboards, guitar), Bailey (bass guitar), and Dudley (drums, percussion). The album’s production was more polished than earlier releases. A companion release, Hand Of Love (1987, PIAS Belgium), appeared as a single.

Iron Years (1987, Polydor Netherlands) was a compilation of earlier material.

Dissolution and Aftermath

The Sound’s end was precipitated by Borland’s deteriorating mental health during the Thunder Up tour. While touring Spain in 1987, Borland suffered a complete breakdown; drummer Michael Dudley had to bring an incoherent Borland home on a plane. The band’s final gig took place at Zoetermeer, Netherlands — Borland walked offstage halfway through the set. The remaining members subsequently told Borland that the band needed a break and that he should seek help. The Sound formally dissolved in 1988.

Dudley later described the breakup in detail in a 2004 interview, providing one of the clearest accounts of the dissolution.

Adrian Borland moved to the Netherlands, which had always been the Sound’s most receptive territory, and continued as a solo artist. His solo albums — Beautiful Ammunition (1994, Resolve Records), Cinematic (1996, Resolve Records), 5:00 AM (1997, Earth Records) — maintained his characteristic emotional intensity. Posthumous releases continued well into the 2020s: Lovefield (2019), 2 Meter Sessions 1987–1995 (2021), and others on Dutch imprints Sounds Haarlem Likes Vinyl and Stichting Opposite Direction. Borland also performed with Adrian Borland & The Citizens and White Rose Transmission during the 1990s. He died by suicide on April 26, 1999.

Colvin Mayers (Colvin “Max” Mayers) died on Boxing Day 1993.

Reissues of The Sound’s catalog began in the late 1990s via Renascent, which released Shock Of Daylight & Heads And Hearts (1996), Propaganda (1999), The BBC Recordings (2004), and The Dutch Radio Recordings 1 (2006). Edsel Records later released two substantial CD compilations in 2014–2015.

Sound and Style

The Sound occupied a space between the angular austerity of post-punk proper and the more melodic terrain of new wave. Borland’s guitar playing was the dominant textural element — layered, reverb-heavy, with a sense of both urgency and weight. Mayers’s keyboards added atmosphere and occasionally countermelody. The rhythm section of Green/Bailey and Dudley was functional and propulsive but rarely called attention to itself.

Lyrically, Borland’s writing was introspective and often existential — preoccupied with social alienation, inner crisis, and a searching quality that didn’t resolve easily. “I Can’t Escape Myself” was the song that most people encountered first and that encapsulated the approach: a relentless lyric set against a churning guitar riff. The band’s work was closer to Bruce Springsteen’s earnestness (without the Americana) than to the ironic distance of many UK contemporaries, which contributed both to their cult appeal and their commercial marginalization.

Netherlands Connection

The Sound developed their strongest following in the Netherlands, touring there extensively across the band’s existence. Documented Dutch Radio recordings capture performances at: Amsterdam Paradiso (08.03.81), Utrecht No Nukes Festival (09.04.82), Arnhem Stokvishal (24.01.83), Den Haag Parkpop Festival (01.07.84), and Utrecht Vrije Vloer (09.04.85). The Dutch connection ran deep enough that Borland relocated there after the band’s dissolution, eventually recording his posthumously-released 2 Meter Sessions 1987–1995 (2021, Sounds Haarlem Likes Vinyl) and working with Dutch archivists well into the 1990s. The last Sound gig took place in Zoetermeer, Netherlands.

Critical Reception

Jeopardy (1980) received uniformly strong notices on release — 5-star reviews from NME, Sounds, and Melody Maker. Despite this, the band never broke commercially. From The Lions Mouth (1981) has retrospectively been identified as their strongest work, with “Winning,” “Sense Of Purpose,” and “Contact The Fact” frequently cited as highlights. Contemporary critical praise did not translate to chart performance or mainstream recognition.

The retrospective reassessment came largely after the band had already ended and after Borland’s death in 1999. The 2001 Renascent reissue campaign — which Borland did not live to see — brought the catalog to a new audience and generated the “overdue critical praise” that the band had largely missed in real time. The Sound are consistently described as a band that should have had the impact of Joy Division, Echo & The Bunnymen, or The Cure — but didn’t, despite the consistent quality of the work.
