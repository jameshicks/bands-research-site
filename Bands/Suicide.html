Biography

Pre-Suicide: Alan Vega’s visual art career (1960–1970)

Before music, Alan Bermowitz (later Alan Vega) was a visual artist working in light sculpture and assemblage. He studied physics and fine art at Brooklyn College under Ad Reinhardt and Kurt Seligmann, graduating in 1960. Through the 1960s he was involved with the Art Workers’ Coalition, a radical artists’ collective that protested major museums. In 1969, with funding from the New York State Council on the Arts, he co-founded MUSEUM: A Project of Living Artists — a 24-hour multimedia gallery at 729 Broadway in Manhattan. Working under the pseudonym “Alan Suicide,” he transitioned from painting to light sculptures made from electronic debris: discarded TVs, radios, and fluorescent tubes. He held a residency at the OK Harris Gallery in SoHo (exhibiting there until 1975, with Barbara Gladstone showing his work into the 1980s). His assemblages included neon crucifix constructions made from found metal in an arte povera manner. [source: Wikipedia/Alan Vega, as of 2026-06-06; ArtNews]

A turning point came in August 1969 when Bermowitz saw The Stooges perform at the New York State Pavilion — an event he described as a revelation that showed him “you didn’t have to do static artworks, you could create situations.” [source: Wikipedia/Alan Vega, as of 2026-06-06]

He met Martin Reverby (Martin Rev) in 1970, and the two began experimenting with electronic music. An early lineup also included guitarist Paul Liebgott; Liebgott was dropped in 1971 and Suicide continued as a duo.

Formation, name, and early live history (1970–1976)

Suicide’s first performance took place on June 19, 1970, at the MUSEUM gallery itself (729 Broadway), billed simply as “Punk Music” — one of the earliest documented uses of that term in a musical context. Early flyers described shows as “Punk Music by Suicide.” Vega had coined “punk music” for their sound, adopted from a Lester Bangs review of the Stooges. [source: concert chronology, fromthearchives.com; Wikipedia/Suicide, as of 2026-06-06]

The band name derived from a Ghost Rider comic book issue titled “Satan Suicide,” though Vega contextualized it more broadly: “We were talking about society’s suicide, especially American society. New York City was collapsing. The [Vietnam War] was going on.” [source: 2002 interview, cited in Wikipedia/Suicide (band), as of 2026-06-06]

Early shows (1970–1972) were at the MUSEUM gallery and associated arts spaces, including the OK Harris Gallery (Nov. 20, 1970) and Village Vanguard (Dec. 13, 1970 Sunday matinee). The duo then became fixtures at the Mercer Arts Center, sharing bills with the New York Dolls, Eric Emerson and the Magic Tramps, and other proto-punk acts. David Johansen played harmonica with Suicide at an early Mercer Arts show. After the Mercer Arts Center physically collapsed in August 1973, Suicide moved to Max’s Kansas City and CBGB (before eventually being banned from CBGB). Their performances were deliberately confrontational — Vega taunted audiences with a bicycle chain, sometimes barricaded exits, and the band stated explicitly that they were never interested in entertainment: “In those days, audiences wanted to go see a band for entertainment. They came off the street to see us and they got the street thrown right back in their faces.” Shows occasionally ended in actual violence. A 1978 Belgian performance had to be dispersed with tear gas. [source: Wikipedia/Suicide (band), as of 2026-06-06; Louder Sound]

Marty Thau — former manager of the New York Dolls (managed 1972–1975) and co-founder of Red Star Records — became an early champion of the band after hearing their demo and seeing them live. He signed them to Red Star and organized the debut recording session.

Debut album (1977)

Suicide (Red Star Records, 1977; cat. RS 1) was produced by Craig Leon and Marty Thau, engineered by Larry Alexander. The album contained seven tracks: “Ghost Rider,” “Rocket U.S.A.,” “Cheree,” “Johnny,” “Girl,” “Frankie Teardrop” (10:25), and “Che.” Styles catalogued on Discogs: Minimal, No Wave, Punk, Synthpunk.

Recording circumstances: The album was recorded over four days at Ultima Sound Studios in Blauvelt, New York (the same facility where Bruce Springsteen had recorded in the early 1970s when it was called 914 Sound Studios). Marty Thau, who had signed Suicide to Red Star after seeing them live, brought in Craig Leon to co-produce. The band entered the session with five years of live performance behind them and all material already rehearsed. Leon applied production techniques he had developed working with Bob Marley and Lee “Scratch” Perry, using an Eventide digital delay unit to create dub-style echo effects on Vega’s vocals — effects created live on the desk and printed to tape rather than added in post. After four days, Leon departed to fulfill another production commitment in Los Angeles, leaving Thau with rough monitor mixes. Thau remixed “Ghost Rider,” “Rocket U.S.A.,” and “Cheree”; Leon returned to New York and the two then remixed “Frankie Teardrop” together. The album was mastered at Frankford/Wayne in New York. [source: Wikipedia/Suicide (1977 album), as of 2026-06-06; The Quietus]

Conflict — studio attribution: The Discogs matrix for the original pressing reads “Atlantic Studios” and cutting by G.P. (George Piros). Wikipedia and The Quietus both state the recording took place at Ultima Sound Studios, Blauvelt, NY. “Atlantic Studios” likely refers to the mastering or cutting facility rather than the tracking studio, or may be a matrix inscription error. Frankford/Wayne is also named as the mastering location in other sources. Needs resolution.

The record was effectively ignored on release but became a foundational document of proto-punk and synth-punk, heavily cited by industrial, post-punk, and electronic artists throughout the 1980s and beyond.

Singles and Ric Ocasek connection (1978–1979)

“Cheree” (b/w “I Remember”) was released as a UK 7” (Bronze/Red Star, 1978). “Dream Baby Dream” / “Radiation” (Island Records, 1979) was the first release produced by Ric Ocasek of The Cars — establishing a production relationship that would carry over to the second album.

Second album and ZE Records (1980)

Suicide: Alan Vega · Martin Rev (ZE/Island, 1980; Germany pressing cat. 202 117) was produced by Ric Ocasek. The album marked a sharp stylistic pivot: where the debut was raw and confrontational, the second album was slick and synthetic — ten tracks of pulsing electro-pop with strong Giorgio Moroder influence. Credits: Martin Rev (electronics), Alan Vega (vocals), Larry Alexander (engineer), Garry Rindfuss and James Farber (assistant engineers). Tracklist: “Diamonds, Fur Coat, Champagne,” “Mr. Ray (To Howard T.),” “Sweetheart,” “Fast Money Music,” “Touch Me,” “Harlem,” “Be Bop Kid,” “Las Vegas Man,” “Shadazz,” “Dance.” The album was released on ZE Records / Island Records as ILPS 7007 in the UK and Europe. The duo had been properly signed to ZE (not merely distributed through its Island deal). The shift in sound reportedly caused friction.

Concurrent with the second album, Alan Vega released his solo debut Alan Vega (PVC Records / ZE / Celluloid, 1980), self-produced with engineer David Lichtenstein and guitarist Phil Hawk.

Live document and reformation (1981–1986)

1/2 Alive (ROIR, 1981) was a cassette-only live release. Credits: Alan Vega (vocals, co-composed), Martin Rev (instruments, co-composed), Wayne Vlcan (mix), Stanley Moskowitz (mastering), John Hanti (executive producer). Liner notes by Lester Bangs. Design by Jim Sclavunos (later of Nick Cave and the Bad Seeds / Grinderman). The release was notable for having Bangs — one of the most celebrated rock critics of the era — writing the notes.

Ghost Riders (ROIR-UK, 1986) compiled further live material.

A Way of Life (1988)

A Way of Life (Chapter 22, 1988) was Suicide’s third studio album, again produced by Ric Ocasek. Credits: Alan Vega (vocals, concept, written-by), Martin Rev (instruments, performer, written-by), Joe Barbaria (engineer), Bridget Davy, John Magnusson, Ken Steiger (assistant engineers), Renaud Monfourny (back cover photography). Ocasek also took the cover photos. The Ocasek production relationship — spanning the 1979 single, the 1980 album, and the 1988 record — was the band’s most sustained outside collaboration.

American Supreme and later period (2002–2016)

American Supreme (Blast First / Mute, 2002) was Suicide’s fourth studio album. Self-produced. Credits: Alan Vega (vocals, composed), Martin Rev (keyboards, composed), Perkin Barnes (engineer, tracks 1–11), Stephen Wright (live sound engineer). Essay by Matthew Worley. Released on Blast First and Mute.

The duo remained intermittently active through the 2000s and into the 2010s. Their final concert together took place at the Barbican Centre in London on July 9, 2015, billed as “A Punk Mass” — the billing invoking their earliest 1970 concert descriptions, with solo sets by both Rev and Vega preceding a Suicide headlining performance. [source: Electronic Sound]

Alan Vega died in his sleep on July 16, 2016, at age 78 in New York City. No cause of death was officially released, though Vega had suffered a stroke and a heart attack in 2012, which had also limited his mobility and caused him to shift focus to less physically demanding art (painting). The death was announced publicly by Henry Rollins, who posted an official statement from Vega’s family on his website. [source: NPR; Variety] Martin Rev continued recording solo material.
