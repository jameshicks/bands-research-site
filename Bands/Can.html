Can

German experimental rock band formed in Cologne, c.1968. Originally called Inner Space (3). Key pioneers of Krautrock. Discogs artist ID: 17203.

Overview

Can were formed in Cologne in 1968 by four principal musicians: Holger Czukay (bass, recording), Jaki Liebezeit (drums), Irmin Schmidt (keyboards), and Michael Karoli (guitar). The band began under the name Inner Space, becoming “The Can” when American vocalist Malcolm Mooney joined. After Mooney’s departure in 1970, Japanese vocalist Damo Suzuki fronted the group through their most celebrated period (1970–1973), yielding Tago Mago, Ege Bamyasi, and Future Days. Following Suzuki’s departure the band continued as an instrumental-led collective, adding new members and shifting toward funk and electronic influences through the mid-to-late 1970s. They dissolved around 1978, reunited briefly in 1986, and released one further studio album, Rite Time (1989). Their archival label Spoon Records continues issuing unreleased material and live recordings. The band received the German Echo Award for lifetime achievement in March 2003.

Biography by Era

Formation and Inner Space (1968)

Can emerged from the Cologne new music scene. Holger Czukay had studied composition under Karlheinz Stockhausen at the Hochschule für Musik in Cologne in the mid-1960s; during that period he met Michael Karoli, one of his guitar students. Irmin Schmidt came from a background in classical conducting. Jaki Liebezeit had been active in the Cologne jazz scene, playing with the Globe Unity Orchestra and Manfred Schoof ensembles before Can.

The initial lineup — Czukay, Liebezeit, Schmidt, Karoli, and flautist David Johnson — operated as Inner Space. American vocalist Malcolm Mooney, a sculptor living in Europe, joined and the group became “The Can.” Mooney’s raw, stream-of-consciousness vocal style anchored the debut.

The band established their own recording base, the Inner Space Studio, at Schloss Nörvenich near Weilerswist (outside Cologne). Nearly all recordings were made there by Czukay on basic equipment; self-production became a defining feature. Conny Plank engineered some early sessions though is not prominently credited on the main studio albums.

The Mooney Era: Monster Movie (1969)

Monster Movie (1969) was released on Music Factory (20) — a private Munich label (Discogs ID 970979, label code SRS 001) — in a limited pressing. The debut showcases the core aesthetic: locked Liebezeit groove, Schmidt’s organ, Karoli’s exploratory guitar, and Czukay’s bass and engineering. Side B is entirely occupied by the 20-minute improvisation “Yoo Doo Right,” edited down from a much longer session tape.

Mooney received writing and vocal credit across all tracks but departed in 1970, reportedly following a breakdown. He returned for the reunion album Rite Time (1989) and the archival Delay 1968 (1981).

Soundtracks (1970) and the Arrival of Damo Suzuki

Soundtracks (1970, Liberty, catno LBS 83 437 I) is a transitional album: most tracks feature Mooney (still credited on “Soul Desert” and “She Brings The Rain”) but the bulk reflects the new vocal direction established by Damo Suzuki. Suzuki had been discovered busking in Munich by Czukay and Karoli; he joined the band and performed with them the same evening, subsequently moving into the communal space at Schloss Nörvenich. The album collects film-score commissions and studio pieces; “Mother Sky” (14:30) is the most fully realized Suzuki-era piece on the record.

The Classic Era: Tago Mago / Ege Bamyasi / Future Days (1971–1973)

The three albums recorded with Damo Suzuki as primary vocalist are universally regarded as Can’s peak and among the most influential records in post-rock, experimental, and electronic music.

Tago Mago (1971, United Artists Records, catno UAS 29 211/12 X) is a double LP on United Artists. The five-piece lineup — Czukay (bass), Liebezeit (drums), Karoli (guitar), Schmidt (organ, electric piano), Suzuki (vocals) — produced material ranging from the percussive side-long “Halleluwah” to the abstract electronics of “Aumgn” and “Peking O.” The album established Can as a major international act and is the most cited in post-rock and no wave contexts.

Ege Bamyasi (1972, United Artists Records, catno UAS 29414) is tighter and more concise. Czukay adds electric bass engineering; the single “Spoon” (from the German TV show Das Messer) became a surprise chart hit in Germany. “Vitamin C” and “I’m So Green” are among the band’s most accessible pieces. Karoli plays shenai on several tracks in addition to guitar.

Future Days (1973, United Artists Records, catno UAS 29505) is the most ambient and beatific of the trio. The twenty-minute “Bel Air” closes side B; the title track and “Spray” demonstrate a drift toward textural lightness. This was Suzuki’s final studio album with the band; he left in 1973, converting to the Jehovah’s Witnesses.

Post-Suzuki Period: Collective Vocal Era (1974–1976)

Without a permanent vocalist, Can distributed singing duties among band members — primarily Karoli and Schmidt — and brought in guest vocalists on specific tracks.

Soon Over Babaluma (1974, United Artists Records, catno UAS 29 673 I): the first album without a dedicated vocalist. Karoli takes the lead on “Dizzy Dizzy” and “Chain Reaction” and “Quantum Physics”; Schmidt sings “Come Sta, La Luna.” The album moves Can toward a denser, more textural sound with Karoli adding electric violin.

Landed (1975, Virgin, catno 840096): the first album on Virgin, marking the move away from United Artists. Czukay mixes; Peter Gilmour (Discogs ID 480353) co-writes lyrics on several tracks. “Hunters And Collectors” is among the most admired tracks from this period. Olaf Kübler plays tenor saxophone on “Red Hot Indians.”

Flow Motion (1976, EMI Electrola / Harvest, catno 1C 062-31 837): the first album on the EMI/Harvest deal. Contains “I Want More” (3:29), which became an international hit single reaching the UK top 30 — a pop breakthrough that surprised the band’s experimental fanbase. Guest vocalists René Tinner and Peter Gilmour appear on “I Want More” and “…And More.” Simon Puxley co-produces “Cascade Waltz.”

The Late Period: Saw Delight / Out Of Reach / Can (1977–1978)

The addition of Rosko Gee (bass, ex-Traffic; Discogs ID 347591) and Rebop Kwaku Baah (percussion, ex-Traffic; Discogs ID 414036) marked a decisive shift toward funk and African-influenced rhythms. Czukay’s role was reduced to electronics and special sounds on Saw Delight, and he departed the band shortly after.

Saw Delight (1977, EMI Electrola / Harvest, catno 1C 064-32 156): Gee on bass and vocals; Rebop on percussion and vocals; Czukay on electronics (wave receiver) and special sounds. The 15-minute “Animal Waves” is the most extended piece. Critically divided reception: some heard a creative evolution, others heard the band losing its distinctive identity.

Out Of Reach (1978, EMI Electrola / Harvest, catno 1C 066-32 715): Czukay absent; the core writing is by Liebezeit, Schmidt, and Karoli. Rosko Gee and Rebop Kwaku Baah featured prominently. Conny Plank mixed the album.

Can (1978, EMI Electrola / Harvest, catno 1C 066-45 099): the final studio album of the original run. Czukay credited only as “Edited By” — a post-production role. Liebezeit, Karoli, Schmidt, Gee, and Rebop Kwaku Baah are the performing core. “All Gates Open” and “Safe” are credited as written in part by Rebop Kwaku Baah.

Archival and Reunion Releases (1981–)

Delay 1968 (1981, Spoon Records, catno SPOON 012): archival release of pre-Monster Movie recordings from 1968, featuring the original Mooney lineup. Czukay engineered and edited. The first release on the Spoon Records label, founded by Can manager Hildegard Schmidt in 1980 to control and issue the band’s catalog.

Rite Time (1989, Mercury, catno 838 883-1): the reunion album, recorded after the 1986 reunion of Czukay, Karoli, Mooney, Schmidt, and Liebezeit. Schmidt and Karoli co-produced and mixed; Czukay plays French horn, dictaphone, synthesizer, and bass.

The Lost Tapes (2012, Spoon Records, catno CDSPOON55): three-CD box set of unreleased archive recordings spanning both the Mooney and Suzuki eras. Compiled by Irmin Schmidt and Jono Podmore; curated with Daniel Miller (of Mute). David Johnson (4) appears on flute on several early tracks. Features previously unreleased live material (“Spoon — Live”) and studio experiments.

Spoon Records (Discogs ID 2187) was founded in 1980 by Hildegard Schmidt (Discogs ID 1162982), Can’s manager, to release the group’s catalog and solo/side-project material. The label is based in the UK (parent: Spoon Records UK Ltd., ID 673761). From the mid/late 1980s Mute Records took up UK distribution; from 1991 Mute handled international distribution and co-releases. Sublabels include Can Solo Edition (ID 395683) and Future Days Music (ID 3792065). Label code: LC 7395.

Open Questions

  • Exact reason for Czukay’s departure in 1977: creative tensions over funk direction are documented in profiles but the precise timeline and any personal factors are not confirmed from Discogs alone.
  • David Johnson (4) (Discogs ID 298779) — his exact period of involvement with Inner Space / early Can before Monster Movie is not fully resolved; he appears on The Lost Tapes on early-era tracks but is not credited on any official studio album.
  • Conny Plank’s role in early Can sessions: he is referenced in the band profile but has no formal credit on the main studio albums through the Discogs database; his mixing credit on Out Of Reach (1978) is the only confirmed album-level credit.
  • The 1986 reunion: exact dates, whether any live recordings were made, and what the immediate trigger was — not answerable from Discogs data alone.
  • Thaiga Raj Raja Ratnam (Discogs ID 10843882) and Michael Cousins (2) (Discogs ID 2190024) are listed as Can members in the Discogs group data but appear on no known studio album; context for their membership unknown.
  • Inner Space (3) catalog: the Discogs alias entry (ID 1211958) lists only Czukay, Schmidt, and Liebezeit as members (not Karoli) — the reason for Karoli’s absence from this listing is not explained.
