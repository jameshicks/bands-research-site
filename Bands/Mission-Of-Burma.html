Original Run (1979–1983)

Formed February 1979 in Boston. Roger Miller (guitar/vocals), Clint Conley (bass/vocals), and Peter Prescott (drums/vocals) came together as a trio; Martin Swope joined as a live tape-manipulation operator — running pre-recorded tape loops through the PA from the side of the stage, a structural element of their sound from the start.

Released their debut single “Academy Fight Song” b/w “Max Ernst” (AHS 104) on Ace Of Hearts Records in 1980. “Academy Fight Song” (written by Conley) became a touchstone of American post-punk. All early production by Richard W. Harte.

The Signals, Calls, And Marches EP (AHS 1006, 1981) — six tracks including “That’s When I Reach For My Revolver” (Conley) and the Conley/Miller co-write “All World Cowboy Romance” — established the template: melodic post-punk with dissonant tape overlays, engineered by John Kiehl. Design by Holly Anderson, who also co-wrote material.

Vs. (AHS 10010, 1982) was the full-length debut: 12 tracks, same core personnel, with Swope credited for tape manipulations, loops, and additional percussion. Miller played guitar, vocals, piano, trumpet, and percussion. “Mica” co-written by Conley and Holly Anderson. Produced by Richard W. Harte.

Miller’s tinnitus — noise-induced hearing damage from years of loud performance — became severe enough to force dissolution in 1983. The band played a final show and broke up. Miller states he first acquired tinnitus in 1977, before Burma formed; the condition worsened across the band’s active years, and was notably exacerbated by their final European tour. (rogerclarkmiller.com/tinnitus/, retrieved 2026-07-05)

Compilation Period (1985–2001)

The Horrible Truth About Burma (New Rose Records, France, 1985) — live LP, the only official release during the dormant period. Forget (Taang!, 1988) compiled material. Both documented and sustained the band’s reputation across the decade.

Taang! Records — how they obtained rights to Burma material

Taang!‘s Burma releases (Forget, 1988; the self-titled Mission of Burma EP, 1988) were previously unreleased recordings, not reissues of the Ace of Hearts catalog. The arrangement was a direct band-to-label deal: label founder Curtis Casella approached the band after the breakup asking “you got anything?”, and the band agreed to release working tapes they held — described in the band’s own words as “just live four tracks, no overdubs, just raced through.” These were recordings originally made for copyright-registration purposes. Clint Conley’s account: “They were sort of accidentally released. It didn’t take much to tempt us…” (Peter Prescott: “…to pry them loose.”). When asked if they opposed the release, Conley confirmed: “No, we came to an agreement with him.” (Source: Perfect Sound Forever interview, furious.com/perfect/missionofburmainterview.html, date undated but post-reunion.)

The Ace of Hearts catalog proper (Signals, Calls, And Marches; Vs.; The Horrible Truth About Burma) was reissued in 1997 under exclusive license from Ace of Hearts to Rykodisc — not Taang!. The 1997 Rykodisc editions are the remastered versions with bonus tracks. (Sources: Wikipedia, Signals, Calls, and Marches article; Vs. article, as of 2026-08-19; Lollipop Magazine review, 1997.)

Rights dispute — Let There Be Burma. Roger Miller later stated that Taang!‘s Let There Be Burma (a European double-LP that compiled Forget with the self-titled EP) included tracks released without the band’s clearance: “He should not have released that. He did not get clearance from us to release those other songs.” This indicates the original band-Taang! agreement covered a specific set of recordings and that Casella later exceeded it. Conley added: “I’m not sure we’re eager to have that reissued.” (Source: Perfect Sound Forever interview, same as above.)

Reunion (2002–2012)

Reunited 2002. Martin Swope declined to participate; Bob Weston — Chicago-based engineer (Shellac, Chicago Mastering Service) — stepped in for tape manipulations and live mixing. The reunion was otherwise the original three-piece of Miller, Conley, and Prescott.

Miller’s tinnitus was not resolved or substantially improved by 2002 — the condition remained chronic (“there is currently no cure” in his own words). The reunion was made possible through an aggressive hearing-protection regime: Miller wears maximum-attenuation solid-rubber custom earplugs (made by Brookline Hearing Services with no frequency curve, for maximum protection), plays with his amp positioned to the side rather than behind him, uses no monitor speakers on stage, and performs behind a plexiglass shield separating him from Prescott’s drum kit. At rehearsals he stands in a separate room from the rest of the band. He began wearing earplugs consistently from 1991 onward; during Burma reunion shows he used what WBUR described as “rifle range-quality ear protection.” Miller also noted that Clint Conley has developed tinnitus as well. In the Drowned in Sound interview Miller stated directly: “I was talking to Clint about it recently and he also has tinnitus, he just doesn’t talk about it. He says his ears are ringing more too!” No onset date is given, and Conley himself does not discuss it in any accessible interview. The condition does not appear to have affected Conley’s participation in the 2002 reunion or subsequent activity — he played all four reunion studio albums and has made no public statement linking his hearing to any absence or limitation. (drownedinsound.com/in_depth/4149167-mission-of-burma-is-the-pivot-of-my-life---dis-meets-roger-miller)

Miller has written about his tinnitus in depth on his own site, noting the character of the sound shifted over decades from “very specific pitches” to “more a white noise, with some high frequencies on top — like the whine of a TV set.” He tried acupuncture and high-dose Vitamin B without benefit. He characterises himself as having been “the poster boy for tinnitus in the mid-80s after Burma folded due to tinnitus.”

Sources: rogerclarkmiller.com/tinnitus/ (retrieved 2026-07-05); WBUR, 2019-03-08; Drowned In Sound interview

Martin Swope and the 2002 Reunion

No statement from Swope himself explaining his decision is on record in any accessible source. The picture assembled from band-member accounts is as follows:

Geography and withdrawal from music. By the early 1990s Swope had moved to Hawaii and, in Roger Miller’s description, “dropped off the map altogether.” By the time the reunion was being organised (2002), Miller had not heard from Swope in approximately a decade. (Source: Drowned in Sound interview with Roger Miller, drownedinsound.com/in_depth/4149167-mission-of-burma-is-the-pivot-of-my-life---dis-meets-roger-miller)

Retiring nature. Miller characterised Swope’s preference for the sound desk — rather than the stage — as always having suited “his retiring nature.” The implicit account is that Swope’s temperament was consistent with opting out. (Same source.)

Scale of the initial commitment. The Salon account from July 2002, written at the time of the first reunion shows, notes only that “Martin Swope has declined to take part in the reunion activities” and that the band was “good-naturedly dedicating the shows to him.” There is no explanation offered beyond his absence. The Ear Candy interview with Clint Conley (earcandymag.com) confirms only that Swope was “holed up somewhere in Hawaii” — no stated reason. (Sources: salon.com/2002/07/09/mission_burma/ (retrieved 2026-07-28); earcandymag.com/clintconley.htm (retrieved 2026-07-28))

Summary. The available record gives no statement from Swope himself and no precise reason from the three core members. The consistent account across all sources is: Swope had relocated to Hawaii, had substantially withdrawn from music, and “politely declined.” Whether he was specifically invited, whether he cited a personal reason, or whether contact was simply not re-established is not documented in any accessible press. The Quietus December 2012 Roger Miller interview (James Ubaghs, published 4 December 2012; now live at thequietus.com/interviews/mission-of-burma-roger-miller-interview/, previously returned 403) was read in full on 2026-10-01 and contains no mention of Swope — the interview covers the recording of Unsound, the band’s formation history, Miller’s musical influences, the internet’s effect on music scenes, and tinnitus advice. Martin Swope is not named.

Notes (web)

Matador Records split (2012)

Wikipedia confirms the separation: “In 2012 Mission of Burma parted ways with Matador and recorded their fifth full-length album, Unsound, for Fire Records” (wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_of_Burma, as of 2026-07-28). The date of the split is pinned to 23 January 2012 in several aggregator sources (Last.fm, fan wikis), though these do not cite a primary source for the specific date.

Reason for the split — Peter Prescott, Urban Milwaukee interview (September 2012):

In a September 2012 interview with Urban Milwaukee (urbanmilwaukee.com/2012/09/25/interview-mission-of-burmas-peter-prescott/), Peter Prescott described the Matador parting in detail. His account:

  • Matador had been personally connected to Burma from the start: “For a while, there were a few people at Matador who were kids who used to come to Burma shows back in the day.”
  • The label believed they could break the band commercially: “They had this idea that they could sell a lot of records with Burma, which was a really nice gesture.” But the fit never materialised: “our sound was too gnarly then, and it’s too gnarly now.”
  • The parting was mutual and amicable — framed as a natural expiry rather than a conflict: “I think when the time came for us to make another record, I think we both agreed that it would probably be best if we made them in a different way and a different place.” He summarised it as “really just a synchronicity that had worn out.”
  • Matador retained the back catalog; Prescott stated “there’s no animosity.”

The consistent picture across the 2012 press is that the band started recording Unsound without a label already in place — a situation Prescott described elsewhere (cited in Paste Magazine) as “actually a really good thing,” characterising the absence of label pressure as liberating. Unsound was recorded at the band’s Boston rehearsal space and is described as “a lot more reckless and out of control” than previous records. Fire Records (UK) came in after recording; Merge Records handled US distribution for Unsound (FV263LP).

No band member or label representative is quoted anywhere in accessible press explaining why Fire Records specifically was chosen (as opposed to other potential suitors), or whether the band shopped the completed album or signed before recording was finished. The Quietus December 2012 Roger Miller interview (thequietus.com/articles/10882-mission-of-burma-roger-miller-interview) and the Paste Magazine Peter Prescott interview (pastemagazine.com/music/mission-of-burma/catching-up-with-mission-of-burma) both returned 403 as of 2026-07-28 and 2026-08-01 respectively; these may contain further detail.

Summary: The Matador split was a mutual parting-of-ways driven by commercial mismatch — the label had hoped to break Burma to a larger audience, but the band’s sound proved too abrasive for that goal. The relationship ran three albums (ONoffON, The Obliterati, The Sound The Speed The Light) and then wound down without acrimony. (Source: urbanmilwaukee.com/2012/09/25/interview-mission-of-burmas-peter-prescott/, September 2012)

Fire Records signing — Unsound (2012)

What the recording timeline establishes. Unsound was tracked at Analog Divide (the band’s Boston rehearsal space/studio) in June 2011, then mixed at Wooly Mammoth, Waltham, MA in January 2012. (Sources: Fire Records product page firerecords.com/product/mission-of-burma-unsound/; Wikipedia Unsound article en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsound_(Mission_of_Burma_album), as of 2026-08-25.) The Matador split is dated 23 January 2012 in multiple aggregator sources. The album was therefore substantially complete — recorded and in mixing — at or around the time the Matador relationship ended. This strongly implies the deal with Fire Records came after (or concurrent with) the completion of recording, not before.

No direct quote on record. No accessible interview source contains a band member or label representative explaining why Fire Records specifically was chosen, how the parties were introduced, or whether the band shopped the completed record to multiple labels. The Quietus December 2012 Roger Miller interview (James Ubaghs, 4 December 2012; now live at thequietus.com/interviews/mission-of-burma-roger-miller-interview/; original /articles/10882-... URL is dead, no Wayback Machine captures exist) was read in full on 2026-10-01 — Fire Records is not mentioned anywhere in the interview body. The only Fire reference is a footer note: “Learn How: The Essential Mission Of Burma is out now via Fire.” The interview covers Unsound’s recording, the band’s history and reunion, Miller’s musical formation, tinnitus, and internet’s effect on music scenes; no label-signing discussion of any kind. The Paste Magazine Peter Prescott interview (pastemagazine.com/music/mission-of-burma/catching-up-with-mission-of-burma) remains 403 as of October 2026; no Wayback capture confirmed accessible.

Fire/Merge relationship. The Discogs catalog number FV263LP uses Fire Records’ FV prefix. Merge Records appears as co-label on the release, consistent with Fire’s standard practice of co-releasing or licensing to a US partner for North American distribution. No formal Fire–Merge distribution agreement for this period has been documented in any accessible source. Fire Records maintains New York offices in addition to its London base, and has routinely co-released US indie artists with US labels (e.g. Guided By Voices’ Let’s Go Eat the Factory, 2012, where Fire handled UK and Guided By Voices Inc. handled US). No evidence of a pre-existing Fire/Merge arrangement specifically for Burma has emerged. Merge’s involvement on Unsound appears to be standard US co-release, not a pre-established pipeline.

Summary. The most supportable reading of the available evidence: the album was recorded and largely or fully mixed before any Fire Records deal was formalised; the label came in after Matador and the band parted ways, consistent with Peter Prescott’s description of starting Unsound “without a label” as “a really good thing.” Why Fire specifically — as opposed to other potential suitors — is not documented in any accessible source.
