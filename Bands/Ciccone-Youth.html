Ciccone Youth

Oct 20, 202613 min read

Ciccone Youth

Side-project of Sonic Youth formed in 1986, incorporating Mike Watt (of Minutemen / fIREHOSE) as a fifth member. Discogs artist ID: 55966. Name variations: “Ciccone” (Discogs); Sonic Youth aliases on Discogs also include Sonic Death and Sonic Youth With Friends.

The group is primarily known for The Whitey Album (1988/1989, Blast First / Enigma (4)), a collage record mixing noise-rock originals, pop covers, found sound, and hip-hop-influenced beats, most notoriously a deconstruction of Madonna’s “Into the Groove.” The name “Ciccone Youth” is itself a reference: Madonna’s birth surname is Ciccone.

Background & Context

Origins and Concept

Ciccone Youth grew out of multiple intersecting threads in Sonic Youth’s early history. The most direct origin was a running band in-joke: Sonic Youth had long talked about covering the Beatles’ White Album in its entirety, a joke that somehow mutated into a twisted beatbox/sampler exercise nominally dedicated to Madonna. The project name itself — “Ciccone Youth” — is a hybrid of Sonic Youth and Madonna’s birth surname, Madonna Louise Ciccone. The band had made no secret of their fascination with Madonna throughout the mid-1980s, discussing her in interviews and listing “Madonna, Sean, and Me” as an alternate title for the closing track “Expressway to Yr. Skull” on EVOL (1986). (Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14)

The project was also conceived, in part, as a therapeutic gesture toward Mike Watt. Following the death of his closest friend and Minutemen bandmate D. Boon in December 1985, Watt fell into deep depression. He drove to the East Coast with his girlfriend Kira Roessler (who was beginning an internship at Yale), then continued to New York City, staying with Kim Gordon and Thurston Moore. He spent time with Sonic Youth during the EVOL sessions, playing bass on two album tracks (“In the Kingdom #9” and a cover of “Bubblegum”). The Ciccone Youth project was conceived as part of an effort to draw Watt back into making music. (Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14)

Madonna, Downtown NYC, and the Cultural Moment

Sonic Youth and Madonna shared a formative geography. Both had emerged from the same early-1980s downtown Manhattan underground, playing the same venues — Danceteria, CBGB, Tier 3, Club 57. Thurston Moore has stated that he and Madonna were neighborhood acquaintances who would say hello and crossed paths socially; she was dating a friend of his at one point. Moore has also claimed that Madonna “was actually in a couple of no-wave bands” prior to her pop breakthrough, citing a pre-Swans ensemble called Spinal Root Gang — though this claim circulates without independent corroboration and should be treated with caution. (Source: itsallmadonnasfault.tumblr.com; weak sourcing — fan-aggregated Moore quote from a 2018 Guardian interview ahead of Madonna’s 60th birthday.)

Moore told The Guardian in 2018 that Madonna “had credibility — she was really ahead of the game” and that “Into the Groove” was “brilliant to the point where I thought it would be a great song to cover through the prism of Sonic Youth.” Kim Gordon was also an early public proponent of Madonna within underground circles, defending her before the mainstream had come around. Covering “Into the Groove” in 1986 — when Madonna was transitioning from downtown scene fixture to global pop phenomenon — was simultaneously a sincere act of admiration and a subversive gesture: running one of the most commercially ubiquitous songs of the moment through the band’s noise-and-atonality filter.

When the full Whitey Album was assembled in 1987–88, Sonic Youth sent advance vinyl to Warner Bros. to reach Madonna through her sister, who worked in the label’s art department. Word came back that Madonna “had no problem with it, acknowledging she remembered the band from her NYC Danceteria days.” A widespread but unconfirmed rumour also holds that she personally discouraged Warner Bros. from pursuing legal action; this is plausible but not documented in primary sources. (Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14)

Recording Circumstances

The 1986 Ciccone Youth tracks (“Into the Groovey,” “Tuff Titty Rap”) were recorded during the EVOL sessions in March 1986 at BC Studio, Brooklyn, with Martin Bisi engineering. “Burnin’ Up” was recorded by Mike Watt separately at Radio Tokyo Studios in California, with Ethan James on drum programming/engineering and Greg Ginn adding lead guitar.

The full Whitey Album was recorded and mixed from approximately November 1987 to January 1988 at Wharton Tiers’ studio — commonly referred to as Fun City (his Manhattan basement studio, where he had been recording underground bands since 1981) or informally as “Wharton’s Palace of Confusion.” (Source: Sister Wikipedia article / search results; the informal name appears in fan and contemporary press references.) The sessions followed the conclusion of the Sister Tour and preceded work on Daydream Nation. The band used drum machines and samplers as core instruments — a deliberate departure from their standard live setup — while retaining the dissonance and noise-textural approach of their main output.

A notable production aside: Kim Gordon recorded her lead vocal on the Robert Palmer cover “Addicted to Love” in a karaoke booth, and directed and filmed the accompanying video at a Macy’s department store self-service video booth, where for approximately $19.99 customers could lip-sync against a blue screen. (Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14)

The “(Silence)” track (track 2, 1:02) is understood as a tribute to John Cage, fitting into a broader SY thread of Cageian influence.

Relationship to Daydream Nation

Sonic Youth originally intended to release The Whitey Album simultaneously with Daydream Nation (1988). Associates close to the band persuaded them to delay the Ciccone Youth release so as not to split attention from Daydream Nation. The album finally appeared in January 1989. The two records make natural companion pieces: one the band’s most canonically “serious” work, the other their most openly Dadaist.

Suzanne Sasic

Sasic is credited as “red head on ‘Two Cool Rock Chicks Listening to Neu’” on The Whitey Album. Web sources identify her as Sonic Youth’s lighting director at the time — the track is a conversational piece in which Kim Gordon and Sasic discuss managing Dinosaur Jr. with J Mascis adding guitar. No further recording credits for Sasic have been located on Discogs or in web searches. (Source: WhoSampled entry for the track and Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14.) Note: the track title in the tracklist above reads “Two Cool Rock Chicks Listening To Neu” — Discogs credits “Suzanne Sasic” as the vocal credit under the header “Red Head.”

History

Formation (1986)

Ciccone Youth emerged from the downtown NYC / West Coast punk crossover milieu. The first release was the US 7” single “Burnin’ Up” (1986, New Alliance Records, cat. NAR 030), a Madonna cover produced by Mike Watt at Radio Tokyo Studios with Ethan James on drum programming and engineering, and Greg Ginn (of Black Flag) contributing lead guitar and an overdub edit. The B-side / AA sides were “Tuff Titty Rap” and “Into The Groovy” — both recorded in New York with the full Sonic Youth lineup plus Martin Bisi engineering and co-producing.

The single was simultaneously released in the UK on Blast First (cat. BFFP 8T) as a 12” also called Into The Groove(y) / Burnin’ Up, with the tracklist slightly shuffled. The Blast First pressing’s runout etchings read: “IT’S A TUFF TITTY. INTO THE GRAVY” (Side A) and “DON’T BE AFRAID TO POGO, DON’T BE AFRAID TO RELATE” (Side B) — the latter phrase also appears on the US matrix.

The Whitey Album (1988–1989)

The full-length The Whitey Album was recorded in 1988 and released in late 1988 (US promo) / early 1989 (general release). It was produced, directed, and written by Ciccone Youth as a collective. Engineer: Wharton Tiers (tracks 1–6, 8–15, 17 on the final LP sequence); Mike Watt recorded track 7 himself (“Burnin’ Up (Mike Watt Orig. Demo)”). Mastered by Howie Weinberg. Sleeve xeroxed by Todd’s Copy Shop, NYC.

The record spans 17 tracks and blends multiple modes: noisescapes (“Needle-Gun”), near-silence (“(Silence)”), funk-influenced beats (“G-Force,” “Platoon II”), a long Sonic Youth rework (“Making The Nature Scene,” credited to Sonic Youth as written-by), a Robert Palmer cover (“Addicted To Love”), a short rap (“Tuff Titty Rap”), and the centrepiece “Into The Groovey” — a full-band deconstruction of Madonna’s “Into the Groove,” written by Madonna and Stephen Bray. Track 10, “Two Cool Rock Chicks Listening To Neu,” features J Mascis on guitar and Suzanne Sasic on vocals. Track 7 (“Burnin’ Up (Mike Watt Orig. Demo)”) is a solo Watt home recording of the same Madonna cover, written-by credit to Madonna.

Other Appearances (1988)

Ciccone Youth are listed under a “Music By” credit on From Science To Ritual (1988, Germany, cassette, label: An Intensive Incentive Production), a compilation of experimental / industrial artists also featuring Einstürzende Neubauten, Merzbow, Diamanda Galás, Nurse With Wound, Negativland, Controlled Bleeding, and others. Lee Ranaldo appears as a separate credit on the same cassette. This is a compilation appearance rather than a dedicated release.

Similarly, Russian label “буд кон” issued individual track releases (“G-Force,” “Into The Groove,” “March Of Ciccone Youth”) with no year listed — likely unauthorized or unofficial.

Reception & Legacy

Initial Reception (1988–1989)

Contemporary critical response was divided. The project was widely read as a prank or art provocation, and several reviewers didn’t look past that framing. Trouser Press dismissed it as “a self-indulgent mess” where “the joke doesn’t translate.” The Village Voice and Entertainment Weekly were also lukewarm (C grades from both). On the other side: NME awarded 8/10, Rolling Stone gave 4/5, AllMusic 4/5 stars, and Pitchfork scored it 7.8/10 (likely a retrospective score). (Wikipedia, as of 2025-12-14)

The 1986 single had already demonstrated there was an audience: “Into the Groovey” sold approximately 8,000 copies in the UK, driven in part by dance club play — an unusual crossover for a Sonic Youth-adjacent release. Steve Shelley told a fanzine in 1986 that the band “had very little feedback” pre-release and “heard Madonna wouldn’t sue them if they put it out.” (Wikipedia / fan sources)

Legacy and Reappraisal

The Whitey Album has developed a strong cult reputation as the band’s most playful and experimental artifact, and is now typically grouped with the Sister / EVOL / Daydream Nation run as evidence of the band’s range rather than treated as a footnote. It is often described as “a brilliant lark” or “surrealist pop experiment” — a record that reveals SY’s Dadaist capabilities as fully as Daydream Nation reveals their compositional ambition. The album’s use of sampling, drum machines, found sound, and pop-cover appropriation has also been cited as an influence on later indie-experimental cross-genre hybrids.

The record received new attention when Sonic Youth uploaded it to Bandcamp (2020) as part of the Sonic Youth Archive digital releases, indicating the band’s own ownership of it as a legitimate part of their catalog rather than a curio. The 2022 Goofin’ Records vinyl reissue further cemented its canonical status.

Thurston Moore’s 2018 Guardian interview and the album’s Bandcamp release both drew renewed press coverage positioning The Whitey Album as a prescient record: a noise-underground band sincerely engaging with peak-commercial pop at the exact moment the indie/mainstream divide was most charged. Moore’s assertion that critics questioned whether covering Madonna was “giving her credibility” — and his counter that “she already had credibility” — captures the cultural stakes of the project in 1986/88 with retrospective clarity.
