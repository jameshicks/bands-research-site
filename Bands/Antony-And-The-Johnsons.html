Overview

Antony And The Johnsons was an experimental and chamber pop collective based in New York City, formed in 1995 around the singer and songwriter Anohni — then performing and recording as Antony. The group was most prolific through the 2000s, with twelve releases in that decade and five more in the 2010s, releasing primarily through Rough Trade and Secretly Canadian, with earlier material appearing on Durtro. Saxophonist and multi-instrumentalist Doug Wieselman was the most constant collaborator, appearing across 54 shared credits, with Steven Bernstein, Stewart Lerman, and Bryce Goggin also recurring throughout the catalogue. The collective went on hiatus in 2015 and returned in 2023 under the name Anohni And The Johnsons.

Notes (web) — 2023 return as Anohni And The Johnsons

Sources searched: Bandcamp Daily, Crack Magazine, Dazed Digital, The Fader, Line of Best Fit, Xtra Magazine, Gagosian Quarterly (403), New Statesman (JS-walled), Grammy.com. Checked July–August 2023 press cycle. All findings below are from named-byline press and artist interviews.

Was the return deliberate or organic?

Anohni stated the album arrived without a pre-formed plan: “I wasn’t sure that I was going to do another record. I didn’t require it of myself.” Sessions with London producer Jimmy Hogarth provided the catalyst; Anohni described the finished album as “definitely a love child.” [Dazed Digital, 2023: https://www.dazeddigital.com/music/article/60313/1/anohni-interview-2023-my-back-was-a-bridge-for-you-to-cross-album-review]

Why the Johnsons name rather than solo?

No interview found where Anohni gives a direct, explicit answer to this. The closest is her framing of the Marsha P. Johnson tribute as continuous across her whole adult career: “I named my group after her when I was 24, and walked under her name, in a way, my whole adult life.” [Dazed Digital, 2023, ibid.] The album cover prominently features a photograph of Marsha P. Johnson; the record’s themes of queer lineage, tribute, and intergenerational connection appear to have made the collective/band framing feel appropriate. The name itself is described as “an amalgamation of her name and prior band name, itself an homage to gay liberation activist Marsha P Johnson.” [Dazed Digital, ibid.] Crack Magazine notes Anohni originally “explained to every audience why the group was called the Johnsons” — the 2023 return continues that memorial function. [Crack Magazine cover story 2023: https://crackmagazine.net/article/profiles/cover-story-anohni-interview-2023/]

No source explicitly states that the band format vs. solo format was a conscious artistic declaration separate from the album’s tribute themes.

Stylistic shift from Hopelessness (2016)

Hopelessness was described by Anohni as “the most strategic record I had made, with the intention to disarm the listener” — confrontational electronic/pop. For the 2023 album she said she “carried forward the frustration from Hopelessness but transformed it into something both more expansive and more personal.” [Line of Best Fit interview 2023: https://www.thelineofbestfit.com/features/interviews/anohni-reimagining-resilience-face-cataclysmic-change]

The soul direction was framed as tribute rather than reaction: Anohni wanted to “open up a conversation and to talk about the gift” of the soul music heritage that shaped her — influences cited include Nina Simone, Jimmy Scott, and Marvin Gaye (specifically What’s Going On, 1971). She sought to “investigate my personal relationship with soul-based singing.” [Line of Best Fit, ibid.; Xtra Magazine 2023: https://xtramagazine.com/culture/anohni-johnsons-back-bridge-cross-256095] Producer Jimmy Hogarth, known for contemporary soul work, assembled a live band for the sessions in place of the electronic production approach of Hopelessness.

The thematic concerns were continuous — systemic critique, ecocide, complicity — but the register shifted from outward-facing political protest to something “more stripped back, more vulnerable, and more analogue.” [Xtra Magazine, ibid.]

What was not found

No interview from this press cycle directly addresses the question “why return under the band name rather than as solo Anohni.” The return under the Johnsons name appears implicit in the album’s Marsha P. Johnson tribute framing and the soul/collective musical approach, but Anohni did not articulate this distinction in any source checked.
