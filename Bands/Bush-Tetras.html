Formation

Pat Place and Dee Pop began jamming after the Contortions dissolved (spring 1978). They recruited Laura Kennedy — who was living with Place — and jammed as a trio for six months before finding a singer. Adele Bertei played one show at The Kitchen before they turned to Cynthia Sley. First proper gig: Tier 3, February 1979.

The name: Dee Pop wanted “Bush Babies” (a small African primate); Pat Place wanted “Neon Tetras” (a fish). They combined one word from each. Pop admitted it “means absolutely nothing."

"Too Many Creeps” and 99 Records

The 99 Records origin

During a shooting incident at a Black Uhuru concert at Hunter College, Dee Pop’s mother (a DownBeat photographer) was hiding behind a car next to Ed Bahlman, owner of 99 Records (99 MacDougal Street, Greenwich Village). She told him about her son’s band. Bahlman attended a Bush Tetras show at Hurrah’s and signed them. They became 99 Records’ second signing, after ESG.

”Too Many Creeps” (99 Records, 99-02, 1980)

Their debut 7” EP. Music by Pat Place (reportedly written in five minutes while working at Bleecker Street Cinema), lyrics by Cynthia Sley — reflecting the daily reality of street harassment on the Lower East Side. Recorded at a Manhattan jingle studio with borrowed equipment.

  • #57 Billboard Club Chart
  • ~30,000 copies sold — extraordinary for an independent 7”
  • B-sides: “Snakes Crawl” / “You Taste Like the Tropics”
  • Became a staple of downtown NYC clubs and college radio

The Fetish Records Connection

Rod Pearce (Fetish Records, London) had already approached them before the 99 Records deal. After the success of “Too Many Creeps,” Fetish signed them for UK releases. Three records followed:

They performed at London’s Rainbow Theatre in 1981 as part of the Stiff Records Start Swimming showcase, alongside The Bongos, The Raybeats, and The dBs — a trans-Atlantic no-wave/post-punk showcase for British audiences.

The Topper Headon Connection

Bush Tetras were booked to open at The Clash’s Bond International Casino residency — a legendary 17-night run in Times Square, spring 1981. There, Dee Pop and Topper Headon became close friends.

Headon offered to produce their next EP. Rituals was recorded at Electric Lady Studios (Jimi Hendrix’s studio, West 8th Street, Greenwich Village) in July 1981, engineered by Brad Samuelson, mastered at Sterling Sound.

The relationship ran deeper than one session: Dee Pop substituted for Headon on Clash tour dates as Headon’s drug addiction worsened. Pop was reportedly considered for full membership in The Clash before Headon’s brief return. Headon was fired from the band in May 1982.

Headon contributed a written appreciation to the 2021 Rhythm and Paranoia box set.

Notes (web — Dee Pop / Clash deputising, July 2026)

The existing vault claim (“Dee Pop substituted for Headon on Clash tour dates… was reportedly considered for full membership before Headon’s brief return”) is partially supported by web sources but no source provides specific dates, shows, or tour names.

Source-by-source findings:

  • Village Sun obituary (url): “He also performed with The Clash, closely considered for full membership before Topper Headon’s return.” — This is the most specific web claim. It confirms performing with The Clash and consideration for membership, but gives no dates or shows.
  • amNewYork (url): Lists “The Clash” among Dee Pop’s “notable gigs” in a single sentence, no elaboration.
  • Modern Drummer 2007 profile (url): Lists “rockers like The Clash, Richard Lloyd, Michael Karoli from Can, The Gun Club, Jayne County” — no detail on nature or dates of Clash involvement.
  • Louder Than War 2019 Dee Pop interview (url): The only Clash reference is Bush Tetras playing on the same bills as The Clash in England. The interview does not mention Dee Pop ever drumming for The Clash.
  • Wikipedia — List of the Clash band members (url, as of 2026-07-28): Dee Pop is absent. Substitute drummers listed: Rob Harper (Dec 1976–Jan 1977), Terry Chimes (multiple stints, including May–Nov 1982 when Headon was fired), Pete Howard (May 1983–1986). Terry Chimes was the immediate replacement when Headon left in May 1982, not Dee Pop.
  • No setlists, contemporary concert reviews, or dated primary sources found anywhere confirming Dee Pop appearing behind The Clash’s drum kit at a specific show.
  • blackmarketclash.co.uk — 1982 gigs archive (url): Headon fired May 21, 1982. Terry Chimes was contacted by manager Bernie Rhodes on May 24 — five days before the first post-Headon show (Asbury Park, May 29). This leaves effectively no gap in which another drummer could have handled official tour dates before Chimes stepped in. After Headon’s firing the band’s stated plan was “to continue as a trio with guest drummers as required” — but no names of any such guest drummers are documented in this archive.

Assessment: The “substituted for Headon on tour dates” and “considered for full membership” claims in the vault are sourced from secondary obituary reporting and are not contradicted, but they are also not corroborated by any primary source, Clash-side account, setlist, or contemporary review. Terry Chimes is the documented replacement when Headon was fired (May 1982), contacted within three days and playing the first post-Headon show six days later. Dee Pop’s involvement, if it occurred, may have been informal rehearsals before Terry Chimes was confirmed, or during an earlier period of Headon’s declining availability before the formal firing — or the claim may conflate the close personal friendship between Dee Pop and Headon with actual dep drumming. The vault’s current wording (“substituted for Headon on Clash tour dates”) is stronger than the sources warrant and should be treated with caution.
