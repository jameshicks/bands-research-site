Buddy Rich

Buddy Rich

Aug 09, 202611 min read

Buddy Rich

Born: September 30, 1917, Brooklyn, New York (birth name Bernard Rich). Died: April 2, 1987, Los Angeles (heart failure following surgery for a malignant brain tumor). American jazz drummer and bandleader. Discogs artist ID: 57620

Aliases used: Bernard Trapps, Buddy Poor.

Widely regarded as the most technically gifted drummer in jazz history — extraordinary speed, power, and fluency without formal training, performing from childhood as a tap dancer and self-taught drummer. Led big bands intermittently from the 1940s through the 1980s, with his most celebrated ensemble being the Swingin’ New Big Band formed in 1966. His recorded output spans swing, bebop, hard bop, post-bop, and crossover-rock big band styles.

Biography

1930s–1940s: Sideman Era — Swing Orchestras and JATP

Rich came up as a sideman in the swing era, working with many of the major big bands: Tommy Dorsey, Artie Shaw, Benny Goodman, Harry James, Bunny Berigan, and Woody Herman. He also played in smaller contexts alongside Charlie Parker, Lester Young, and Coleman Hawkins.

From the late 1940s he was a fixture in Norman Granz’s Jazz at the Philharmonic concerts and recordings, appearing alongside Oscar Peterson, Flip Phillips, Roy Eldridge, Harry Edison, and others in JATP all-star configurations. These recordings were issued on Granz’s Clef and Norgran imprints. The V-Disc recordings of 1946–47 preserve some of the earliest documentation of Rich’s solo drumming in a big band context.

Rich also led or co-led small-group sessions through the late 1940s and early 1950s under his own name on Norgran, including the trio formation and various all-star dates.

1954–1958: Norgran, Clef, and Verve — Small Groups and Vocal Dates

Under Norman Granz’s labels — Norgran, then Clef, then Verve — Rich recorded a series of small-group albums. The Norgran sessions from 1954–56 typically featured West Coast and swing-era veterans:

  • The Swinging Buddy Rich (Norgran, 1954): with Harry Edison (trumpet), Willie Smith (2) (alto saxophone), Milt Bernhart (trombone), Gerald Wiggins (piano), Joe Comfort (bass). Supervised by Norman Granz.
  • Buddy and Sweets (Norgran, 1955): a dual-lead date co-billed with Harry Edison.
  • Krupa and Rich (Clef, 1956): celebrated drum-battle album co-starring Gene Krupa, with Hank Jones on piano.
  • The Lionel Hampton–Art Tatum–Buddy Rich Trio (Clef, 1956): three-way summit with Lionel Hampton and Art Tatum.
  • Buddy Rich Sings Johnny Mercer (Verve, 1956): vocal album arranged and conducted by Buddy Bregman — Rich as singer, not drummer.
  • Buddy Rich Just Sings (Verve, 1957): second vocal outing.
  • Buddy Rich in Miami (Verve, 1958): studio album.

Creed Taylor produced some of the later Verve small-group sessions (documented in the 2005 Mosaic box set).

1959: Mercury and the Drum Battle Albums

By 1959 Rich had moved to Mercury, where Jack Tracy served as A&R director/recording director. Tracy produced and wrote liner notes for several Rich Mercury dates:

  • Rich Versus Roach (Mercury, 1959): the definitive drum-battle album, pitting Rich against Max Roach across live and studio tracks. Personnel included Phil Woods (alto saxophone), Stanley Turrentine (tenor saxophone), Julian Priester and Willie Dennis (trombones), Tommy Turrentine (trumpet), John Bunch (piano), Phil Leshin and Bobby Boswell (bass). Jack Tracy wrote the liner notes; Bill Stoddard engineered.
  • The Voice Is Rich (Mercury, 1959): another vocal album, produced by Jack Tracy as “Recording Director.”

1961–1966: Argo, Limelight, and the Verve Small Groups

After Mercury, Rich appeared on Argo (a Chess subsidiary), with Jack Tracy again in a supervisory capacity over the label’s broader jazz roster. A Lulu’s Back in Town date appeared on Argo in 1961.

The 2005 Mosaic box Argo, Emarcy and Verve Small Group Buddy Rich Sessions documents the range of this period comprehensively, with Norman Granz producing the Norgran/early Verve material, Jack Tracy producing the Argo/Mercury/Limelight material, and Creed Taylor producing the later Verve small-group sessions. Session musicians across this box included: Oscar Peterson (piano), Ray Brown (bass), Barney Kessel (guitar), Freddie Green (guitar), Ben Webster (tenor), Flip Phillips (tenor), Benny Carter and Willie Smith (alto), Harry Edison, Joe Newman, Thad Jones (trumpets), and vibraphone/marimba work by Mike Mainieri.

Rich also recorded the unusual Rich À La Rakha (World Pacific, 1968) — a cross-cultural percussion summit with Indian tabla master Alla Rakha (father of Zakir Hussain), plus flautist Paul Horn, produced by Richard Bock. The album blends jazz drums with tabla, sitar, and dholak.

1966–1969: Pacific Jazz and the Swingin’ New Big Band

Rich formed his most celebrated ensemble — the Swingin’ New Big Band — in 1966, signing to Pacific Jazz. The label’s owner and producer was Richard Bock.

  • Swingin’ New Big Band (Pacific Jazz, 1966): debut of the new ensemble. Personnel: Gene Quill and Pete Yellin (alto saxophone), Jay Corre and Marty Flax (tenor saxophone), Steve Perlow (baritone saxophone), Bobby Shew, Yoshito Murakami, Walter Battagello (trumpets), Jim Trimble and Dennis Good (trombones), John Bunch (piano), Barry Zweig (guitar), Carson Smith (bass). Engineered by Wally Heider.
  • The Sounds of ‘66 (Reprise, 1966): co-billed with Sammy Davis Jr., with Rich’s orchestra backing Davis. Arranged by Ernie Freeman and George Rhodes, produced by Jimmy Bowen.
  • The Driver (EmArcy, 1967): UK single.
  • Rich À La Rakha (World Pacific, 1968): see above.

The big band featured recurring tenor saxophonist Pat LaBarbera (who rejoined multiple times) and arrangements by his brother John LaBarbera.

1966–1974: Reprise, Liberty, and Groove Merchant — Rock-Era Big Band

Rich’s big band was known for covering rock and pop hits in big-band arrangements — material by the Beatles, Cream, and others — making it commercially accessible in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

The Groove Merchant period (1973–75) produced a cluster of studio and live albums, all produced by Sonny Lester:

  • The Roar of ‘74 (Groove Merchant, 1974): with Pat LaBarbera (tenor), Joe Romano (alto), Alan Kaplan and Keith O’Quinn (trombones), Joe Beck (guitar), Tony Levin (bass). Engineered by Malcolm Addey.
  • Very Live at Buddy’s Place (Groove Merchant, 1974): live recording at Rich’s own nightclub.
  • Big Band Machine (Groove Merchant, 1975): with arrangements by John LaBarbera.
  • The Last Blues Album Vol. 1 (Groove Merchant, 1974).
  • Nirvana (Groove Merchant, 1974).
  • The Roar of ‘74, Transition, The Bull (1974–75).

1971–1972: RCA Victor

  • A Different Drummer (RCA Victor, 1971): arrangements by John LaBarbera. Personnel included Pat LaBarbera (tenor), Candido (congas), David Spinozza (guitar), Dan Morgenstern (liner notes). Produced by Pete Spargo.
  • Rich in London (RCA Victor, 1972): live at Ronnie Scott’s.

1975–1980: Pablo and Scattered Collaborations

Rich appeared on Norman Granz’s Pablo Records in the mid-1970s, continuing the JATP/small-group thread:

  • The Tatum Group Masterpieces (Pablo, 1975) and …Again! (Pablo, 1976): archival Art Tatum-led material with Rich.

The Both Sides album (Mercury, 1976) returned to Mercury briefly. By the late 1970s, Rich led dates for Chiaroscuro, RCA, and smaller labels while continuing to front a big band on the club circuit.

1981–1987: Final Years

  • Lionel Hampton Presents: Buddy Rich (Who’s Who in Jazz, 1977): a Hampton-produced vehicle.
  • No Jive (Novus/RCA, 1992, released posthumously).
  • Time Being (Bluebird/RCA, 1987): recorded shortly before his death.
  • Just in Time – The Final Recording (Gearbox Records, 2019): released posthumously from his final sessions.

Rich continued touring and leading big bands almost until the end of his life. He died April 2, 1987, following brain tumor surgery.

Connections

  • Jack Tracy — produced and supervised the Mercury/Argo/Limelight period, the most commercially consistent arc of Rich’s recording career; 31 shared releases.
  • Norman Granz — ran the Norgran/Clef/Verve/JATP ecosystem that employed Rich throughout the 1950s and 1960s; produced the bulk of the early small-group recordings. See also Norman Granz.
  • Max Roach — Rich Versus Roach (Mercury, 1959) is often cited as the definitive drum-battle record; the two were friendly rivals.
  • Gene Krupa — Krupa and Rich (Clef, 1956) and The Drum Battle at JATP (Verve, 1960); Krupa was the older influence Rich consciously measured himself against.
  • Alla Rakha — father of Zakir Hussain; the 1968 World Pacific summit was an early example of jazz-Indian percussion crossover.
  • Pat LaBarbera and John LaBarbera — the LaBarbera brothers (Pat as tenor soloist, John as arranger) were central to the big band’s sound in the 1971–75 period.
  • Woody Herman — Rich played in Herman’s various Herds across different decades; Herman was among the bandleaders most associated with Rich outside his own bands.
  • Tommy Dorsey — Rich’s tenure with Dorsey in the early 1940s is considered formative; he left and rejoined Dorsey several times.
  • Sammy Davis Jr. — The Sounds of ‘66 (Reprise) represents Rich’s big band in a showbiz crossover context.
