David Sylvian

Real name: David Alan Batt Born: 23 February 1958, Lewisham, London Role: Vocalist, songwriter, composer, producer, guitarist, keyboardist Discogs artist ID: 10069 Alias: Mr. X (7)

British artist. Lead vocalist and principal songwriter of Japan (1974–1982). Extensive solo career from 1984 onward. Collaborated with Ryuichi Sakamoto, Robert Fripp, Holger Czukay, and many others. Labels: Virgin, then Samadhisound (his own imprint).

Brother of Steve Jansen (born Stephen Batt). Both founded Japan with Mick Karn and Richard Barbieri in Catford, South London.

Japan Credits

Principal songwriter throughout Japan’s catalog. Composed nearly all material on Adolescent Sex (1978), Obscure Alternatives (1978), Quiet Life (1979), and Gentlemen Take Polaroids (1980). Co-produced Tin Drum (1981) with Steve Nye; self-produced the “Ghosts” single (1982). “Life In Tokyo” (1979) co-written with Giorgio Moroder.

Harold Budd Collaboration

Sylvian’s relationship with Harold Budd was rooted in long-standing mutual admiration and developed into a significant act of artistic advocacy. Sylvian had first heard the unreleased album that would become Avalon Sutra in 2001 and attempted to find a record deal for it. In his words: “Amazingly, I first heard the album in 2001, when I tried to help Harold find a deal for that album. Everyone turned it down.” (davidsylvian.com Q&A with Jason Cowley)

The two met in person by chance in 2003 in Los Angeles. Budd recalled the meeting: “It wasn’t until I met, by accident, David Sylvian in Los Angeles and we had lunch.” At that lunch Sylvian offered to release Avalon Sutra on the newly established Samadhisound label, which had been made viable by the self-release success of Sylvian’s own Blemish (2003). Budd accepted without hesitation. (The Confessions of Harold Budd, The Mouth Magazine, January 2014)

Sylvian described Budd as “An artist who cannot get arrested in his own country,” and framed the Samadhisound release as part of a broader label philosophy: “It’s now very gratifying to have a label and to be able to offer a platform to artists we admire, such as Harold Budd.” (davidsylvian.com Q&A with Jason Cowley)

Budd reciprocated the admiration: “David Sylvian’s artistry and music I admire so much. Of all my contemporaries it is he who I find the most interesting.” When describing the recording experience: “When I was recording with David Sylvian, who is into layering, just putting together these amazing layers of sound, I was so impressed by that.” (Harold Budd Interviewed, The Quietus, January 2014)

A notable complication in Budd’s own framing: in a March 2014 Fact Magazine interview, Budd stated “The one collaboration that never occurred and never would occur would be David Sylvian, whose work I admire above all others,” explaining that combining two strong things does not make something twice as good — “In fact it could be a disaster, and I’ve never wanted that to happen.” (Fact Magazine, March 2014) This appears to refer to a full joint album or formal artistic partnership — distinct from the Samadhisound label relationship and the limited production work Budd described in the Quietus piece two months earlier. The two statements are not strictly incompatible: Sylvian released Budd’s solo work and co-produced one Rabelais disc track, but the artists never made a record together as co-authors.

Literary connection

Beyond music, Sylvian wrote the introduction to Budd’s poetry collection Aurora Teardrops (published circa 2013–2014). The introduction ran to two pages and was described as “a beautiful encapsulation of the poetry and the poet, composed in David’s characteristic evocative style.” Sylvian also signed the 26 deluxe lettered copies of the book; logistics required the signature sheets to be couriered to Sylvian at a location where he was stationary. The deluxe edition included a manuscript score for Budd’s Avalon Sutra track “It’s Steeper Near the Roses (Dedicated to David Sylvian).” (davidsylvian.net; bookstellyouwhy.com interview with Harold Budd and Jane Maru) The Aurora Teardrops introduction includes this Sylvian line on Budd’s poetry: “There’s no attempt at concealment, no decorous distraction, no sleight of hand. Radiating out from between the lines, the bones of the ribcage, love from the resonant heart of a soulful man.”

What was produced together

Avalon Sutra / As Long as I Can Hold My Breath (Samadhisound ss004, early 2005) — double CD. Disc 1 (Avalon Sutra) is Budd’s piano improvisations with string arrangements and electronic drones, including his first string quartet writing in over thirty years. Disc 2 (As Long as I Can Hold My Breath) is a rework by Akira Rabelais. Sylvian served as art director and co-produced one track on disc 2 alongside Rabelais. Track 3 on disc 1, “It’s Steeper Near the Roses,” is explicitly dedicated to Sylvian. Budd presented the album as his final recording and performed a final concert in Brighton in May 2005. (davidsylvian.net; sylvianvista.com)

Perhaps (Samadhisound SOUND-DL001, digital, 2007) — live solo piano recording by Budd, a tribute to his late friend and Cal Arts colleague James Tenney, recorded at CalArts on 8 December 2006. A second Samadhisound release, indicating Budd did not fully retire after Avalon Sutra. (Samadhi Sound Wikipedia)

The earlier Marco Polo (1995) release by Nicola Alesini and Pier Luigi Andreoni (Discogs release 202056) features both Budd and Sylvian as credited performers alongside Roger Eno and David Torn — this predates the Samadhisound relationship and may represent an earlier point of contact, though the circumstances are unconfirmed from available sources.
