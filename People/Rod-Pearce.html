Rod Pearce

Born: c.1958 Died: May 1997, Mexico (aged 39; murdered) Role: Record label founder, manager

Fetish Records

Rod Pearce founded Fetish Records in 1978, aged approximately 20, running it as a one-man operation from a tiny two-room flat on Denbigh Street, Pimlico, travelling London by motorcycle. He is described by contemporaries as “an unflappable optimist” and “one of the coolest people I have ever met.”

The Founding Story

The label began not from a grand artistic vision but from an opportunistic reissue deal. In mid-1978, Steve Brown — working at Miles Copeland’s Faulty Products distribution company — received a call from Genesis P-Orridge about reissuing Throbbing Gristle’s sold-out debut The Second Annual Report. Brown had the contacts but not the money. He approached Pearce, whom he describes as “someone involved in the bootleg record scene” who “had (plenty of) cash” and “always carried his passport and drove a quite large motorbike.”

Pearce put up the capital. The name “Fetish” was his idea. The first pressing of the TG reissue was 2,000 copies; 1,000 shipped to Bayer Records in Philadelphia.

After this, Pearce took the label forward largely on his own.

What He Built

Fetish became “an opportune, if haphazard, meeting of New York, Sheffield, and Hackney” (Jon Savage, The Last Testament sleeve notes). The transatlantic logic — UK industrial alongside NYC no-wave — was genuinely distinctive for the era. How Pearce developed the New York connections is undocumented in any surviving interview.

He signed 23 Skidoo after seeing them at a North London Polytechnic performance in March 1981. He brought in Neville Brody as art director, giving him unusual creative freedom — Brody later said the Fetish musicians were “totally open to the idea of me working under my own steam.”

How Pearce Built the NYC Connections

The New York arm of Fetish was assembled through a series of direct encounters in the US, not through a pre-existing London industry network.

Snatch as the bridgehead (1980). The first NYC-adjacent release on Fetish was the Snatch 12” Shopping for Clothes (April 1980, FET 001 or early cat). Snatch — the duo of Judy Nylon and Patti Palladin — were American expatriates based in London, making them the easiest transatlantic connection: Pearce could meet them without leaving the UK. Their presence on the label may have given Fetish early credibility among the NYC underground.

WKGB and the Devo orbit (1979–80). WKGB, a New Jersey no-wave electronics duo (David Goessling and Dennis Kelley), appear as Fetish’s second release (FET 002, “Non-Stop” / “Ultramarine”, recorded June 1979 at Mix-O-Lydian Recording Studio, Boonton NJ; released 1980). According to a 2021 NJArts.net interview with Bongos members, Pearce had already signed WKGB “after hearing them through the Devo connection.” WKGB had a verifiable link to Devo: their demo tape was used as pre-show music on Devo’s early tours, and they opened for Devo at Central Park in July 1980. [Source: NJArts.net, 2021; WKGB history via NTS]

How Pearce heard about WKGB — what the sources say and do not say (web research, October 2026). The NJArts article (Rob Norris, 2021) states that WKGB “caught the ear of Devo” and that “Rod Pearce heard them” in the context of their Central Park performance. However, the article does not explicitly state that Pearce was present at the Central Park show or that it was the discovery moment. A critical timeline conflict makes the Central Park show an impossible origin point: “Non-Stop” / “Ultramarine” was recorded in June 1979 (confirmed by Discogs release metadata and the Modulisme Bandcamp Dennis Kelley biographical note), and the Fetish single was released in 1980 — more than a year before the Central Park Devo show (July 1980). Pearce must therefore have known about and signed WKGB before the Central Park date. The more plausible mechanism is the pre-show demo tape: WKGB’s tape was used as pre-show music on Devo’s early tours (per NTS artist page), which would have circulated their sound within Devo’s orbit before 1979. Whether Devo members or associates actively recommended WKGB to Pearce, whether Pearce encountered the tape through Devo’s UK connections, or whether there was another route entirely, is not stated in any accessible source. The NJArts article appears to conflate WKGB’s Devo association (pre-show tape use, then the Central Park opening) with the moment Pearce discovered them, without specifying a precise mechanism.

No Dennis Kelley interview with this level of detail has been found in any web-accessible source. The Modulisme Bandcamp biographical note (Dennis Kelley page for “Synthi Study #4”) provides the most specific WKGB timeline but says only that the Central Park show preceded Bearsville Studio sessions — it does not address how Pearce or Fetish Records found them. [Sources: NJArts.net, 2021; Modulisme Bandcamp — Dennis Kelley, “Synthi Study #4”; NTS — WKGB; Discogs WKGB release metadata (FET 002)]

The Bongos: signed on a napkin at Maxwell’s (1980). The most fully documented NYC signing. Pearce travelled to Maxwell’s in Hoboken, New Jersey, specifically to see WKGB play. Dennis Kelley of WKGB recommended he stay to watch The Bongos. According to band member Rob Norris: “he made us an offer that night and wrote it down on a napkin, as I remember.” Barone confirmed: “we signed it right there at Maxwell’s, in the restaurant area.” In the TapeOp interview, Barone describes Pearce as “very young — he was like 21 himself and starting this label in London — happened to see us and really liked what we were doing, so he signed us.” Their debut recordings (“Glow in the Dark” / “Telephoto Lens”) were made at Mix-O-Lydian Studios, New Jersey. [Source: NJArts.net, 2021; TapeOp interview with Richard Barone]

Bush Tetras: success attracted the label (1981). Bush Tetras’ debut 7” “Too Many Creeps” was released in 1980 on the NYC label 99 Records and reached #57 on Billboard’s club play chart. According to multiple accounts, the success of “Too Many Creeps” attracted Fetish’s attention; Pearce signed them for their follow-up, “Things That Go Boom in the Night” (1981), which charted at #43 on the UK Indie Chart. No interview or memoir provides a more specific account of how Pearce encountered the band or who made first contact. [Source: Bush Tetras Wikipedia (as of 2026-07-15); AllMusic]

8 Eyed Spy: posthumous release (1981). 8 Eyed Spy broke up in late 1980 following the death of bassist George Scott III on 5 August 1980. Their self-titled Fetish LP (FR2003, released 18 October 1981) couples studio recordings made in October 1980 at Blank Tapes, NYC (produced/engineered by Bob Blank), with live material — the whole thing remixed by Scott Lit and Chris Stamey (of The dB’s) at 39th Street Music Studio, NYC. A single, “Diddy Wah Diddy” / “Dead You Me Beside” (FE19), followed in February 1982 — drawn from the LP, not a pre-existing active-band release. Both releases were therefore entirely posthumous.

ZE Records tour sponsorship (February 1980) — distinct from the record deal. In February 1980, ZE Records — the NYC label run by Michael Zilkha and Michel Esteban — sponsored 8 Eyed Spy’s West Coast/California tour. The fromthearchives.org chronology is explicit that “8 Eyed Spy is not on ZE” and the sponsorship was a consequence of Lydia Lunch’s individual ZE contract (her solo LP Queen of Siam was released on ZE on 9 February 1980): ZE agreed to cover tour costs because Lunch was a ZE artist, not because the band had a deal. The tour was reportedly cut short when ZE pulled the plug because the band played none of the Queen of Siam material; ZE was noted as owing the band $4,000, with responsibility attributed to “the band’s tour manager Bob Segerman.” This ZE relationship is separate from and predates the record deal Pat Irwin describes — the deal Irwin mentions was in place by mid-1980, with studio time imminent when Scott died in August 1980, and the eventual release appeared on Fetish, not ZE. [Source: fromthearchives.org — 8 Eyed Spy Concert Chronology]

The Fetish deal could not have been agreed with the band as a going concern; it was negotiated with Lunch (and/or the remaining members) after the band had already dissolved. No source documents who initiated contact or how the arrangement was reached. Several contextual factors are worth noting, however:

  • Lunch’s UK profile. Teenage Jesus and the Jerks received significant John Peel airplay in 1978–80; Peel continued playing 8 Eyed Spy material and revisited it as late as March 1988. [Source: John Peel Wiki — 8 Eyed Spy] This would have made Lunch a known quantity to any attentive London indie operator without Pearce needing to discover her independently.
  • Lunch’s active UK presence 1981–82. By November 1981 she was performing in London (recorded live at The Venue with Steve Severin; released as The Agony Is the Ecstacy on 4AD, February 1982), suggesting she had UK label relationships in place at approximately the same time the Fetish LP appeared. [Source: Wikipedia — Drunk on the Pope’s Blood/The Agony Is the Ecstacy (as of 2026-09-27)]
  • The Stamey/dB’s remix angle. Chris Stamey, who remixed the 8 Eyed Spy LP, was playing NYC-adjacent circles (The dB’s’ first two albums came out on UK Albion Records in 1981, and The dB’s appeared at the Stiff Start Swimming Rainbow Theatre showcase alongside Bush Tetras). This is circumstantial — no source links Stamey to Pearce or to the deal — but the remix credit means someone trusted by Fetish was already in the room for the LP’s post-production.
  • The Bongos/Bush Tetras pattern. Fetish’s other NYC signings (The Bongos, Bush Tetras) were reached through direct scouting of the Maxwell’s/NYC club circuit. Whether Pearce encountered 8 Eyed Spy the same way is unknown; their breakup preceded the LP deal, so any live encounter would predate the deal itself.

Web research (September 2026, updated October 2026, multiple passes) has not produced any source naming who brokered the deal. Paradoxia (Lunch’s 1997 memoir) is not available in digitised or searchable form and was not accessible; it remains an untested source. Rock’s Backpages indexes interviews with Lydia Lunch and 8 Eyed Spy but the archive is paywalled.

ROIR cassette and Chris Stamey. The Live ROIR cassette (A101, May 1981) — a separate posthumous release of live recordings from January–August 1980 — was mixed at Greene St. Recording, mastered at ASR Recording Service, and lists Chris Stamey as having “reprocessed” the live tapes. ROIR was founded in 1979 by Neil Cooper and was New York-based; “Concept By” credit on at least one track goes to Cooper. The ROIR Live cassette therefore predates the Fetish LP (October 1981) and represents a distinct deal — the live material went to a New York label while the studio and mixed-live material went to Fetish in London. Stamey’s involvement in both the ROIR cassette and the Fetish LP (as remixer) is notable: he appears to have been a trusted technical hand for the estate/remaining members across both releases.

The ROIR deal’s provenance (answered October 2026, eighth pass). Neil Cooper ran The ’80s, a live music club on Manhattan’s Upper East Side, where he personally booked Lydia Lunch alongside James Chance, Suicide, Bush Tetras, Bad Brains, and other NYC underground acts. His ROIR acquisition model was to offer advances to acts he already had a booking relationship with in exchange for cassette rights — no broker. Because Cooper was booking Lunch throughout 1979–80, the relationship predates George Scott’s death (5 August 1980). Cooper almost certainly approached Lunch about the cassette deal during the band’s active run or in its immediate aftermath, not through a third party. Chris Stamey’s technical role across both posthumous releases (reprocessing the ROIR tapes; remixing the Fetish LP) is consistent with being the trusted engineer hired after deals were separately arranged, not as a deal-maker linking the two. [Sources: Wikipedia — Neil Cooper (record executive) (as of 2026-10-06); ROIR Wikipedia (as of 2026-10-05); ROIR-USA.com]

One new narrative detail (fromthearchives.com, uncited): The fromthearchives.org concert chronology states that for the October 1980 studio sessions, “the remaining members had convinced Lydia to re-join the band for these sessions,” and separately that “Lydia was not involved in the remixing/compiling process of this album.” These claims carry no cited source, but if accurate they imply that the push to make the LP came from Sclavunos, Irwin, and/or Paumgarten (not from Lunch herself), and that Lunch had largely disengaged from the project by the time it reached Fetish. This would be consistent with the deal being arranged by the remaining members and/or a third party without Lunch’s active involvement. [Source: fromthearchives.org — 8 Eyed Spy Concert Chronology]

Two Pat Irwin quotes speak directly to the deal timeline. First, from a 2019 PleaseKillMe interview: “Somebody wanted to put a record out, and then George died, and that was just it.” Irwin does not name who “somebody” was. Second — and more direct — from a Leah Roseman podcast transcript (April 2023): “8 Eyed Spy had a record deal, but we were just about ready to go into the studio and record, and George died.” The Roseman quote is explicit: a deal was in place and studio time was imminent when Scott died in August 1980. This makes it very likely the October 1980 Blank Tapes session was organised to fulfil a deal already contracted pre-death, not a fresh posthumous arrangement. Whether the counterparty was Fetish or another label, and whether that deal was later renegotiated given the band’s dissolution, remains unstated in either quote.

The Roseman transcript also adds: “Lydia just didn’t want to do a band without George, and I really respect that. At the time, I didn’t understand. I was like, ‘Well, let’s just do it.‘” This clarifies that Irwin was willing to continue but Lunch was not — consistent with the fromthearchives.org claim that the remaining members had to convince Lunch to participate in the October sessions.

[Sources: PleaseKillMe — Pat Irwin interview (Valerie Simadis, 2019); Leah Roseman podcast — Pat Irwin transcript (April 2023)]

The Rainbow Theatre moment (February 1981). On 20 February 1981, Stiff Records organised A New York Band Celebration at the Rainbow Theatre, London — a one-night showcase featuring The Bongos, Bush Tetras, The Raybeats, The dBs, and The Fleshtones, recorded as the compilation Start Swimming (Stiff Records, SINK 1). By this date, Fetish had already signed The Bongos and was working with Bush Tetras. The concert is better understood as a consequence of the NYC–London connections Pearce had already built, not their origin. [Source: 433rpm blog; Bush Tetras Wikipedia (as of 2026-07-15)]

Summary pattern. Pearce appears to have physically travelled to the US — specifically the New Jersey / NYC club scene centred on Maxwell’s — to scout acts directly, rather than working through agents or London intermediaries. The WKGB connection (itself routed through the Devo orbit) gave him a Maxwell’s foothold from which he discovered The Bongos. Bush Tetras were pulled in by the commercial signal of “Too Many Creeps.” The Snatch and 8 Eyed Spy routes remain less documented.

The Closure

By early 1982, 23 Skidoo’s Seven Songs reached #1 on the indie charts — the label’s commercial high point. But Pearce was already closing down, “disheartened at rock ‘n’ roll’s indifference.” The last release was The Last Testament compilation (1983). Fetish was formally dissolved in 1986.

The irony is noted by Keith Laws (who knew Pearce personally): “Fetish’s greatest success was to occur at the point when mogul Rod Pearce was shutting up shop.”

The Last Testament (1983, FR 2011)

The label’s farewell compilation. Sleeve designed by Neville Brody; notes by Jon Savage and Sandy Robertson (described as “goodbye” letters). Full tracklist:

Side A: WKGB — “Non-Stop” / The Bongos — “Nuts and Bolts” / Perry Haines — “What’s Funk?” / 8 Eyed Spy — “Lazy in Love” / Clock DVA — “The Opening” (live) / Z’EV — “Wipe Out” / Stephen Mallinder — “Del Sol”

Side B: Bush Tetras — “Das Ah Riot” / 23 Skidoo — “Last Words” / Clock DVA — “Remain-Remain” (live) / Throbbing Gristle — “Discipline” (live) / 23 Skidoo — “Hawaii 5-0”

After Fetish: Managing King

After Fetish folded, Pearce co-managed King (Coventry) — a Coventry pop group who had a major UK hit with “Love & Pride” (CBS, 1984/85) — alongside Perry Haines, the fashion designer who co-founded i-D magazine with Terry Jones (7). (Perry Haines also appears on The Last Testament with a track called “What’s Funk?” — a connection that predates King.) A striking career turn: from avant-garde industrial to mainstream pop.

Discogs credits on King releases list Perry Haines and Dolphin Lovers Ltd. as management — Dolphin Lovers Ltd. may be the company entity Pearce operated through. Management credits run from the 1984 debut LP through Paul King’s 1990 solo run on Siren Records.

Death in Mexico, 1997

After the music industry, Pearce moved to Mexico to start a motorbike tour business.

In May 1997, his body was found on a beach. He had been “virtually decapitated” and “hacked to death with a machete.” He was 39. Mexican authorities initially ruled it a motorcycle accident.

Friends refused to accept this. They investigated independently and found witnesses who had seen Pearce arguing with two men and a woman, one of whom carried a machete. A woman was heard shouting “kill him.” Witnesses also identified a 4x4 vehicle similar to one owned by the brother of Pearce’s then-girlfriend.

His friends forced a prosecution. Jemima Alaves Dobles — his ex-girlfriend — was charged with murder and spent three years in jail before being acquitted.

The two witnesses who gave evidence were subsequently murdered — shot with a .38 — and their bodies dumped at the same location where Pearce’s body had been found.

A friend, Rod Buchnan, investigated the case and published “Burying the Truth” in The Times. His conclusion: “there are holes in the truth as we see it and there is certainly no happy ending.” A TIME magazine article also covered the case.

Web sources (added 2026-10-06 — ROIR deal provenance, eighth pass)

Key finding: Neil Cooper ran The ’80s club on Manhattan’s Upper East Side and personally booked Lydia Lunch (along with James Chance, Suicide, Bush Tetras, the Fleshtones, Bad Brains, Johnny Thunders, and others) during the 1979–80 period when 8 Eyed Spy were active. His ROIR acquisition strategy was to offer advances to acts he already had a booking relationship with in exchange for cassette rights — no broker, no intermediary. The live recordings (January–August 1980) cover the band’s entire active life; Cooper’s relationship with Lunch predates George Scott’s death (5 August 1980). The ROIR Live cassette deal was almost certainly initiated by Cooper directly, via the existing booking relationship, before or immediately after the band’s dissolution. Chris Stamey as common thread: Stamey reprocessed the ROIR tapes and remixed the Fetish LP, but no source documents him as deal-maker for either release. His role across both packages is consistent with being the trusted technical engineer hired after the two deals were separately arranged.

  • Wikipedia — Neil Cooper (record executive) (as of 2026-10-06) — key source: confirms Cooper booked Lydia Lunch at The ’80s; confirms his acquisition model (“I gave them advances, and they gave me the rights to put out music on cassettes”); names 8 Eyed Spy Live With Lydia Lunch as among ROIR’s first releases. This is the first source to explain the mechanism of the ROIR deal.
  • ROIR-USA.com — label’s current site; confirms founding 1979, first release James Chance (1981); no additional deal detail for 8 Eyed Spy.
  • Chris Stamey — A Spy in the House of Loud (University of Texas Press, 2018) — memoir reviewed; no review or interview found that mentions 8 Eyed Spy or ROIR. Stamey’s memoir focuses on his own songwriting and the dB’s; no-wave production work not covered in accessible excerpts or reviews. [Source: The Current interview, May 2018]
