Live Performance

Pharmakon’s live practice is consistently described across multiple critical accounts as one of the most physically confrontational in contemporary noise music. The core principle is abolishing the performer-audience boundary entirely.

Performance philosophy

Chardiet has articulated the conceptual basis directly: “I have trouble staying on stage because it reinforces the 4th wall that is necessary for entertainment.” She rejects the entertainment framing altogether — “Even though I perform, I don’t think of what I do as entertainment” — describing her intent instead as: “I want to channel energy, sending it back and forth, touching that deep nerve of pre-word thought.” Her position is that “every person in the room is implicated,” denying the passive-observer model. (Elevate Festival interview)

She emphasises the physiological rather than merely psychological dimension: “When you’re playing these frequencies, loops and percussive elements, they’re physically vibrating in your body — in between your ears, through your feet and your stomach, in your gut… it’s not just your brain processing the sounds but your body.” (FACT interview, March 2017)

She also notes the gendered dimension of her physical authority: “I’m five feet tall, and not very physically intimidating… if I was a six foot tall white guy, would I get away with it?” — an awareness she says shapes how audiences receive the confrontation. (The Creative Independent)

Pre-performance, she has described intense anxiety that builds throughout the day and then drops away entirely once music starts: “Everything just goes blank except for the music.” (The Fader, June 2013)

Physical stage setup

She uses two tables: one with keyboards, effects pedals and distortion gear for the core sonic material; a second carrying a heavily damaged sheet metal surface fitted with a clipped microphone running to distortion, reverb and looping pedals — this functions as a primary instrument and is itself physically marked by prior performances. She carries her microphone on a long cord that doubles as a physical tool for audience entanglement. (KEXP live review, Kremwerk, Seattle, February 2015)

Movement and audience contact

Accounts across multiple venues and years are consistent: she leaves the stage area and enters the crowd, encroaching on personal space, pushing against or pulling attendees via the microphone cord, crawling through the venue on all fours, and at times wrapping the cord around audience members to draw them physically across the room.

  • At a basement show reviewed by NOW Magazine (Toronto, S.H.I.B.G.B’s): she “marched through the audience, heaving herself toward us while holding a machine against her chest” and “pushed backwards against strangers while screaming out her soul,” ending the set “on her back on the floor, writhing and wailing.” (NOW Magazine)
  • At Kremwerk, Seattle (February 2015): during the track that opened with “Autoimmune,” she “jumped down into the crowd with her microphone,” screaming “I don’t belong here” while writhing around the feet of audience members, tracing a U-shaped path through the packed room and nearly causing a crowd scramble to raise her mic cord. (KEXP)
  • At Oberlin College’s ‘Sco (reviewed by Oberlin Review): “circled around the ‘Sco on all fours and clawed at attendees’ legs,” positioned herself inches from spectators to scream directly in their faces, and wrapped the mic cord around audience members. (Oberlin Review)
  • At a gallery concert reviewed by Spectrum Culture (January 2015): “walked out into the audience, encroaching on others’ space, pushing and pulling them, crawling and slithering through the sea of legs like a snake” while weaponising the mic cord to pull people physically. (Spectrum Culture)

Sound and volume

Sets are built from amplified sheet metal, keyboard-generated drone, looped phrases that become hypnotic mantras, and screams that sustain for minutes at painful volumes — the Spectrum Culture review notes attendees wearing ear protection and some leaving. Her vocals combine heavily reverbed chanting with “demonic shrieking”; she has spoken about the value of vocal imperfection: “the times when the voice cracks and you can hear vocal cords being stressed or broken — those ugly, awkward moments are as important” as anything else in the set. (The Fader)

Audience responses

Reviewers note that initial hesitation typically gives way to physical engagement — the NOW Toronto review records the crowd moving from stillness to body-slamming. Many audience members approach Chardiet after shows in a processing state; she describes them saying things like “This fucked me up, basically, and I need to talk to you about what it made me think of.” Some respond through heckling, which she characterises as “the verbal equivalent of nervous laughter.” (The Creative Independent)

Contact (2017) — conceptual link to live practice

The album was structured around four stages of trance and research into out-of-body states across cultures, specifically as an attempt to bring the immersive physiological experience of live performance into recorded listening. She also performed at CTM Festival at Berghain, Berlin, around this release. (The Quietus interview; FACT)

Devour (2019) — live energy captured in studio

For Devour Chardiet recorded a live studio session specifically to replicate concert energy — more urgent vocal delivery, primal and impulsive. She framed it as capturing “the energy exchange that happens between the audience and the performer, and the physicality of the amp in the room affecting people’s bodies.” (FACT interview; SPIN, 2019)
