Angus MacLise

Percussionist, poet. Discogs artist ID: 65710.

Original drummer of The Velvet Underground (pre-recording lineup, 1964–1965); also a member of Theatre Of Eternal Music.

Overview

MacLise was the Velvet Underground’s first drummer, brought in through his connection to the NYC avant-garde and La Monte Young’s milieu. He left the band before they began paid performances — reportedly refusing on principle to play for money. He was replaced by Maureen Tucker in 1965. He never officially recorded with the band.

MacLise was also a percussionist in Theatre Of Eternal Music alongside John Cale, La Monte Young, and Tony Conrad. He was also a poet and associated with the Fugs and underground literary scene. He died in Kathmandu in 1979.

Notes on the record

The Discogs catalog is almost entirely posthumous and archival — MacLise died in 1979, so all dated commercial releases (1987 onward) are archival compilations, reissues, or recordings made by others using his material. The earliest dated release is the 1970 Aspen No. 9 co-editing credit. The Inside The Dream Syndicate album documents 1965 Theatre of Eternal Music sessions; New York Electronic, 1965 similarly captures mid-1960s recordings. The Dreamweapon series (I–IV, Boo-Hooray, 2011) accompanied the exhibition of his archive.

Literary Activities

MacLise was a significant figure in the underground literary scene across three decades and two continents, as poet, editor, typographer, and small-press publisher.

Pre-Dead Language Press: Imprimatur 1281 (White Plains, 1957)

Before the Dead Language Press was formally established, MacLise and Piero Heliczer co-produced imprimatur m.cc.lxxx.i the completed works of angus maclise (White Plains, 1957) — a letterpress publication predating MacLise’s Paris period. A facsimile of the title poem was reprinted in the deluxe edition of the 2011 Boo-Hooray exhibition catalog Dreamweapon. The original is described as “unobtainable.” [source: deadlanguagepress.wordpress.com; arcanabooks.com/catalog/book/024648/]

Dead Language Press (Paris / New York, 1958–c.1962)

MacLise co-founded the Dead Language Press in Paris in 1958 with his high school friend, avant-garde filmmaker Piero Heliczer. The press published poetry in letterpress editions and issued work by Gregory Corso, Anselm Hollo, Olivia de Haulleville, Hendrik Jan Marsman, and Jack Smith alongside MacLise’s own writing. When both men returned to New York in the early 1960s the press continued there. Other Dead Language Press titles (not MacLise-authored) include Anselm Hollo’s loverman (1963) and Konrad Bayer / Oswald Wiener’s starker toback (Paris, 1963). [source: deadlanguagepress.wordpress.com]

Key Dead Language Press publications by or co-edited by MacLise:

  • Straight Farthest Blood Towards (Opening Section) (Paris: The Dead Language Press, 1959) — folded letterpress sheet (6.25” × 22”), MacLise’s debut publication. [source: verdantpress.com/checklist/angus-maclise/books-and-broadsides/]
  • Wednesday Paper (co-edited with Piero Heliczer; New York: The Dead Language Press, c.1961) — anthology featuring Corso, Anselm Hollo, Gustav Schiele. [source: verdantpress.com]
  • Year, A Wednesday Paper Supplement (New York: The Dead Language Press, 1961) — accordion-fold broadside, twelve panels (4.6” × 9”), poetically renaming every day of the calendar year; letterpress by Heliczer, cover after Aubrey Beardsley. [source: verdantpress.com/checklist/angus-maclise/books-and-broadsides/]
  • The Beautiful Book by Jack Smith (New York: The Dead Language Press, 1962) — printed by Heliczer; MacLise involved as pressman. [source: verdantpress.com]

Fluxus and George Maciunas (New York, 1960s)

MacLise was a regular participant in Fluxus events in New York and contributed to the early Fluxus newspaper cc V TRE (edited by George Brecht and George Maciunas), which featured over sixty artists including Yoko Ono and Nam June Paik. [source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLise, as of 2025-07-13]

The Fluxus connection also produced one of his most substantial visual-literary works:

  • The New Universal Solar Calendar (New York: produced by George Maciunas, 1969) — large broadside (20.5” × 23.5”), black calligraphy and illustrations offset printed on white stock; layout by Paolo Lionni. Only one OCLC-located copy, now at Columbia University. Continues the renaming-of-days theme from Year. [source: biblio.com; exhibitions.lib.udel.edu/beat-visions-and-the-counterculture/]

New Cinema Festival: Rites of the Dreamweapon (New York, 1965)

MacLise’s multimedia piece Rites of the Dreamweapon was presented at the New Cinema Festival I (also called the Expanded Cinema Festival) at Filmmaker’s Cinematheque, New York, 1965 — organized by Jonas Mekas and featuring work by Robert Rauschenberg, Claes Oldenburg, Nam June Paik, and others. The program was co-presented with Jack Smith.

Jonas Mekas reviewed the opening programs in the Village Voice: “The first three programs of the New Cinema Festival — the work of Angus McLise [sic], Nam June Paik, and Jerry Joffen [sic] — dissolved the edges of this art called cinema into a frontiersland mystery.” The piece also made a lasting impression on playwright Richard Foreman, who later cited it in interviews. [source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLise, as of 2025-07-13; typepunchmatrix.com/pages/books/44185/]

Richard Foreman citation — research note (2026-07-24):

The Wikipedia article on MacLise (footnote 18) claims Foreman “praised it years later in an interview,” citing: Kate Davy, Richard Foreman and the Ontological-Hysteric Theatre (Ann Arbor: UMI Research Press, 1981, ISBN 978-0835712200), pp. 138–139. This is a critical study of Foreman’s theater that incorporates extended interviews/conversations with him.

The Foreman passage at those pages — recoverable via warholstars.org (which uses the abbreviation “DJ” for David E. James’s anthology, cross-referencing the same content) — reads: “During the second half of the sixties, when I was spending anywhere from four to seven nights a week at the Film-Makers’ Cinematheque, not only was a vast quantity of film discovered and shown, but a significant number of theatrical events were staged… What impressed me most — what has stayed in my memory most clearly — were the events, the ‘theater pieces,’ that in one way or another partook of the aesthetic of Jack Smith.” [source: warholstars.org/expanded_cinema.html, citing Davy (1981) pp. 138–139]

Caution: The recoverable Foreman text praises Jack Smith’s aesthetic at the festival generally, not Rites of the Dreamweapon or MacLise by name. Whether Foreman names MacLise specifically elsewhere in the Davy book, or whether the Wikipedia claim is an over-reading of Foreman’s general praise for the festival’s theatrical events, cannot be confirmed without physical access to Davy (1981) pp. 138–139.

A near-identical Foreman passage appears in his essay “During the Second Half of the Sixties” in: David E. James (ed.), To Free the Cinema: Jonas Mekas and the New York Underground (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1992), pp. 138–139 — the warholstars citation key “DJ” decodes to this James anthology (confirmed at warholstars.org/sources.html). The passage is consistent across both sources. [source: warholstars.org/sources.html; press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691023458/to-free-the-cinema]

Verification needed: Obtain physical copy (or interlibrary loan scan) of Kate Davy, Richard Foreman and the Ontological-Hysteric Theatre (1981), pp. 138–139, to confirm whether Foreman names MacLise or Rites of the Dreamweapon by name. Columbia RBML (MacLise archive) or NYU Fales Library (Richard Foreman and Kate Manheim Papers, MSS 152) may hold relevant primary material. [NYU finding aid: findingaids.library.nyu.edu/fales/mss_152/]

The festival program itself is a collectible item: New Cinema Festival I at Filmmaker’s Cinematheque Presents Rites of the Dreamweapon (1965), listed as a rare artifact by booksellers including Boo-Hooray and Type Punch Matrix.

Exploding Plastic Inevitable / Warhol Multimedia (1966)

According to Sterling Morrison, Andy Warhol’s multimedia performance format — “Andy Warhol Uptight” and the Exploding Plastic Inevitable — was directly modeled on earlier “ritual happenings” developed by MacLise and Heliczer, an influence Warhol’s circle absorbed through the shared downtown scene. [source: pleasekillme.com/angus-maclise/; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLise, as of 2025-07-13]

MacLise also rejoined the Velvet Underground briefly in June 1966 (during Lou Reed’s hospitalization for hepatitis) for a five-night run at Poor Richard’s in Chicago, June 21–26, as part of the Exploding Plastic Inevitable tour. [source: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLise, as of 2025-07-13]

Aspen Magazine No. 9 (Winter-Spring 1971)

MacLise and his wife Hetty MacLise co-edited Aspen No. 9 (“Dreamweapon”), the iconic “magazine-in-a-box.” The issue was devoted to psychedelic and countercultural art and literature; Hetty designed the cover. Published by Roaring Fork Press, NYC; contains thirteen numbered items, no advertisements. [source: anothermag.com/art-photography/7220/aspen-magazine-a-surprise-box-of-delights; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_MacLise, as of 2025-07-13]

Date note: The colophon does not print a year (hence bibliographic convention of bracketed inferred date). UbuWeb’s digitization of the actual issues cites “Winter-Spring [1971]” — i.e., inferred as 1971. Bookseller listings (AbeBooks, Arcana Books, PicClick) consistently describe it as “Winter/Spring 1970.” The discrepancy reflects the seasonal designation straddling the 1970/71 calendar boundary: “Winter” of 1970/71 begins in December 1970, so both labels describe the same object. UbuWeb’s bibliographic judgment is 1971 as the cover-date year; 1970 as used by booksellers refers to the winter (December) start of that season. Wikipedia’s Aspen (magazine) article confirms No. 10 was published in 1971 as the final issue, which is consistent with No. 9 being Winter-Spring 1971 immediately before it. [source: ubu.com/aspen/aspen9/ (Winter-Spring [1971]); en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspen_(magazine) (as of 2026-07-19); arcanabooks.com/catalog/book/023495/ (1970); abebooks.com listing (1970)]

Kathmandu Period: Dreamweapon Press and Bardo Matrix (1972–1979)

After traveling through Canada, France, Greece, and India, Angus and Hetty MacLise settled in Kathmandu in the early 1970s. MacLise helped operate the Spirit Catcher Bookshop on Jhochhen Tole (“Freak Street”), which functioned as a weekly forum for poetry readings, music, and community from approximately 1972 to 1979. [source: kathmandupost.com/miscellaneous/2018/12/22/catching-spirits]

MacLise founded Ting Pa, a bilingual (Tibetan/English) literary and poetry journal printed on handmade paper folios, published in Kathmandu. A complete run is preserved in collections; it is considered extremely rare. [source: granarybooks.cdn.bibliopolis.com/images/upload/bardomatrixcompleteillustr-final.pdf]

In 1974 MacLise co-founded the Bardo Matrix publishing venture in Kathmandu with poet and photographer Ira Cohen. The collective initially also included John Chick and Dana Young. Bardo Matrix published poetry pamphlets, books, and broadsides letterpress-printed on handmade Nepali paper, typically in editions of 50–500 copies. MacLise’s own imprint, Dreamweapon Press, operated alongside it.

Poetry Performance

MacLise gave a live poetry reading on September 17, 1976 in New York City at the “Millennium Poetry and Multimedia Performance,” reading poems to a backdrop of Nepalese music he had recorded in Kathmandu. The recording was released posthumously as The Kathmandu Cycle on cassette by Counter Culture Chronicles / Sea Urchin Editions. [source: thewire.co.uk/news/53971/1976-poetry-reading-velvet-underground-angus-maclise]

Invasion of Thunderbolt Pagoda Score (1968)

MacLise scored and performed in Ira Cohen’s 1968 psychedelic short film The Invasion of Thunderbolt Pagoda. The soundtrack was performed by the Universal Mutant Repertory Company, whose confirmed personnel include: Angus MacLise, Tony Conrad, Henry Flynt, Jackson Mac Low, Raja Samayana, Loren Standlee, and Ziska Baum. The ensemble also performed as The Joyous Lake on some documentation. The score features improvised percussion, pan-piping, tabla, flute, organ, guitar, tambura, dulcimer, and vocals. [source: blogthehum.com/2016/05/25/the-non-musical-works-of-angus-maclise/; arika.org.uk/invasion-thunderbolt-pagoda/]

Gnaoua Magazine — Clarification

The Ira Cohen collection at Granary Books is described as “Bardo Matrix, Gnaoua, and The Great Society featuring Angus MacLise, Jack Smith, and Piero Heliczer,” which has sometimes implied MacLise contributed to Gnaoua No. 1 (Tangier, Spring 1964). He did not: the full contributor list of Gnaoua 1 — Burroughs, Ginsberg, Gysin, Michael McClure, Harold Norse, Brion Gysin, Ian Sommerville, Irving Rosenthal, Jack Smith, and others — does not include MacLise. The Granary description refers to the collection as a whole (which also contains Bardo Matrix and The Great Society material featuring MacLise), not to Gnaoua specifically. [source: fromasecretlocation.com/gnaoua/; granarybooks.com]

The Great Society Magazine — Clarification

The Great Society was a two-issue small-press literary magazine co-edited by Ira Cohen and Robert Richkin (spelled “Rickin” in No. 1):

  • No. 1 (1966): published by Tompkins Square Books, New York. Cover illustration by Jack Smith. Contributors include Piero Heliczer, Ray and Bonnie Bremser, Allen Ginsberg, Clive Matson, Andrew Heath, and others. [source: verdantpress.com/checklist/ira-cohen/publications-edited-printed-and-published/]
  • No. 2 (1967): published by A Heddaoua Publication, New York. Cover illustration by Jack Smith. Extensive contributor list includes William Burroughs, Paul Bowles, John Wieners, Harold Norse, Janine Pommy-Vega, Piero Heliczer, Paolo Lionni, Jean-Jacques Lebel, Jack Smith, and others. [source: verdantpress.com/checklist/ira-cohen/publications-edited-printed-and-published/]

Angus MacLise does not appear in the contributor lists for either issue (per Verdant Press checklist, which is the authoritative bibliography for both Cohen and MacLise publications). The Granary Books collection title — “Bardo Matrix, Gnaoua, and The Great Society featuring Angus MacLise, Jack Smith, and Piero Heliczer” — names the notable figures associated with the collection as a whole, not contributors to The Great Society specifically. Jack Smith and Piero Heliczer both appear in both issues; MacLise’s presence in the Granary collection derives from the Bardo Matrix component, not from The Great Society. This is the same structural ambiguity as the Gnaoua attribution (see above). [source: verdantpress.com/checklist/ira-cohen/publications-edited-printed-and-published/; verdantpress.com/checklist/angus-maclise/contributions-to-periodicals/; granarybooks.com/pages/books/1926/ira-cohen-collection-with-bardo-matrix-press-gnaoua-and-the-great-society-featuring-angus-maclise]

Fugs / Underground Scene Note

The existing Discogs-sourced reference in this file to MacLise being “associated with the Fugs” reflects shared scene participation — both MacLise and Ed Sanders were part of the same Lower East Side/downtown New York underground in the mid-1960s — rather than documented direct collaboration or membership. A 1965 happening flier documents Warhol, Burroughs, Ginsberg, the Fugs, and MacLise at the same eight-hour event, confirming they moved in overlapping circles; no primary source confirming MacLise performed or recorded with the Fugs has been located. [source: dangerousminds.net/comments/angus_maclise_original_velvet_underground_drummer/]

Archives

Columbia University, Rare Book and Manuscript Library — The primary MacLise archive. The bulk of the materials were given by Hetty MacLise to La Monte Young for safekeeping in 1980 and remained in Young’s possession until acquired by Manhattan’s Boo-Hooray Gallery for the 2011 exhibition Dreamweapon: The Art and Life of Angus MacLise 1938–1979. After the exhibition, Johan Kugelberg of Boo-Hooray transferred the collection to Columbia (c.2012). Contents: poetry manuscripts and typescripts, prose, lists, theatrical pieces, stage designs, text fragments, calligraphy, artwork, correspondence, photographs, event flyers, artists’ books, journals, and ephemera; most handwritten material dates from the 1970s, publications covered up to the 1990s. [source: thewire.co.uk/news/23997/angus-maclise-archive-acquired-by-columbia-university; findingaids.library.columbia.edu/ead/nnc-rb/ldpd_10260984]

Finding aid: https://findingaids.library.columbia.edu/ead/nnc-rb/ldpd_10260984

Piero Heliczer and Dead Language Press Archive, Princeton University — Holds material from the press MacLise co-founded; relevant for the early 1958–1962 publications. [source: boo-hooray.com/pages/archives/128/piero-heliczer-dead-language-press-archive]

Ira Cohen Collection (Granary Books / various) — Cohen’s archive includes Bardo Matrix material, Gnaoua magazine, and items featuring MacLise. [source: granarybooks.com/pages/books/1926/ira-cohen-collection-with-bardo-matrix-press-gnaoua-and-the-great-society-featuring-angus-maclise]
