Jonathan Richman

Jonathan Richman

Oct 15, 202612 min read

Jonathan Richman

Discogs ID: 237653
Born: May 16, 1951, Natick, Massachusetts, USA
Real name: Jonathan Michael Richman

American singer-songwriter and guitarist. Founder of The Modern Lovers (1970–1974), whose self-titled debut (recorded 1972–1973, released 1976) is a foundational proto-punk/post-Velvet Underground document. After disbanding the original lineup, Richman pivoted sharply toward a gentler, more whimsical acoustic style — a direction he has pursued, with considerable consistency and periodic oddness, ever since. He has recorded and released new material continuously from the mid-1970s through 2025. (Wikipedia: Jonathan Richman)

Richman is also credited with recommending Doug Yule to The Velvet Underground when they needed a replacement for John Cale in 1968.

Early Life and The Velvet Underground Obsession

Richman grew up in Natick, Massachusetts (a Boston suburb) and became infatuated with The Velvet Underground while still in high school. After graduating in 1969, he moved to New York City, where he spent time on Velvets manager Steve Sesnick’s sofa and lived briefly in the Hotel Albert before returning to Boston. He opened a show for the Velvets on at least one occasion during this New York period. (Wikipedia)

The Modern Lovers (1970–1974)

Back in Boston, Richman formed The Modern Lovers with childhood friend John Felice (guitar), David Robinson (3) (drums), and Rolfe Anderson (bass). Their first gig was in September 1970, supporting Andy Paley’s band the Sidewinders. The setlist already included “Roadrunner,” “She Cracked,” and “Hospital.”

By early 1971, Anderson and Felice had departed; they were replaced by Harvard students Ernie Brooks (bass) and Jerry Harrison (keyboards), completing the classic lineup. The band became popular in the Boston area and attracted major-label interest from Warner Bros. and A&M.

In April 1972, the Modern Lovers traveled to Los Angeles for two demo sessions: one produced by John Cale for Warner Bros. and one produced by Allan Mason for A&M. These recordings — raw, Velvet Underground-influenced three-chord rock at a time when glam and prog dominated — eventually became the The Modern Lovers LP. A second session produced by Kim Fowley in Boston (June 1972) yielded lower-quality demos eventually released as The Original Modern Lovers (1981). On New Year’s Eve 1972, the Modern Lovers supported the New York Dolls at the Mercer Arts Center on a bill that also featured Suicide and Wayne County. (Wikipedia)

In early 1973, after playing a residency at the Inverurie Hotel in Bermuda — where Richman was strongly influenced by the laid-back local music — he began pushing for a mellower, more lyrical direction. This stylistic divergence, combined with growing personality clashes, led to the band’s dissolution in February 1974. By this point, Richman wanted to scrap the existing recorded tracks and start fresh. Harrison went on to join Talking Heads; Brooks relocated to New York. (Wikipedia)

Jonathan Richman & The Modern Lovers (1976–1988)

In 1975, Richman moved to California and signed with the independent Beserkley label (run by Matthew King Kaufman). His first Beserkley recordings appeared on the 1975 compilation Beserkley Chartbusters, where he was backed by members of Earth Quake and the Rubinoos.

In January 1976, Richman assembled a new Modern Lovers: original drummer David Robinson (3), former Rubinoos bassist Greg “Curly” Keranen, and Leroy Radcliffe on guitar. This incarnation, billed as Jonathan Richman & The Modern Lovers, turned away from the electric Velvet Underground sound toward a gentler mix of 1950s rock and roll, harmony vocals, and whimsical themes — Martians, leprechauns, the Abominable Snowman, mosquitoes. The album Jonathan Richman and the Modern Lovers was released in May 1976, three months before the original 1972 demos finally appeared as The Modern Lovers.

Robinson soon departed to join Ric Ocasek in forming The Cars, frustrated by Richman’s insistence on lower volume levels. He was replaced by D. Sharpe, an avant-garde jazz drummer from the Boston scene who later played with pianist Carla Bley. (Wikipedia)

The 1977 single “Egyptian Reggae” (an instrumental based on Jamaican musician Earl Zero’s song “None Shall Escape the Judgement”) reached number 5 in the UK Singles Chart; “Roadrunner” had previously reached number 11. Richman was embraced in the UK as a punk progenitor even as his music was moving in the opposite direction. Rock ‘n’ Roll with the Modern Lovers (1977) climbed the European charts.

After Back in Your Life (Beserkley, 1979) — half solo, half with band — Richman went on sabbatical, staying in Appleton, Maine and playing local bars in Belfast, Maine for a few years.

He returned to recording in 1981 with a series of fluid lineups under the Modern Lovers name, including Keranen again plus drummer Michael Guardabascio, keyboardist Ken Forfia, vocalist/guitarist Ellie Marshall, and vocalist Beth Harrington. From 1981–1984, most live work was a trio with Keranen and Marshall. From 1985, it was bassist Asa Brebner and drummer Andy Paley. From 1986–1988: guitarist Brennan Totten and drummer Johnny Avila.

Signing with Rounder Records in 1987, Richman recorded Modern Lovers 88 — his final album under the Modern Lovers name — produced by Brennan Totten at Bennett House, Grass Valley, California. After this, the “Modern Lovers” moniker was retired. (Wikipedia)

Solo Career (1988–present)

From 1988 to 1992, Richman performed mostly solo. His Rounder albums from this period — Jonathan Richman (1989), Jonathan Goes Country (1990), Having A Party With Jonathan Richman (1991) — were produced by Brennan Totten. Having A Party… was released in the UK on Cheree Records (cat. CHEREE22).

Around the time of I, Jonathan (1992, Rounder Records), Richman formed a long-running performance duo with drummer Tommy Larkins (Giant Sand, Yard Trauma, Naked Prey). Larkins would continue to play and record with Richman for more than 30 years. Engineer Ned Claflin and guitarist John Girton were also regulars across the 1990s–2000s Rounder period.

The 1993 track “Hot Nights” appeared on the AIDS-benefit compilation No Alternative, produced by the Red Hot Organization. (Wikipedia)

Richman signed to Neil Young’s Vapor Records for Surrender to Jonathan (1996), where he worked with Andy Paley as producer and an array of session players including H.B. Barnum (orchestrations) and accordion player Frank Marocco. He continued on Vapor through Her Mystery Not Of High Heels And Eye Shadow (2001) and Not So Much To Be Loved As To Love (2004).

He appeared in the Farrelly Brothers film There’s Something About Mary (1998), playing himself as a Greek chorus figure. (Wikipedia)

From 2016 onward, releases have appeared on his own Blue Arrow Records: Ishkode! Ishkode! (2016), SA! (2018), Want To Visit My Inner House? (2022), Yatasamaroun (2023), and Only Frozen Sky Anyway (2025). This period has brought in collaborators including Jerry Harrison (returning on SA!), Jake Sprecher, and Nicole Montalbano (Richman’s wife since 2003, credited on several releases).

Richman was previously married to Gail Donahue (1982–circa 1996). His daughter Jenny Rae Richman has appeared in credits on later releases.

Retrospective Perspectives: Original Members on the Band Dynamic

All surviving members of the classic 1971–1974 lineup who have spoken on record converge on a consistent view: the Modern Lovers was Richman’s creative vehicle, not a democracy, though the members contributed meaningfully to execution and arrangement.

John Felice (guitar, early lineup)

The bluntest statement on record comes from original guitarist John Felice: “You’ve got to remember: The Modern Lovers was always Jonathan’s band.” In the same interview Felice notes that Richman “had a real problem in giving up any of that territory” when other members wanted to play songs Felice had written — a friction point that contributed to Felice’s own departure. Felice says the remaining members (Brooks, Harrison, Robinson) all wanted to play his songs, but Richman resisted. (boredout305.tumblr.com/post/126122892688/john-felice-interview-part-two)

Jerry Harrison (keyboards)

Harrison’s accounts consistently emphasise Richman’s personal philosophy as inseparable from the band’s music: “It was not an act in the slightest. It was him. He had this ethos, this entire belief system about how to live one’s life.” On Richman’s songwriting: “His songwriting was very informed by his visual imagery. It was very much a part of the mythology he was trying to create.” Harrison describes joining after Richman came to his Harvard apartment “raving about the Velvet Underground and the Stooges” and being drawn to Richman’s “confrontational” musical philosophy — which then shifted toward a quieter direction mid-recording, which Harrison found “frustrating.” (Tape Op #153, tapeop.com/interviews/153/jerry-harrison; Rolling Stone, rollingstone.com/music/music-news/modern-lovers-roadrunner-proposed-as-massachusetts-official-rock-song-247992/)

Harrison has maintained an active relationship with Richman in later years — he contributed to SA! (2018, Blue Arrow Records) and co-produced Richman’s 2025 album Only Frozen Sky Anyway. (WTF with Marc Maron, Episode 1351, shows.acast.com/wtf-with-marc-maron-podcast/episodes/episode-1351-jerry-harrison)

Ernie Brooks (bass)

Brooks gives the most nuanced account. He acknowledges Richman’s primary creative authorship — “Jonathan definitely wrote the lyrics to ‘Roadrunner,’ and we did the arrangement” — but emphasises that the band shaped the material significantly, starting from ideas that were “pretty simple. Mostly on one chord.” Crucially, he recalls a formal agreement the band made early on: all songs would be published collectively as “The Modern Lovers,” splitting publishing rights equally, “kind of like the Ramones” — a way to recoup expenses and acknowledge collective contribution. Brooks describes the breakup friction as Richman refusing to perform older songs live and believing that electric amplification caused harm: “There’s something wrong in profiting from all these things.” Brooks’s own position was pragmatic: “We are a functioning band, you know? We’re not going to be so particular; we’ll do something if people like it.” He recalls never fully falling out with Richman personally: “I never said, ‘Fuck you, Jonathan!‘” (Please Kill Me / Legs McNeil interview, 2017, pleasekillme.com/modern-lovers-bassist-ernie-brooks/; FFanzeen, 2010, ffanzeen.blogspot.com/2010/04/ernie-brooks-lover-looks-back-at-roots.html)

David Robinson (drums)

Robinson’s retrospective view is the most admiring. He characterises Richman as possessing “a kind of obsessive integrity,” placing “so much faith in his own feelings about what mattered” — a commitment to principle that sometimes worked against commercial success (including challenging whether the band still believed in their own material when Warner Bros. came calling). Robinson views the Modern Lovers as a more artistically significant body of work than The Cars: “In a lot of ways The Modern Lovers was way more interesting than The Cars. It was a much more groundbreaking and inspiring sort of story.” Looking back: “When I listen to The Modern Lovers now, I’m astounded by our audacity.” (theothercape.com/blog/david-robinson; mmone.org/david-robinson/)

Summary

All four members who have spoken on record describe the band as Richman’s project in terms of creative ownership and songs. The collective publishing arrangement (all songs credited to The Modern Lovers, equal splits) was a practical accommodation rather than a reflection of co-writing. The band’s role was arrangement and execution; Richman controlled material, direction, and what the band would and would not perform. Members’ frustration at the 1974 dissolution stemmed not from disputes about creative credit but from Richman’s unilateral decision to abandon the electric rock sound entirely and refuse to perform previously recorded material. Post-split accounts from all three surviving members are retrospectively warm rather than bitter, and Harrison’s ongoing collaboration with Richman (2018, 2025) underlines that the personal relationships outlasted the stylistic conflict.
