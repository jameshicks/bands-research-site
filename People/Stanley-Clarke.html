Stanley Clarke

Jazz-fusion electric bassist, acoustic bassist, composer, and film scorer. Born June 30, 1951, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Discogs artist ID: 87519. Real name: Stanley Marvin Clarke.

One of the first jazz musicians to elevate the electric bass as a front-line solo instrument. Co-founder of Return To Forever with Chick Corea (1972). His self-titled 1974 Atlantic/Nemperor debut and School Days (1976) redefined what was possible for the bass guitar in jazz and rock contexts. Active across six decades with a discography spanning jazz fusion, funk, R&B, classical crossover, and film scores.

Overview

Early Career (1971–1973)

Clarke came up in the early 1970s Philadelphia and New York jazz scenes, passing through groups led by Joe Henderson, Art Blakey, and Stan Getz before landing with Chick Corea. His debut as a leader — Children of Forever (Polydor, 1973) — featured Chick Corea on electric piano, Lenny White on drums, Pat Martino on guitar, Dee Dee Bridgewater and Andy Bey on vocals, and Chick Corea producing. It was a relatively conventional post-bop-fusion album but foreshadowed his ambitions.

Simultaneously, Clarke was a founding member of Return To Forever, Chick Corea’s pivotal fusion band. The early RTF lineup (Light as a Feather, 1973; Hymn of the Seventh Galaxy, 1973) also included Airto Moreira, Flora Purim, and Joe Farrell, then shifted to a harder electric sound with Bill Connors and Lenny White for the Hymn of the Seventh Galaxy–era band. Al Di Meola replaced Connors from No Mystery (1975) onward.

Nemperor / Atlantic Era (1974–1977)

Clarke’s self-titled 1974 Atlantic/Nemperor debut established the template: aggressive acoustic and electric bass playing, rock and jazz textures mixed freely, and an all-star cast. Tony Williams played drums; Bill Connors played guitar; Jan Hammer contributed keyboards and Moog synthesizer; a large NYC brass section filled out the arrangements. Clarke produced and arranged the record himself.

Journey to Love (1975) brought in Jeff Beck on guitar for one track, alongside George Duke on keyboards, David Sancious on guitar and keys, and Steve Gadd on drums. Engineer Ken Scott (who had worked with Bowie and the Beatles) was a constant presence across these early albums.

School Days (1976) became his most celebrated record — a ferocious showcase for the electric bass as lead instrument, with John McLaughlin on acoustic guitar, Billy Cobham and Steve Gadd sharing drum duties, George Duke on keys, and David Sancious on guitar and synthesizer. The title track is among the most covered bass showcases in jazz-rock history.

Epic / Nemperor Era and Crossover (1978–1989)

Moving further into pop and funk territory, Clarke signed to Epic (Nemperor remained a subsidiary imprint through Epic). Modern Man (1978) was a slick, synthesizer-heavy funk-jazz effort, self-produced and self-arranged. The live double album I Wanna Play for You (1979) captured the touring band of the period.

Rocks, Pebbles and Sand (1980) and Let Me Know You (1982) continued in an R&B-jazz crossover vein, featuring large studio ensembles with LA-session brass and strings. Time Exposure (1984) brought in Stewart Copeland and other guest players. Hideaway (1986) featured Stevie Wonder, Larry Graham, and Howard Hewett, deepening the commercial R&B direction.

If This Bass Could Only Talk (1988, Portrait) was a return to more jazz-rooted territory.

Also during this period: Clarke co-led the Griffith Park Collection sessions (Elektra Musician, 1982–83) with Freddie Hubbard, Joe Henderson, Chick Corea, and Lenny White, forming a significant acoustic jazz supergroup project. The Echoes of an Era series (Elektra, 1982) with Chaka Khan on vocals also came from this circle.

The Stanley Clarke / George Duke Partnership

Clarke and George Duke developed a recurring collaboration outside of their respective solo careers. The duo recorded jointly as Stanley Clarke/George Duke and co-wrote material throughout the mid-to-late 1970s (see Journey to Love, School Days). Their shared catalog runs to approximately 90 releases in the Discogs database — among the most sustained musical partnerships in Clarke’s career.

Film Scoring (1990s onward)

Clarke made a significant move into film and TV scoring from the early 1990s. He scored Passenger 57 (1992), the Boyz n the Hood soundtrack elements, The Five Heartbeats, Higher Learning, and Barbershop: The Next Cut (2016), among many others. His East River Drive (Epic, 1993) reflected this period’s rhythmic sophistication.

Later Work (1995–2020)

The Rite of Strings (1995) was a high-profile acoustic collaboration with Al Di Meola and Jean-Luc Ponty, revisiting the fusion era’s acoustic jazz-guitar language. Clarke played acoustic bass throughout; the record placed him squarely back in the jazz conversation.

The Toys of Men (2007, Heads Up) and The Playmaker (2009) continued his fusion work into the 21st century. D-Stringz (2015, Impulse!) focused on acoustic bass, and Thunder (2020, Dreyfus Jazz) marked his most recent major release.

Clarke has been associated with Animal Logic (late 1980s new-wave fusion group with Stewart Copeland and vocalist Deborah Holland), S. M. V. (bass trio with Marcus Miller and Victor Wooten), and The Stanley Clarke Band.
