Collaborated with Pascal Comelade on Le Plan De Paris (cougouyou 16, 2020) on Cougouyou Music.

Philosophical Influences: Deleuze, Lyotard, and the Music

Academic Background

Pinhas completed a PhD in Philosophy at the Paris-Sorbonne in 1974 and taught philosophy there for one year before committing fully to Heldon. His doctoral dissertation examined connections between science fiction, the unconscious, and analogue electronic music — threading together Frank Herbert’s Dune, temporal theory, and sonic practice. source: cyclicdefrost.com

During his years at Paris VIII (Vincennes) and the Sorbonne, Pinhas immersed himself in the core poststructuralist figures of the era. In his own words: “I’ve done studies of philosophy at university for 17 years. I saw Foucault on stage like a rock star, Deleuze and Lacan too.” He describes studying “on Tuesday with Deleuze, on Wednesday with Lyotard,” as part of an intensive philosophical formation centred on Freud, Nietzsche, and Marx. source: Red Bull Music Academy interview, 2015

Deleuze: Direct Collaboration and Conceptual Crossover

Pinhas’s relationship with Deleuze went beyond student-teacher to lifelong friendship and artistic collaboration. In 1972, Deleuze appeared in the studio during the recording of “Le Voyageur” by Pinhas’s pre-Heldon group Schizo, reading an excerpt from Nietzsche’s Human, All Too Human (“The Wanderer,” aphorism 638) in spoken voice over Pinhas’s bolero-like guitar backing. The track was later re-recorded as “Ouais, Marchais, Mieux Qu’en 68 (Ex: Le Voyageur)” for Heldon’s debut album Electronique Guerilla (1974). source: substack/@infinitespeeds; source: jahsonic.com

Pinhas attended all of Deleuze’s seminars from 1970 to 1987. When musical questions arose in Deleuze’s seminars, Deleuze regularly deferred to Pinhas as the musical interlocutor. source: Red Bull Music Academy interview

On 3 August 1977, Pinhas co-presented a session at Vincennes with Deleuze on the philosophy of music. In it, Pinhas developed a theory of “metallic synthesis” — a compositional mode that mixes Chronos (clock time) and Aion (intensive time) through continuous displacement of accent structures. He distinguished two schools: Schoenberg’s serial approach (variation, condensation, juxtaposition) versus “metallic music” (Hendrix, Glass, Ravel, Reich, Fripp, Eno), which he characterised as operating through a “diagrammatic mode of composition” using “translations” rather than “transitions,” built on “interference and harmonic resonance” and “totally inclusive syntheses.” Against Deleuze’s preference for non-pulsed time as the deterritorializing force in music, Pinhas argued that Glass’s pulsed repetitive music could paradoxically “disorganize the organic body” through accent displacement — enabling “a devenir moléculaire” (molecular becoming) within chronological time — and warned that purely non-pulsed music risks becoming “completely nihilist.” source: Purdue Deleuze seminar archive

The convergence of philosophical and musical problems was, for Pinhas, not metaphorical but structural. He explained it directly: “I was just writing notes for Gilles Deleuze and I was quite into Nietzsche’s philosophy at the time. I tried to make notes for the Deleuze book Mille Plateaux and I wanted to make a synthesis of philosophy with synthesizers. All my problems in philosophy were crossing with musical problems. Ideas about time, about repetition, about events. You have the same problems in music.” source: Red Bull Music Academy interview

The Rhizome in Practice: Rhizosphere (1977)

His first solo LP Rhizosphère is the clearest instance of Deleuzian philosophy inscribed directly into an album’s conceptual architecture. The title is taken from Deleuze and Guattari’s rhizome concept (developed in L’Anti-Oedipe, 1972, and elaborated in Mille Plateaux, 1980) — a non-hierarchical, proliferating network without centre or root, as opposed to the arborescent (tree-like) model of thought. The back sleeve carries a dedication: “Richard Pinhas à Gilles Deleuze.” The liner notes include a Deleuze-Guattari passage comparing music to rhizomes: “La música ha seguido aumentando sus líneas de fuga…la forma musical…es comparable a la mala hierba, un rizoma” (“Music keeps increasing its lines of flight…musical form…compares to weeds, a rhizome”). source: Electronic Orgy blog

Pinhas described his compositional strategy on that album through the language of diagrams operating on “molecular material,” creating conditions for sonic metamorphosis — echoing Deleuze and Guattari’s vocabulary of molecular versus molar, and diagrammatic versus representational. The album was performed almost entirely on synthesizers, with the side-long title track as a sustained exploration of what Pinhas called “a real sense of topography” — spatial organisation of time that “initially I did on purpose because it felt the best way. But then it became unconscious.” source: Psychedelic Baby Magazine interview, 2025

Chronolyse and Timeless Repetition

Chronolyse (1978) explores the philosophical concepts Pinhas held “dearest”: timeless repetition and the superimposition of events. The album tracks are named for characters from Frank Herbert’s Dune — another instance of the sci-fi/philosophy convergence that defined his PhD. The synthesis of science fiction cosmology with Deleuzian temporality (the Aion/Chronos split) is structural to the album’s conception. source: Cuneiform Records press materials, via cyclicdefrost.com

Lyotard: Presence Without Specific Texts

The influence of Lyotard is documented in terms of proximity and intellectual formation rather than traceable application of specific doctrines. The Quietus notes that Pinhas “would have also encountered Foucault, Baudrillard and Lyotard” in the same seminars as Deleuze. source: The Quietus The Huck Magazine interview describes his formation as immersed in “postmodern philosophy” broadly, with Heldon’s work at the intersection of “where postmodern philosophy and intelligent sci-fi converged.” source: Huck Magazine

No interview or academic source found to date articulates a specific Lyotard concept (libidinal economy, the figural, Discours, figure) as a direct compositional input. Lyotard’s influence appears to have operated through the general intellectual climate Pinhas absorbed at Vincennes — the shared poststructuralist discourse around desire, intensity, and the critique of representation — rather than as a distinct, traceable strand.

Later Philosophical Work: Les larmes de Nietzsche (2001)

After his return to music in the 1990s, Pinhas made his philosophical debts explicit in print. In 2001 he published Les larmes de Nietzsche: Deleuze et la musique (Flammarion), which traces a line from Nietzsche through Deleuze to contemporary music — Kraftwerk, Lou Reed, the Sex Pistols — described by the publisher as “rapid philosophical and musical moments of wild and lyrical thought.” The book includes a preface by writer Maurice G. Dantec and is characterised by critics as “tracing a luminous network in an inspired Deleuzian vein.” source: Google Books / Flammarion via Decitre

The 1995 album Cyborg Sally contains a 10-minute centrepiece track titled “Gilles Deleuze: Beyond Hyperion,” made after Deleuze’s death that year. The 1982 album L’Ethique references Spinoza (via its title, the name of Spinoza’s major work). source: Prepared Guitar blog interview

Summary: How Philosophy Shapes the Music

The philosophical influence is most traceable in four registers:

  1. Temporal structure: The core preoccupation with time, repetition, and duration — central to Deleuze’s Aion/Chronos distinction and to Bergson (whom Deleuze extensively read) — maps directly onto Pinhas’s extended, repetitive, drone-based compositions. Extended instrumental sections in Heldon and on solo albums are not commercial indulgence but philosophical propositions about non-linear time.

  2. Conceptual titles and dedications: Rhizosphère (rhizome theory), Chronolyse (chrono-analysis of duration), L’Ethique (Spinoza), De l’Un et du Multiple (philosophical ontology), “Gilles Deleuze: Beyond Hyperion” — titles name the intellectual sources rather than conceal them.

  3. Diagrammatic composition: The 1977 Vincennes session reveals that Pinhas thought about composition in terms of Deleuzian diagrams, molecular/molar distinctions, and the tension between pulsed (territorialized) and non-pulsed (deterritorializing) time — and that he actively pushed back on Deleuze’s own reading of music, defending the deterritorializing potential of pulsed repetitive music (Glass, Reich) against Deleuze’s preference for non-pulsed forms.

  4. Deterritorialization as aesthetic goal: Heldon’s hybrid of rock instrumentation (real drums, electric guitar) with modular synthesis and tape delay was not simply eclectic — it enacted deterritorialization of both rock and academic electronic music, refusing the territories of each.
