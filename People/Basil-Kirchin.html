From Discogs

Basil Kirchin (August 8, 1927 - June 18, 2005) was a British drummer and composer. His career included playing drums in his father’s big band at the age of 13, through scoring films, to experimenting with the manipulation of recorded sounds.

His debut was at the Paramount, Tottenham Court Road in London in 1940 where he played drums in a dance band led by his father Ivor. After the war, Kirchin played with Harry Roy and Ted Heath but returned to work with his father in The Kirchin Band in 1951, performing with the likes of Billy Eckstein and Sarah Vaughan. The band recorded for Decca and Parlophone (the latter being produced by George Martin). Kirchin was able to record the bands’ live performances.

In the late 1950’s Kirchin went to India and spent five months in the Ramakrishna Temple in West Bengal. He then moved to Sydney but as his possessions were lost in the move, including his recordings of The Kirchin Band. In 1961 he returned to Britain and worked with Keith Herd. He also produced material for the De Wolfe library alongside session musicians like Jimmy Page and Mick Ronson. In 1967 the Arts Council awarded him a grant to purchase a Nagra tape recorder which he used to collect ambient sounds, animal noises at London Zoo and the voices of autistic children. Overview

Basil Kirchin (1927–2005) was a British drummer and composer whose career traced an arc from teenage big-band work through commercial film scoring to pioneering tape-based sound experimentation. He debuted at 13 in his father Ivor’s dance band, worked with Harry Roy and Ted Heath, then rejoined The Kirchin Band in 1951 — cutting sides for Decca and Parlophone, the Parlophone sessions produced by George Martin before his Beatles years. A late-1950s sojourn to India and then Sydney cost him his early archive: his recorded documentation of the Kirchin Band was lost in the move. Returning to Britain in 1961, he produced library music for De Wolfe with session players including Jimmy Page and Mick Ronson, and scored films such as Catch Us If You Can (1965), The Shuttered Room (1967), and The Abominable Dr. Phibes (1971), while a 1967 Arts Council grant funded a Nagra recorder he used to capture ambient sounds, zoo animals, and the voices of autistic children for his experimental pieces. Trunk Records has been the primary custodian of his catalogue since 2003, accounting for most of his reissue activity.
