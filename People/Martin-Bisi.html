Martin Bisi

Full name: Martin Bisi Role: Recording engineer, producer, musician Active: 1980 – present Base: Brooklyn, New York Discogs ID: 141327 Discogs artist page: https://www.discogs.com/artist/141327

Biography

Origins and OAO Studios (1980–1982)

Martin Bisi entered the NYC recording world at the start of the 1980s as a collaborator of Bill Laswell, co-founding OAO Studios in Brooklyn in 1980. The studio was embedded in the downtown Manhattan / Brooklyn no-wave and post-punk moment from day one. Bisi’s earliest Discogs credits are as producer on records for Material and on Celluloid-family projects circulating through the Laswell orbit — among them the Temporary Music Compilation (1980) and James White’s Killing Time (1981, engineered at OAO).

The first major calling-card was Material’s Memory Serves (Celluloid, 1981), where Bisi served as both engineer/mixer and co-producer alongside Material and Laswell. The same year he recorded Curlew’s debut for Landslide and engineered Brian Eno’s Ambient 4 (On Land) (Editions EG, 1982) — an early indicator of the range of artists who would trust his ears.

BC Studio and the Downtown Boom (1983–1989)

OAO Studios transitioned into BC Studio (the name change appears in credits across 1983–85), and the facility became a central node in the NYC experimental underground. Bisi’s credit roll through this period is extraordinary in its breadth:

  • Herbie Hancock — Future Shock (Columbia, 1983): OAO engineering credit on the album containing “Rockit,” one of the most commercially successful avant-funk records of the era, produced by Material / Bill Laswell.
  • The Golden Palominos — self-titled (Celluloid, 1983): co-producer, engineer, and mixer alongside Bill Laswell and Anton Fier.
  • Sonic Youth — Bad Moon Rising (Homestead/Blast First, 1985): producer and engineer, co-credited with John Erskine. The band’s second LP, marking their move toward longer, more atmospheric songs.
  • Sonic Youth — EVOL (Blast First, 1986): engineer and co-producer (credited “w/”). Landmark noise-rock album featuring Kim Gordon, Thurston Moore, Lee Ranaldo, Steve Shelley.
  • Ciccone Youth — 1986 single “Tuff Titty Rap” / “Into The Groovy” (New Alliance / Blast First): engineer, drum programming, and co-producer. Also engineered “Into The Groovey” (track 16) on The Whitey Album (1988/89).
  • Sonic Youth — Starpower single (SST, 1987): producer and engineer.
  • Material — Seven Souls (Virgin, 1989): engineer at BC Studio and mixer, alongside Robert Musso at Platinum Island. The album features William S. Burroughs on vocals, Sly Dunbar, Nicky Skopelitis, and Rammellzee.
  • Foetus (Foetus Interruptus) — Thaw (Self Immolation, 1988): engineer. JG Thirlwell — see JG Thirlwell.
  • Swans — Undertow EP (Enemy Records, 1988): mixer and recorder.
  • Live Skull — Bringing Home the Bait (Homestead, 1985): co-mixer. Cloud One (Homestead, 1986): recorded and mixed. Swingtime (Homestead, 1986): co-mixer and recording engineer. Don’t Get Any On You (Homestead, 1987): co-mixer. Dusted (Homestead, 1987): co-mixer. Snuffer (What Goes On, 1988): producer, engineer, mixer. BC Studio was the primary recording base for the band’s entire Homestead run.
  • Blind Idiot God — self-titled (SST, 1987): engineer, co-producer, and mixer with the band.
  • John Zorn — Spy Vs. Spy: The Music of Ornette Coleman (Elektra Musician, 1989): mixing engineer.

He also recorded Swans Thaw material, and through Celluloid and related labels touched hip-hop and electro — working on early Afrika Bambaataa / Afrika Bambaataa recordings including Shango Message (Celluloid, 1983) and Shango Funk Theology (1984), as well as Fab 5 Freddy’s Change The Beat (Celluloid, 1982).

The Early 1990s and His Own Work (1988–1995)

Bisi continued engineering and producing through the early noise-rock boom that BC Studio was central to:

  • Cop Shoot Cop — Suck City (Atlantic/Big Cat, 1992): recorded and mixed.
  • Cop Shoot Cop — Ask Questions Later (Interscope, 1993): mixer and co-producer.
  • Swans — Love Of Life (Young God Records, 1992): additional engineer and programming.
  • Unsane — Total Destruction (City Slang, 1993); engineered and produced at BC Studio. (Note: the self-titled debut was engineered by Wharton Tiers, not Bisi; Discogs credits confirm Bisi only on Total Destruction.)
  • Swans — Soundtracks For The Blind (Young God/Atavistic, 1996): recorded and mixed at BC Studio.

Under his own name, Bisi released Creole Mass (Frog Records, 1988; reissued New Alliance Records, 1991), playing drums and producing, with Sandra Seymour on guitar and bass. A second solo album, All Will Be Won, appeared in 1993 on New Alliance Records. He also formed Martin Bisi Y Las Cochinas — a project with further releases on New Alliance in the mid-1990s.

Continued Activity and BC35 (1996–present)

BC Studio remained productive through the 2000s and beyond, with Bisi working with an increasingly international roster:

  • The Dresden Dolls — self-titled (8ft. Records, 2003): engineer, producer, electronics (Memoryman pedal), and string arranger on track 12. The band’s debut, featuring Amanda Palmer and Brian Viglione.
  • Barbez — co-producer, recorded by, mixed by on three albums: self-titled CD (Important Records, 2004, imprec036), Insignificance (2005), and Mexico City Impressions / Someone Get Rid Of The King (2007). All recorded at BC Studio.
  • Nona Hendryx — Sink (Self Immolation, 1989) and other recordings.
  • White Hills — ongoing recordings at BC Studio.
  • Swans — The Glowing Man (Young God, 2016): additional recording.
  • Serena-Maneesh — self-titled (Hype City, 2005): mixer.

In 2018–19, Bisi produced BC35 Vol. 1 and Vol. 2 (Bronson Recordings) to mark the 35th anniversary of BC Studio, gathering new tracks from many artists associated with the studio. He also released his own solo album Solstice (Bronson Recordings, 2019), playing guitar, voice, and synth.

He also has an alter-ego project Martin Bisi & El Cochino (label: Stripmine Recordings) and appeared on releases well into the 2020s, including Human Impact (Ipecac Recordings, 2020) and ongoing work with artists on Thrill Jockey and other labels.

BC Studio

BC Studio (originally OAO Studios) was established at 560 President Street, Brooklyn, NY in 1980 by Bisi and Bill Laswell. The “BC” name was in place by the mid-1980s. In Discogs credits the studio appears variously as “OAO Studio,” “OAO Studios,” “B.C. Studio,” “BC Studio,” “B.c. Studio,” and “BC Studios.”

The studio has been in continuous operation for more than four decades, making it one of the longest-running independent recording spaces in New York. Its central role in no-wave, noise rock, post-punk, hip-hop, and avant-jazz placed it at the intersection of virtually every significant downtown NYC scene of the 1980s.

Laswell Relationship and Studio Origins (web sources)

How they met: Bisi’s friends Michael Beinhorn and Fred Maher answered an ad Laswell placed in the Village Voice and joined his nascent collective. They then brought Bisi in, telling him “we’re starting this band, maybe you can do something.” Bisi agreed casually despite having no professional recording experience. Laswell subsequently took Bisi on as a live-sound apprentice at CBGB and brought him on tour as a roadie. (Sources: furious.com interview; Remezcla profile)

Studio founding: In fall 1979, Laswell floated the idea of finding a shared Brooklyn space to live, rehearse, and eventually record. Bisi found an affordable location in a half-abandoned building in the Gowanus area. Recording gear was accumulated incrementally. In late 1980, they met Brian Eno, who was fascinated by the NYC no-wave scene. Eno agreed to help fund the space in exchange for recording his ambient sessions there; the first session took place in January 1981 with Eno present as Bisi first turned on the recording equipment. Eno’s stay at the studio was brief — approximately one to two months — after which the space was left to Laswell and Bisi. (Sources: martinbisi.com/bio; beardedgentlemenmusic.com; Newsweek)

OAO name: “OAO” stood for “Operation All Out,” a phrase drawn from William Burroughs (cited variously as from Naked Lunch and as the title of the first track on Material’s Temporary Music 1). The name was Bisi’s choice. (Sources: Fear and Loathing Fanzine; beardedgentlemenmusic.com)

Working dynamic: Laswell functioned as the primary connector and creative decision-maker — “the one making a lot of the connections” in Bisi’s words — while Bisi handled engineering and the physical realization of recordings. By 1983 (Golden Palominos, Herbie Hancock’s Future Shock), Bisi carried co-producer credits alongside Laswell, indicating the collaboration was substantive rather than purely subordinate. (Source: furious.com interview)

OAO → BC transition: The name OAO was in use for approximately two years (c.1981–83). The shift to BC followed commercial and directional differences after the success of “Rockit.” Laswell wanted to relocate the studio to Manhattan for larger and more commercial productions; Bisi wanted to stay in Brooklyn and maintain the grassroots character of the space. Bisi described his thinking: “I knew I’d get lost in all this, so I decided that I wanted us to settle up, however was fair, and move on separately.” The separation involved Bisi agreeing not to seek royalties on collaborative work — including “Rockit” — in exchange for sole ownership of the studio. The name was changed to BC following the split. (Sources: Fear and Loathing Fanzine; furious.com interview; Wikipedia: Martin Bisi, as of 2026-06-07)

“BC” name meaning: Bisi has indicated the name was chosen for its deliberate understatement — a contrast to the grandiosity of “Operation All Out.” One source reports Bisi gave “Before Christ” as a meaning, consistent with that framing. The initials do not stand for “Bill and Carl” or similar. (Source: Fear and Loathing Fanzine)

Nature of the parting: Multiple sources describe the separation as professionally clean rather than acrimonious. Bisi’s accounts frame it as a clear-eyed choice to protect his artistic independence. No source describes a personal falling out. Laswell departed and Bisi became the studio’s sole operator. (Sources: Newsweek; furious.com interview)

Open Questions

  • Exact Discogs credit timeline for OAO vs. BC name coexistence (1981–85 overlap period) — Discogs shows both names through ~1985; web sources say the change was ~1983 post-Rockit; needs confirmation against liner note dates
  • Public Enemy connection: not confirmed. Despite wide circulation of this claim in biographical blurbs, no primary source supports it. Public Enemy’s early albums (Yo! Bum Rush the Show, 1987; It Takes a Nation, 1988; Fear of a Black Planet, 1990) were recorded at Spectrum City Studios (Hempstead, LI), Greene Street Recording (NYC), Chung King House of Metal (NYC), and Sabella Recording (Roslyn, LI) — none involving BC Studio or Bisi. No interview with Bisi mentions Public Enemy. The claim may be apocryphal or conflated with his Bambaataa/Celluloid hip-hop work.
  • Extent of Bisi’s role in early hip-hop recordings beyond the Discogs-confirmed Celluloid/Bambaataa credits
  • Full story of Martin Bisi Y Las Cochinas and mid-1990s New Alliance activity
  • Which specific Unsane records were tracked at BC Studio beyond the Discogs-confirmed ones
  • Exact wording/context of the royalty waiver on “Rockit” — Bisi’s own interviews describe this obliquely; no legal details are public
