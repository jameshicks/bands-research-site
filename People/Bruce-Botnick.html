Bruce Botnick

Bruce Botnick

Aug 02, 20269 min read

Bruce Botnick

American recording engineer, mixer, and producer. Discogs artist ID: 252436. Active from approximately 1963 to the present.

Primary home bases: Elektra Records (1966–early 1970s), Sunset Sound Recorders (throughout), and A&M Records. Runout signature: “BB” with the first B written backwards.

Biography

Origins — Disney and Early LA Sessions (1963–1965)

Botnick’s earliest Discogs-traceable credits are engineer credits at Disneyland and Buena Vista Records in 1963–1965, recording cast albums and children’s programming including Tubby the Tuba and Mary Poppins. He also engineered for the The Ventures on Liberty/Dolton Records during this period, suggesting he was already working across multiple LA studios as a staff or freelance engineer in his early career.

Elektra/LA Period — The Doors, Love, and the LA Rock Explosion (1966–1972)

By 1966 Botnick had become a central engineer at Elektra Records’ Los Angeles operations, working closely with producer Paul A. Rothchild on nearly every significant Elektra signing of the era. His Elektra run includes virtually the entire catalogs of both The Doors and Love (the band).

The Doors — Botnick engineered all six studio albums released during the band’s lifetime, a sequence that represents one of the most complete engineer-to-band relationships in rock history:

L.A. Woman marks a turning-point: Rothchild withdrew from the project citing artistic differences, and Botnick stepped up to co-produce with the band, recording the album in a rehearsal space at the Doors’ own workshop on Santa Monica Blvd. His transition from engineer to producer on this album is among his most noted career moments.

After Jim Morrison’s death in July 1971, Botnick produced the two remaining Doors albums (Other Voices, 1971; Full Circle, 1972) for the surviving trio.

Love — Botnick engineered Love’s second album Da Capo (1966) alongside Dave Hassinger, and co-produced Forever Changes (1967) with Arthur Lee. Forever Changes is widely regarded as one of the great albums of the 1960s. Botnick’s involvement as co-producer on such a landmark psychedelic-baroque record, alongside his parallel Doors work, positions him as a defining engineer-producer of the late-1960s LA scene.

Other significant Elektra-era credits include:

  • Tim Buckley — engineered multiple albums (1966 onwards)
  • Tim Buckley — engineering through the psychedelic folk period
  • The Turtles — engineered at Sunset Sound
  • Claudine Longet — engineer on her early-period albums
  • The Sandpipers — engineer on the A&M sessions
  • Sérgio Mendes (Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ‘66) — engineered debut A&M album Herb Alpert Presents Sergio Mendes & Brasil ‘66 (1966)
  • Herb Alpert and A&M artists — engineer

MC5 — In 1969, Botnick co-produced (with Jac Holzman) and engineered the MC5’s live debut album Kick Out the Jams (Elektra, 1969), one of proto-punk’s defining documents, recorded at Detroit’s Grande Ballroom. He also engineered Back in the USA (Atlantic, 1970), the studio follow-up produced by Jon Landau.

Buffalo Springfield — uncredited engineer on sessions during this period.

The Rolling Stones — Botnick appears as assistant engineer on 1969 Rolling Stones sessions (likely the Let It Bleed / Sticky Fingers era American sessions).

Broadening — Early 1970s

After L.A. Woman, Botnick continued producing and engineering at high volume across rock, jazz-rock, and pop:

  • Crazy Horse — produced and engineered the debut Crazy Horse album (Reprise, 1971), Neil Young’s backing band’s first LP in their own right
  • Earth, Wind & Fire — engineered the debut Earth, Wind & Fire (Warner Bros., 1971) and quadraphonic remix work on Devotion (1974)
  • The Tony Williams Lifetime — mixed The Old Bum’s Rush (Polydor, 1972), produced by Ben Sidran
  • The New Tony Williams Lifetime — produced and engineered both studio albums, Believe It (1975, Columbia) and Million Dollar Legs (1976). On the latter, Botnick also took a back-cover photography credit (as “Bruce Blotnick”); string and horn recording was handled separately by Doug Botnick (“Douglas Blotnick”)
  • Mark-Almond — engineer and producer on multiple albums (1971–1972), later remixes
  • Ben Sidran — producer on Feel Your Groove (1971) and I Lead a Life (1972), beginning a long working relationship
  • Rita Coolidge — engineer at A&M Studios (1971 onwards)
  • John Sebastian — engineer (1970–1971)
  • Bread — engineer on multiple albums for Elektra/Reprise
  • Dave Mason — engineer and producer (1970 onwards)

Mid-1970s to 1980s — Pop, Rock, and Film Scoring

Botnick’s role expanded into full production work and he became closely associated with a second wave of Los Angeles rock and pop acts:

  • Weather Report — recorded and mixed Tale Spinnin’ (Columbia, 1975), a key mid-period Weather Report album
  • Billy Joel — mixed Turnstiles (Columbia, 1976)
  • Michael Franks — recording engineer across several albums (1975 onwards)
  • Randy Newman — additional engineer (1972 onwards)
  • Eddie Money — produced the self-titled debut Eddie Money (Columbia, 1977) and its hit single “Baby Hold On”; produced multiple subsequent Money albums through the late 1970s–1980s
  • Kenny Loggins — co-producer, engineer, and mixer across roughly 130 releases (1980 onwards), one of his longest post-Doors relationships
  • Les Dudek — producer and engineer (1977–1979)
  • Paul Collins’ Beat — producer and mixer (1979–1980s)

Film scoring with Jerry Goldsmith — Starting around 1979, Botnick began a remarkably sustained collaboration with film composer Jerry Goldsmith that ultimately spans some 345 releases. His primary role was executive producer, editing engineer, and mixer on Goldsmith’s film and television scores. The first major credit in this thread is Star Trek: The Motion Picture (Columbia, 1979) where he served as executive producer and editing engineer.

Disney Era — Film and Broadway Soundtracks (1989–present)

By the late 1980s, Botnick had become the primary soundtrack producer and engineer for Walt Disney Records, recording and producing the film and Broadway soundtracks that defined the Disney Renaissance. Key credits:

He worked closely with Alan Menken and Howard Ashman on the early-90s Disney films, appearing on some 128+ releases connected to Menken and 128 connected to Ashman and Tim Rice.

John Williams (4) — a second major film-scoring partnership, producing and engineering Williams’s soundtrack albums (notably the E.T. The Extra-Terrestrial soundtrack, MCA, 1982) across roughly 152 releases.

The Jerry Goldsmith and John Williams partnerships, combined with Disney, make Botnick one of the dominant soundtrack album producers in Hollywood history from 1979 through the 2000s.

Continuing Doors Custodianship

Throughout his career Botnick has remained the primary archivist, reissue producer, and remix engineer for The Doors catalog. This includes:

  • 40th Anniversary Mixes and expanded editions of all major albums
  • 5.1 Surround Sound remixes (produced and mixed)
  • Dolby Atmos mixes
  • Liner notes contributions across dozens of reissue editions
  • Absolutely Live (1970) — live recording engineer
