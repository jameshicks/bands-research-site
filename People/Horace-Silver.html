Horace Silver

Horace Silver

Aug 06, 20267 min read

Horace Silver

Discogs ID: 29973
Real name: Horace Ward Martin Tavares Silver
Born: 2 September 1928, Norwalk, Connecticut, USA
Died: 18 June 2014, New Rochelle, New York, USA (aged 85)
Labels: Blue Note, Savoy Records, Impulse!, Verve Records, Silveto Records

American jazz pianist, composer, and bandleader. One of the principal architects of hard bop — the mid-1950s jazz style that foregrounded blues tonality, gospel feeling, and rhythmic directness against the more cerebral cool jazz of the period. Silver’s quintet recordings for Blue Note from the late 1950s through the 1970s form a cornerstone of the label’s catalog. He was also a prolific composer; his tunes have been covered extensively and several are jazz standards — “Song For My Father,” “Peace,” “Sister Sadie,” “Nica’s Dream,” “The Preacher,” “Señor Blues,” “Filthy McNasty,” “Doodlin’.” Silver was under exclusive contract to Blue Note for roughly two decades; after departing he founded his own label Silveto Records in 1980.

Son of John Tavares Silver (born in the Cape Verde Islands, of Portuguese descent) and Gertrude Silver (from New Canaan, Connecticut). His Cape Verdean heritage is central to his most celebrated album.

Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis in 2007; did not record after 1998.

Early Career and the Jazz Messengers (1950–1956)

Silver received his big break in 1950 when he backed Stan Getz at a club date in Hartford, Connecticut. This led to a move to New York City and a Blue Note recording debut in 1952 — 78 RPM single “Safari” / “Thou Swell.”

In 1953 Silver co-founded (with drummer Art Blakey) a cooperative small group that became the template for the hard bop quintet. The cooperative dissolved when Silver left to lead his own groups, while Blakey continued the format under the name Art Blakey & The Jazz Messengers. The joint-credit document of their partnership: Horace Silver And The Jazz Messengers (1956, Blue Note BLP 1518).

The Blue Note Quintet Years (1956–1964)

Silver’s Blue Note quintet records of the late 1950s and early 1960s established a template for hard bop — small ensemble, Silver originals, strong blues feeling, and Latin rhythmic influence. The working band of this period centered on the partnership of Blue Mitchell (trumpet) and Junior Cook (tenor saxophone).

Song For My Father (1964, Blue Note BLP 4185)

Silver’s most celebrated album and his best-known composition. Recorded across two sessions in 1963–64. The title track opens the album — its repeating bass line / piano figure is one of the most recognizable in jazz, and later provided the sample for Nas’s “The World Is Yours” (1994, Illmatic).

The album’s front cover features a photograph of Silver’s father, John Tavares Silver, a Cape Verdean immigrant. The music reflects that heritage: “Song For My Father” uses a bossa nova-inflected rhythm; the whole album has a cross-cultural warmth distinct from Silver’s earlier Blue Note hard bop.

Late Blue Note Period (1964–1979)

Silver continued releasing on Blue Note through the 1970s, with evolving personnel and increasingly ambitious concepts — the Silver ‘N series (1975–1980) applied Silver’s writing and piano to different ensemble formats: brass (Silver ‘N Brass, 1975), woodwinds (Silver ‘N Wood, 1976), voices (Silver ‘N Voices, 1976), percussion (Silver ‘N Percussion, 1978), strings (Silver ‘N Strings Play The Music Of The Spheres, 1979).

Silveto Records Era (1980–1993)

Silver founded Silveto Records after his Blue Note contract ended. He released a series of thematic albums exploring spirituality, self-help philosophy, and healing — Guides To Growing Up, Spiritualizing The Senses, There’s No Need To Struggle, The Continuity Of Spirit, Music To Ease Your Disease — on his own imprint, often featuring vocalist Andy Bey and saxophonist Eddie Harris.

He then recorded for Columbia/Sony in the early 1990s: It’s Got To Be Funky (1993) and Pencil Packin’ Papa (1994).

Silver In Seattle: Live At The Penthouse (2025, Blue Note)

Posthumous archival release. Released 24 October 2025 on LP and CD. The concert was recorded at The Penthouse jazz club in Seattle, Washington on August 12 and 19, 1965 — a radio broadcast captured by KING-FM radio host and engineer Jim Wilke. Source: Blue Note announcement (as of 2026-06-28).

Personnel:

RoleName
PianoHorace Silver
TrumpetWoody Shaw
Tenor SaxophoneJoe Henderson

This is the same quintet that recorded Song For My Father (1964) and The Cape Verdean Blues (1965). Woody Shaw replaces Carmell Jones on trumpet — placing this recording in an extremely short window when Shaw was in the band.
