Overview

John Felice is a Boston rock musician and songwriter who began his career as a teenager in The Modern Lovers, departing in January 1972 before forming The Kids, which became The Real Kids. That band anchored his catalogue across punk, power pop, and garage rock, with Norton Records, New Rose Records, and Ace of Hearts Records as recurring label homes. Long-running collaborators Allen Paulino, Billy Borgioli, and Howard Ferguson are prominent throughout his credits, and he also worked with producer Marty Thau and Andy Paley. He additionally participated in The Devotions and The Primevals, and led John Felice & The Lowdowns.

Felice’s Departures from the Modern Lovers — primary sources

There were two distinct exits from the band, not one. The sources are inconsistent on exact dates and collapse them differently, but the sequence is recoverable.

First departure: early 1971 (school / lifestyle)

Wikipedia’s Modern Lovers article records a clean lineup break: “In early 1971, Anderson and Felice departed; they were replaced by Harvard students bassist Ernie Brooks, and keyboardist Jerry Harrison (later of Talking Heads).” (Wikipedia, The Modern Lovers, as of 2026-10-04)

No primary-source interview specifically addresses why Felice left at this moment rather than any other. The secondary consensus is that high school obligations made touring impractical while the band chased West Coast gigs — a practical, not creative, reason. Wikipedia’s Real Kids article notes that Felice “rejoined the group for a few months after his graduation” (summer 1972), consistent with school being the blocking factor. (Wikipedia, The Real Kids, as of 2026-10-04)

Note: Discogs gives “left the band in January 1972” as a single date, apparently conflating or mis-dating the first departure. Wikipedia’s band article puts the first exit in early 1971 and a rejoining in summer 1972 leading to the final break in early 1973. The Wikipedia timeline is better supported by the primary material; see Conflict note below.

Final departure: January 1973 (creative split + internal tensions)

The richer primary-source record concerns the final departure, not the 1971 one. Felice is direct in his 1998 interview (JoJo Blog, 1998 interview, originally published in Rune magazine):

  • Predicted imminent collapse: “I quit the Modern Lovers after that Mercer show because the band was destined to break, I knew it.” The show in question was the Mercer Arts Center (New Year’s Eve 1972/73), and a Boston show at the Stone Phoenix Coffee House a week later was his final performance before the band flew to California to record. The Modern Lovers dissolved within a month of arriving in California.

  • Internal hatred: “We hated each other. We hated each other.” Felice attributes this to accumulated tensions rather than a single incident.

  • Creative territory: The other band members (Ernie Brooks, Jerry Harrison, David Robinson) wanted to play Felice’s material, but “Jonathan couldn’t go for it, because… he’s got a tremendous ego.” In the Bored Out Part 2 interview (Bored Out Part 2) Felice is similarly matter-of-fact: “Jonathan had a real problem in giving up any of that territory. That’s understandable.” And: “The Modern Lovers was always Jonathan’s band.”

  • Songwriting independence: In the Creative Loafing interview (Creative Loafing) Felice says: “I was starting to get ideas to write songs of my own… doing my own thing was a big part of it.” He also states: “I left the band in January of ‘73 and put a band together almost immediately after that.”

  • Lifestyle divergence: The oft-cited personality-difference quote (also in the 1998 Rune interview, preserved on JoJo Blog and cited in Wikipedia’s Real Kids article): “I was like a punk, I was a wise-ass kid. I liked to do a lot of drugs, I liked to drink, and Jonathan was like this wide-eyed, no-drugs, ate nothing but health food…” Wikipedia’s Real Kids article attributes this quote to the 1998 Jojofiles interview (Wikipedia, The Real Kids, as of 2026-10-04).

Assessment

The “early 1971” departure appears primarily practical (school preventing West Coast touring), not a creative schism — Felice willingly rejoined when he graduated. The creative split accumulated through the 1971–73 period as Felice developed his own songwriting voice and found Richman unwilling to share the band’s direction. The final break in January 1973 was driven by both: Felice’s creative independence and the lifestyle incompatibility had made the working relationship untenable. No source — including Felice himself — characterises either exit as driven by management or lineup pressure from outside the Richman–Felice axis; it is consistently framed as a Richman-driven creative dictatorship (Richman’s unwillingness to play Felice material) combined with opposed personal temperaments.

Conflict — departure date: Discogs profiles Felice as leaving “in January 1972.” Wikipedia’s Modern Lovers article places the first departure in “early 1971,” a rejoining after graduation (summer 1972), and a final break circa early 1973. The Creative Loafing and JoJo Blog interviews support “January of ‘73” for the permanent exit. The Discogs date appears to be an error or compression; it is not supported by any named primary source.

Felice–Thau Connection (web sources)

The link between Felice and Marty Thau predates The Real Kids and traces back to the Modern Lovers period. In a 2014 interview Felice states directly: “When I was in the Modern Lovers, he was in the running to be the manager of the Modern Lovers. He was manager of the (New York) Dolls and he wanted to manage us too.” (RPM/Jonathan Perry, 2014)

That management approach did not materialise, but the two stayed in contact. When Thau founded Red Star Records in New York (c. 1977), Felice was ready to commit: “I knew him really well. As soon as he put the Red Star label together, it just fell together perfectly.” (Creative Loafing interview)

A separate Bored Out interview (Parts 1 & 2, 2015) establishes more colour on the Dolls axis: Thau was working under Leber and Krebs management when he handled the Dolls, and it was via that orbit that the Modern Lovers (still a going concern) got booked onto a New York Dolls bill — likely the New Year’s Eve 1972/73 show at the Mercer Arts Center. (Bored Out Part 1)

So the route was: NYC punk/management circuit (Dolls → Thau → Modern Lovers management pitch in 1972–73) → relationship maintained through the Boston/New York scene → Red Star signing ~1977. The connection is not “Boston scene” in isolation nor “NYC punk circuit” alone; it is the cross-pollination of both, routed through the Dolls’ management world during the Modern Lovers’ New York appearances.

Felice expressed genuine belief in Thau’s vision (“Marty knew what he wanted. He had a reputation going back to the ’60s for picking up great bands”) and held no grievance when Red Star collapsed after two releases (Suicide’s LP and the Real Kids debut): “it wasn’t Marty’s fault and I never blamed him for it.” (Bored Out Part 2)
