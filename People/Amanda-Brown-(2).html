Not Not Fun Records — founding story

Not Not Fun Records was founded in Los Angeles in 2004 by Amanda Brown and Britt Brown. The two met at FLAUNT, a Hollywood celebrity-oriented glossy magazine, where Britt was a staff writer and Amanda was his intern. They began dating after she left, and Amanda proposed starting the label. [LA Weekly, hhv-mag]

The impulse was rooted in Amanda’s own history in DIY culture: as a young teenager she had been a riot grrrl, making zines and releasing cassette compilations through her own imprint, Cotton Crown. NNF was, in that sense, a return to that mode. [LA Weekly snippet via search result] She had also operated a small cassette label during her studies in Boston — she attended Massachusetts College of Art and Design (MassArt), where she minored in sculpting. [hhv-mag; LA Weekly]

Britt left his position at FLAUNT and the two originally intended the imprint to be an art project as much as a record label. Their first releases were lo-fi recordings made with a 4-track, sold as $3 mixtapes to friends. [hhv-mag; LA Weekly snippet] Both had loved cassettes since high school, and when the decision was made to start a label the format was never in question. [LA Weekly snippet]

The first release on the label was NNF001: a cassette sampler titled Have An Uptight Party by Britt and Amanda’s own band, Weirdo/Begeirdo. [search snippet via hhv-mag] Early NNF releases came in editions of 32 to 300 copies; the hallmark was handcrafted packaging, which was primarily Amanda’s department — noted as having “tiny little hands, perfect for assembling factory style.” [LA Weekly snippet] The label operated as a cottage industry, duplicating and mailing out releases by hand. [Bandcamp / 100% Silk profile]

The mission was deliberately cross-genre and imperfect: a “wild melting pot” of noise rock, bedroom indie, drone, and synth punk — music that defied conventional categorization, positioned against the sterile mass-produced CDs dominating the market at the time. [hhv-mag] Both Amanda’s and NNF’s roots were described as lying “deep in America’s post-Riot Grrrl Noise/lo-fi DIY underground.” [LA Weekly snippet via search result]

NNF went on to release early work by Weyes Blood, Peaking Lights, and Bethany Cosentino (later of Best Coast), among many others. [Bandcamp / 100% Silk profile]

In 2011 Amanda proposed creating 100% Silk as a sister imprint focused on what she described as “dance-not-dance” music — an interzone between DIY electronics and dance that she and Britt had felt opening up around 2009–2010. [Bandcamp / 100% Silk profile] She became sole owner/operator of 100% Silk.

At some point after the label was established, Amanda and Britt separated their roles; hhv-mag noted (as of 2013) that Britt runs Not Not Fun solo, while the two “remain friends.” [hhv-mag]

Pocahaunted — collaboration with Bethany Cosentino

Brown and Bethany Cosentino formed Pocahaunted in 2006, the year after Brown had founded Not Not Fun Records. According to Cosentino, the introduction came through a boyfriend at the time who was connected to Brown’s label: “He introduced me to her then her and I hung out one night and we were both like ‘let’s play music’.” [Vanyaland, 2016] A separate account from the Stereogum interview (2022) adds that Bobb Bruno — who was neighbors with “Brit and Amanda, who ran Not Not Fun” — was already in Brown’s circle and would later record and perform with Pocahaunted. Cosentino was around 19 at the time, in college and working retail, and the boyfriend encouraged her to start playing music again. [Stereogum, 2022]

The collaborative dynamic was largely improvisational and informal. Cosentino recalled: “We didn’t really discuss what it would be like and the sound of Pocahaunted was just created.” [Vanyaland, 2016] The pair self-described as the “Olsen twins of blissed out drone” and built a sound around wordless vocalizations rather than traditional lyrics. They were exceptionally prolific — releasing roughly one project per month and issuing over 25 releases in three years — all in vinyl and cassette formats with handmade/hand-painted artwork, predominantly on Not Not Fun. They supported Sonic Youth at a concert in Berkeley in 2007. [Wikipedia, Pocahaunted article, as of 2026-09-30: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocahaunted]

Cosentino was candid in multiple interviews that she was never fully invested in Pocahaunted’s sound. “I was never really passionate about it, I never really liked the kind of music we were playing.” [Vanyaland, 2016] More pointedly: “I was driving to band practice listening to Sheryl Crow, and walking in and doing these 12-minute-long drone jams.” [Stereogum, 2022] She characterized the project as “a convenient place to play music, because they had a label, and all this stuff” but remembered thinking: “This is not the kind of music that I want to be playing.” [Stereogum, 2022]

Cosentino departed in 2010, relocating to New York to study creative writing at The New School (she subsequently dropped out and returned to Los Angeles). [Wikipedia, Bethany Cosentino article, as of 2026-09-30: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethany_Cosentino] The split was not acrimonious — she described her departure as practical rather than dramatic. After returning to LA she contacted Bobb Bruno to begin Best Coast, a project she described as “going to be a reflection of music that I listened to, music that I really liked.” [Vanyaland, 2016] The Consequence of Sound article from January 2010 noted that after Cosentino’s departure Brown continued Pocahaunted with new collaborators including Cameron Stallones (Sun Araw), moving toward a “spacey, synth-steel-drummy” sound distinct from the original project’s drone aesthetic. [Consequence of Sound, 2010: https://consequence.net/2010/01/listen-pocahaunted-best-coast/]

Pocahaunted disbanded in August 2010. [Wikipedia, Pocahaunted]
