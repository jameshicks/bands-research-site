Judee Sill

American singer-songwriter. Discogs artist ID: 558172. Real name: Judith Lynne Sill. Born October 7, 1944, Los Angeles, California; died November 23, 1979, North Hollywood, California (drug overdose). First artist signed to David Geffen’s Asylum Records; creator of two studio albums that fused orchestral folk, baroque counterpoint, and Christian mysticism into a body of work largely ignored on release and rehabilitated posthumously.

Biography

Early life and criminal period. Almost nothing about Sill’s early life appears in Discogs data; what is documented elsewhere places her in a difficult childhood marked by loss — her father and stepfather both died when she was young — and a slide into petty crime and heroin addiction in her teens and early twenties. She spent time in reform schools and was involved in robbery. Before becoming a recording artist she worked as a keyboardist in a topless bar and reportedly ran some low-level criminal operations in the Los Angeles area. Her conversion toward songwriting coincided with her getting clean, at least temporarily, and entering the orbit of the Troubadour scene on West Hollywood’s Santa Monica Boulevard in the early 1970s.

Signing to Asylum. In 1971 David Geffen was forming Asylum Records, initially a singer-songwriter boutique focused on Los Angeles-based acoustic artists. Sill was among the first wave he signed — Discogs credits her debut single “Jesus Was A Cross Maker” (Asylum 45-11000) as the label’s first release with catalog number AS 45-11000. She was, per the Discogs profile, the first artist to sign and release on the label. Geffen assembled the roster that would define the “Laurel Canyon sound”: The Eagles, Jackson Browne, Linda Ronstadt, Crosby, Stills & Nash affiliates. Sill occupied an eccentric position in that company — her music was far denser, more deliberately arcane, than the other Asylum artists.

The debut album (1971). Judee Sill (Asylum SD 5050, 1971) was produced by Henry Lewy, whose sleeve credit read “Audio Alchemy” — his playful self-description that appeared on both her albums and on several Joni Mitchell records. Sill wrote and arranged every track; orchestration was handled by Bob Harris (5) (Discogs ID 964677 — Robert Maurice Harris, who also played in Frank Zappa’s Mothers and the Plastic Ono Band) and Don Bagley. Backing vocals came from Rita Coolidge, Clydie King, and Venetta Fields — a regular trio of session vocalists who appeared throughout the West Coast singer-songwriter scene. Cover art and design were by Gary Burden, the same art director behind many Asylum and Reprise covers of the period. Photography was by Henry Diltz, the CSN photographer. The debut album was produced collectively by Lewy alongside Jim Pons and John Beck (4) — though Lewy is generally understood as the primary producer.

One track on the debut had a notable additional credit: “Jesus Was A Cross Maker” — released as a promo single in 1971 with catalog number AS 45-11000 — was produced by Graham Nash, credited on the single (and listed at the track level on the album). Nash had been drawn to Sill’s work and briefly served as her champion within the industry. The single did not chart significantly despite the association.

Tracklist: “Crayon Angels” / “The Phantom Cowboy” / “The Archetypal Man” / “The Lamb Ran Away With The Crown” / “Lady-O” / “Jesus Was A Cross Maker” / “Ridge Rider” / “My Man On Love” / “Lopin’ Along Thru The Cosmos” / “Enchanted Sky Machines” / “Abracadabra.”

Heart Food (1973). The second album, Heart Food (Asylum SD 5063, 1973), went further in both ambition and isolation. By this point Sill had taken sole production credit alongside Lewy’s “Audio Alchemy” role. She is credited as Arranger, Composer, Orchestrator, and Producer — an unusual degree of control for a second album by an artist whose debut had not sold. The orchestrations are more explicitly Bachian; the album is sometimes described as her attempt at a personal cantata. No outside backing vocalists appear; the personnel is leaner. One track, “When The Bridegroom Comes” (B3), has a co-writing credit for David Omer Bearden, who also appeared as a blues harp player in Sill’s live footage from around 1973.

Tracklist: “There’s A Rugged Road” / “The Kiss” / “The Pearl” / “Down Where The Valleys Are Low” / “The Vigilante” / “Soldier Of The Heart” / “The Phoenix” / “When The Bridegroom Comes” / “The Donor.”

The BBC sessions (1972–1973). Sill recorded two sets of sessions for BBC Radio in 1972 and 1973, later collected on Live In London: The BBC Recordings 1972–1973 (Water/Troubadour, 2007). The compilation also includes an interview conducted by Bob Harris (6) — a different Bob Harris from the orchestrator; this is the British broadcaster known for the BBC Whispering Bob Harris. The BBC sessions preserve solo performances of material from both studio albums, recorded with greater intimacy than the orchestrated LPs.

Session work. Outside her own recordings, Sill appeared on a small number of other artists’ records. Most documented in Discogs is her credit on flutist Tim Weisberg’s album 4 (A&M SP 3658, 1974), where she played Acoustic Guitar, Celesta, and Piano on several tracks and contributed backing vocals. This was a jazz-rock session in a different stylistic world from her own work. She also appeared on “Hexagram 16” (Warner Bros., 1971), taking a Guitar credit.

Chariot of Astral Light (unreleased / posthumous). After Heart Food failed commercially, Sill was effectively dropped by Asylum and was unable to complete a third album. She spent the latter 1970s in worsening health, struggling with addiction. The material she recorded during this period, largely with collaborator Tommy Peltier, was eventually released as Chariot Of Astral Light (Black Beauty BB 72008, 2005). This CD documents recordings made with Peltier — who composed most of the material — and a shifting band including Art Johnson (guitar), Bob Harris (keyboards), Lynn Blessing (organ), Dave Parlato (bass), and others. Sill sings and plays organ on several tracks, including “Pocket-Socket,” “Chariot Of Astral Light,” “Here Today,” and others. The release is essentially a document of an unrealized final period rather than a shaped studio album.

A second posthumous compilation, Dreams Come True (Water water940, 2005), gathered early unreleased recordings alongside home recordings and a live video from USC in 1973. Mixed by Jim O’Rourke, restored by Gary Hobish. This set documented Sill’s pre-Asylum voice — poppier, less orchestrated, but showing the harmonic sophistication already in place.

Death. Sill died on November 23, 1979 at her home in the San Fernando Valley, North Hollywood, at age 35. The cause was a drug overdose. She was survived by her spouse, credited on Discogs as Bob Harris (5) (Robert Maurice Harris) — the same orchestrator on her debut album and the “Chariot of Astral Light” recordings.

Posthumous recognition. Starting in the 2000s her catalog was reissued steadily. Abracadabra: The Asylum Years (Rhino/Asylum, 2006) collected both studio albums. Live In London appeared in 2007 on Water and Troubadour. A tribute album, Crayon Angel: A Tribute To The Music Of Judee Sill (American Dust, 2009), assembled contemporary artists covering her songs. Her writing credit also appears on a Warren Zevon release (Mutineer, Giant Records) and, more recently, she is credited as Lyrics By on a 2023 4AD release (Haunted Mountain), suggesting at least one late-period lyric found its way into a newer recording project.
