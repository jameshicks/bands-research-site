James Spaulding

American jazz alto saxophonist and flutist. Full name: James Ralph Spaulding, Jr. Born July 30, 1937, Indianapolis, Indiana. Discogs artist ID: 262505. Active 1961–present.

Biography

Indianapolis Origins and Early Career (1937–1961)

James Ralph Spaulding, Jr. was born July 30, 1937 in Indianapolis, Indiana. Details of his early Indianapolis years are not captured in the Discogs record, but his first documented recording dates place him in New York by 1961. His first traceable session was on Sun Ra’s Jazz In Silhouette (Saturn Records, 1961), and almost simultaneously on Larry “Wild” Wrice’s Wild! for Pacific Jazz — both highly varied early gigs that show him already moving between different jazz circles.

The Blue Note Era (1962–1969)

Spaulding became one of the most reliable alto saxophonists in the Blue Note orbit through the 1960s. His entry point appears to have been Freddie Hubbard’s Hub-Tones (Blue Note, 1962), a hard bop quartet session with Herbie Hancock on piano, Reggie Workman on bass, and Clifford Jarvis on drums — produced by Alfred Lion and recorded by Rudy Van Gelder. That same year’s Breaking Point (1964, Hubbard) brought him back alongside Joe Chambers on drums and Ronnie Mathews on piano.

The most important single relationship of the Blue Note years was with pianist-producer Duke Pearson. Spaulding became the anchor reed voice on Pearson’s sextet and small ensemble recordings, and the two shared 78 releases across the Blue Note catalog — a count that places Spaulding among Pearson’s most consistent collaborators. The core Pearson sextet recordings were:

  • Wahoo (BLP 4191, 1965) — with Donald Byrd (trumpet), Joe Henderson (tenor sax), Bob Cranshaw (bass), Mickey Roker (drums); Pearson wrote all tunes
  • Sweet Honey Bee (BLP 4252, 1967) — the sextet’s peak: Freddie Hubbard replacing Byrd, Ron Carter replacing Cranshaw, Henderson and Spaulding remaining; produced by Alfred Lion
  • The Right Touch (BST 84267, 1967) — a larger ensemble, Hubbard plus Stanley Turrentine on tenor, Garnett Brown on trombone, Jerry Dodgion adding a second reed voice, Grady Tate on drums

Beyond Pearson, Spaulding appeared on an extraordinary range of Blue Note sessions through the decade. He was on Wayne Shorter’s The All Seeing Eye (1966) — a major post-bop/spiritual session with Hubbard, Herbie Hancock, Ron Carter, and Joe Chambers. He appeared on Stanley Turrentine’s Rough ‘N Tumble (1966), alongside McCoy Tyner and Grant Green, with Pearson arranging. He played on McCoy Tyner’s Tender Moments (1968) — an ambitious chamber-jazz date with Lee Morgan, Bennie Maupin, Julian Priester, and French horn/tuba. He was part of Freddie Hubbard’s landmark live double album The Night Of The Cookers (1965, Club La Marchal, volumes 1 and 2) — an electrifying live date featuring both Hubbard and Lee Morgan on trumpets, Harold Mabern on piano, Pete La Roca on drums.

He also appeared on Sam Rivers’s Dimensions & Extensions (recorded 1967, issued 1986 Blue Note) and Wayne Shorter’s Schizophrenia (1969), the latter one of Shorter’s most celebrated Blue Note albums.

Outside Blue Note proper, Atlantic sessions rounded out the period: Freddie Hubbard’s Backlash (1967), Max Roach’s Drums Unlimited (1966), and several other Atlantic dates from 1966–1968.

Sun Ra and the Outer Edges (1961–1970)

Spaulding’s relationship with Sun Ra runs as a distinct parallel thread. He appeared on Jazz In Silhouette (1961), Visits Planet Earth (1966), The Nubians Of Plutonia (1969), and Sound Sun Pleasure!! (1970) — all Saturn/El Saturn Records releases. His presence in the Ra circle alongside the Blue Note mainstream work is an unusual combination and speaks to his breadth. He was also part of Pat Patrick’s circle (Patrick being another Ra associate).

Flying Dutchman and Impulse! Sessions (1969–1971)

Several important sessions in this period were for Bob Thiele’s Flying Dutchman label. Spaulding played flute on Pharoah Sanders’s Karma (Impulse!, 1969) and on Leon Thomas’s Flying Dutchman dates (Spirits Known And Unknown, 1969; The Leon Thomas Album, 1970; Facets, 1973). He also participated in the politically charged spoken-word/jazz project Pete Hamill’s Massacre At My Lai (1969) and Robert Scheer’s A Night At Santa Rita (1969, Flying Dutchman), the latter one of the few releases where he appears as primary artist credit.

Max Roach Quintet and Charles Tolliver (1966–1981)

Spaulding was a member of the Max Roach Quintet — documented on Drums Unlimited (Atlantic, 1966) — and of The Charles Tolliver Quintet, suggesting he maintained active working band relationships beyond pure session work. He also performed with Horace Silver’s Sextet (listed in Discogs groups as The Horace Silver Sextet). A 1981 Swiss recording, Ashanti (Nilva Records), documents him in a European session context.

Own Recordings and Muse Records (1979–1993)

Spaulding’s own leader albums began to appear through Muse Records in the late 1980s–early 1990s, with producer Don Sickler overseeing the sessions:

  • Brilliant Corners (Muse, 1989) — a co-leader date with Wallace Roney (trumpet), Ron Carter (bass), Kenny Washington (drums), Mulgrew Miller (piano); recorded by Rudy Van Gelder; Spaulding alternated alto and flute by track
  • Gotstabe A Better Way (Muse, 1990) — solo leader, produced by Don Sickler
  • Songs Of Courage (Muse, 1993) — with Kenny Barron (piano), Ray Drummond (bass), Louis Hayes (drums), Roland Alexander (tenor sax on selected tracks); engineered by Van Gelder

He also appeared on David Murray’s ensembles — the David Murray Big Band and David Murray Octet — and on Sam Rivers Sextet dates, extending his reach into the post-bop / loft jazz continuum.

Later Career (1994–present)

Spaulding remained active into the 2000s and 2010s. He appeared on Butch Morris’s conducted improvisation projects (Conducted By Lawrence “Butch” Morris, DIW, 1991), on William Parker’s extended projects (William Parker Septet), and on various compilation reissues of his Blue Note work. Later sessions include appearances on Azar Lawrence (Water, Motéma, 2010) and continued presence at jazz events. The Discogs record shows credits as late as 2021–2022 on reissue compilations.

Scene Connections — Blue Note Hard Bop / Post-Bop 1962–1969

Spaulding’s Blue Note years place him at the center of the label’s transition from hard bop to post-bop. He appeared on dates produced by Alfred Lion (the classic era) and under Duke Pearson’s production (the transitional 1967–1969 period). His co-appearances with Freddie Hubbard, Lee Morgan, Wayne Shorter, McCoy Tyner, and Joe Henderson across multiple sessions show him as part of the core rotating ensemble pool that defined Blue Note’s sound in that decade.

The Sun Ra thread is unusual for a Blue Note regular — most of that pool did not venture into the Ra orbit. It suggests Spaulding had an independent avant-garde interest that ran alongside the mainstream studio work.

His later work with David Murray (big band and octet) and the William Parker Septet in the 1990s–2000s positions him as a continuing presence in post-free jazz ensembles.
