Tom Moulton

Real name: Thomas Jerome Moulton
Discogs ID: 3481
Born: November 29, 1940, Schenectady, New York
Also credited as: “A Tom Moulton Mix,” T. Moulton, TJM, Tom “Mr. Ears” Moulton
Aliases: Moto (2), Positive Source, TJM
Groups: Tom Moulton & José Rodriguez

American engineer, mixer, producer, and archivist. Originator of the extended disco remix, the breakdown section (“disco break”), and — by his account, accidentally — the 12-inch single format. His phrase “A Tom Moulton Mix” became a genre designation and a model for how DJ-producers would credit their work throughout the disco era and beyond. Inducted into the Dance Music Hall of Fame as a Remixer (2004). Official archivist of the Bethlehem Jazz and Salsoul music catalogues.

Biography

Early Career and Fire Island (Late 1950s–1973)

Moulton grew up in Schenectady and quit high school to work in a record store in the late 1950s. He had aspirations toward radio DJing but found the payola-ridden AM landscape of the period impenetrable. Instead he moved through the industry’s promotional and sales side: King Records (1959–1961), then RCA, then United Artists. He eventually left in disgust at the industry’s ethics.

He worked as a fashion model at the Bookings and Ford agencies before pivoting toward music production. His first significant mixing project was a self-made tape of overlapping songs he created for the Fire Island bar and restaurant The Sandpiper — a high-profile venue that put his skills in front of music industry figures. Those connections led to pre-release listening sessions and eventually to studio work.

The Extended Mix and the 12-Inch Format (1974–1975)

Moulton’s technical approach inverted the standard pop-production instinct: rather than adding elements to make a record more exciting, he characteristically stripped the multi-track down, extending high frequencies, tightening the low end, and repeating or isolating key passages for maximum dancefloor impact. His phrase for it — “I never made a dance record, I made records you can dance to” — captures the distinction.

The first major landmark was Gloria Gaynor’s Never Can Say Goodbye (MGM Records, M3G 4982, 1975). Moulton created the first continuous-mix album side ever recorded — the entire A-side of the LP plays as a single unbroken mix of “Honey Bee,” “Never Can Say Goodbye,” and “Reach Out I’ll Be There.” This earned him the informal title “father of the disco mix.” (Note: Gaynor’s credited production team was Meco Monardo and Tony Bongiovi; Moulton’s role was the mix construction, credited as “Mixed By” on most pressings.)

The same year, B.T. Express’s “Do It (‘Til You’re Satisfied)” (Scepter Records, SPS 5117, 1974) became one of his first widely circulated remixes — credited as “Remix [Disco Remix]” on the original pressings, its elongated arrangement established the template for what would shortly become the commercial 12-inch single.

The 12-inch format itself emerged partly as a practical accident: the story most often repeated is that Moulton found himself without 7-inch blanks and cut a mix onto a 12-inch blank, discovering the playback volume and fidelity improvement. Whether strictly true or not, his early 12-inch cuts at Frankford/Wayne and other New York mastering labs circulated through the DJ pool system and created demand for the format.

Disco Gold (Scepter Records, SPS 5120, 1975) was one of his earliest mixed/remixed compilation LPs, documenting his approach across multiple artists.

Salsoul and the Sigma Sound Years (1975–1979)

Moulton’s most sustained institutional relationship in the disco era was with Salsoul Records, where he logged the highest credit count of any label in his discography — 480 release credits across the label’s history (Discogs), concentrated in 1975–1980. He worked consistently at Sigma Sound Studios in Philadelphia, the primary facility for the Philadelphia International Records / Salsoul axis.

Atlantic / The Trammps and Mainstream Disco (1975–1979)

On Atlantic, Moulton’s most enduring credit is the album mix of The Trammps’ Disco Inferno (SD 18211, 1976) — the album contains the full 11-minute version of “Disco Inferno,” which became one of the most recognized disco tracks after its use in the 1977 film Saturday Night Fever. His credit appears as “Mixed By [A Tom Moulton Mix]” on the original US pressing.

Other Atlantic work in this period: Eddie Kendricks, The Spinners, Bette Midler, and numerous one-off disco singles across the label’s large pop and soul roster.

West End Records (1976–1984, 2000–2003)

Moulton’s 55 release credits on West End Records (Discogs label ID 1325) span two phases: an active mixing and production role in the label’s first years (1976–1981), and later mastering/archival work from 2000 onward. He is tied with Larry Levan at 44 mixer/engineer credits across the label’s history — the joint highest count in that role category.

His West End work began at the label’s founding. The very first split single, “You Are The Star / Mary Hartman, Mary Hartman” (WES 12101, 1976), carries his credit as “Mixed By [A Tom Moulton Mix].” He mixed or produced virtually the entire first run of West End 12”s:

Grace Jones Trilogy on Island Records (1977–1979)

Between 1977 and 1979 Moulton produced Grace Jones’s first three albums for Island Records:

TitleYearRelease IDNotes
Portfolio197716282595Produced and mixed by Moulton; arranged by Vincent Montana Jr.
Fame1978—Second LP; Moulton as producer
Muse1979—Third LP; includes Jones’s rendition of Édith Piaf’s “La Vie en rose,” one of her breakthrough recordings

Portfolio features the Salsoul/Sigma Sound house band axis — drummer Allan Schwartzberg, bassist Wilbur Bascomb, guitarist Bobby Eli, pianist Ron Kersey, and Sigma engineer Arthur Stoppe. Vincent Montana Jr. arranged and conducted. The tension between Moulton and Montana, both strong personalities with competing visions, was noted by observers; Moulton later cast doubt on Montana’s subsequent adoption of the “A [Name] Mix” credit formulation.

Island Records became Moulton’s second-largest credit-count label in the Discogs data (440 release credits across his history there, primarily the Grace Jones albums and their international pressings), reflecting how widely the Jones material was distributed.

Casablanca and Other Labels (1977–1981)

Moulton mixed or produced across the Casablanca roster (53 release credits). Discogs data shows no Village People credits — the Casablanca acts he actually worked with were Eddie Drennon & The B.B.S. Unlimited (“Would You Dance To My Music,” 1977, Mixed By), The Pips (“Callin’,” 1978, Mixed By), TJM (TJM LP and “Put Yourself In My Place,” 1979, Producer), Loose Change (Loose Change LP and “Straight From The Heart,” 1979, Producer), and People’s Choice (“People’s Choice,” 1980, Producer). The Loose Change LP carries an unusual personal performance credit — “Tambourine, Glockenspiel [Orchestra Bells]” — one of the few Discogs records of Moulton playing an instrument on a session.

”A Tom Moulton Mix” (1975 Onward)

The credit phrase “A Tom Moulton Mix” was the first instance of a DJ/remixer prefacing a track credit with their name in this possessive form. It became a genre-level signal: records carrying it were presumed to be extended, bass-forward, dancefloor-optimized. Moulton’s contemporaries at Salsoul — notably Vincent Montana Jr. — adopted analogous credit phrasing (“A Vincent Montana Production” etc.), which Moulton regarded as imitation. The dispute reflects the credit economy of early disco: these phrases were commercial signals as much as artistic attributions.

The phrase became the title of the 2006 Soul Jazz Records compilation “A Tom Moulton Mix” (Soul Jazz Records, SJR LP73, release ID 670156), compiled by Aldrick De Narp and Romain Tassinari, mastered by Duncan Cowell and Pete Reilly, with Moulton credited as “Mixed By” across all tracks.

Dance Music Hall of Fame Induction (2004)

Moulton was inducted into the Dance Music Hall of Fame in 2004 for his achievements as a Remixer. The ceremony was held in New York City.

Post-2000 Work

Moulton remains active as a remixer and mastering engineer. Credits from the 2000s onward include:

  • Larry Levan Live At The Paradise Garage (Strut/West End, 2000) — Mastered By
  • The Brand New Heavies (featuring N’Dea Davenport) — “I Don’t Know (Why I Love You)” remix (late 2006), described as a cover of the Stevie Wonder/Jackson 5 track
  • Bethlehem Records catalog remastering — 49 credits (1992–2013); official Bethlehem archivist
  • Salsoul Records catalog remastering — continuing through 2022 as official Salsoul archivist
  • Philadelphia International Classics: The Tom Moulton Remixes (3-part series, PIR 2019 001–003) — explicit archival acknowledgment of his 1977 Philly remixes, with Ian Dewhirst providing annotation and Barry Grint as mastering engineer
