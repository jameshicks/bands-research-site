Mike Bloomfield

Born 28 July 1943, Chicago, Illinois; died 15 February 1981, San Francisco, California (drug overdose). Real name: Michael Bernard Bloomfield. Discogs artist ID: 258121.

One of the most admired American blues-rock guitarists of the 1960s. Bloomfield grew up in Chicago and was already playing alongside blues legends before his national breakthrough. He preferred a loud but clean, reverb-heavy tone — rarely using feedback or heavy distortion — and is credited as a primary force behind the mid-to-late 1960s blues revival. In 2003 Rolling Stone ranked him #22 on its 100 Greatest Guitarists list. Gibson subsequently issued a Michael Bloomfield signature Les Paul replicating his 1959 Standard. He died at 37 from a drug overdose, leaving a discography spanning blues, jazz-inflected rock, and solo acoustic work.

Career by Era

1964–1966: The Paul Butterfield Blues Band

Bloomfield joined Paul Butterfield’s integrated Chicago blues band in 1964, becoming one of two lead guitarists alongside Elvin Bishop. The band signed to Elektra and released their eponymous debut in 1965 (produced by Paul Rothchild; Bloomfield on slide guitar). East-West (Elektra, 1966) followed — a landmark album whose title track ran over thirteen minutes and incorporated Indian raga and jazz influences alongside straight Chicago blues. Bloomfield left the band after East-West.

1967–1968: The Electric Flag

Bloomfield co-founded The Electric Flag in San Francisco in 1967. The band — also featuring Barry Goldberg (keyboards), Buddy Miles (drums), Harvey Brooks (bass), Nick Gravenites (vocals), and a brass section — debuted at the Monterey Pop Festival. Their only studio LP, A Long Time Comin’ (Columbia, 1968), was followed by the live The Best Of The Electric Flag (Columbia, 1971). Bloomfield effectively departed in 1968 as the band dissolved; he retained songwriting and Guitar credits on all studio material.

1967: Film work

Bloomfield played lead guitar on the soundtrack to The Trip (1967), a Roger Corman psychedelic film with music by the Electric Flag (Sidewalk Records, 1967).

1968: Super Session and Live Adventures

The co-billed LP Super Session (Columbia, 1968) — Al Kooper producing and playing keyboards, Bloomfield on guitar (side A), Stephen Stills on guitar (side B), with Harvey Brooks on bass and Eddie Hoh on drums — became a gold record and defined the “all-star jam” album format. Fred Catero and Roy Halee engineered. The live double LP The Live Adventures of Mike Bloomfield and Al Kooper (CBS, 1968) followed almost immediately, recorded at the Fillmore West.

1969: Fathers and Sons; It’s Not Killing Me

Bloomfield appeared on Muddy Waters’s Fathers and Sons (Chess, 1969) alongside Paul Butterfield (harmonica), Otis Spann (piano), Donald “Duck” Dunn (bass), and Sam Lay (drums). Produced by Norman Dayron, engineered by Ron Malo at Chess. The same year his first major solo album, It’s Not Killing Me (CBS, 1969), appeared — with backing from the Ace of Cups and Noel Jewkes among others.

1970–1972: Sessions and Collaborations

Bloomfield played piano on the John Paul Hammond Vanguard albums and contributed guitar to several West Coast recordings. He played guitar on the film soundtrack Steel Yard Blues (Warner Bros., 1972) alongside Paul Butterfield, Merl Saunders, and Maria Muldaur, and co-produced records for James Cotton (Verve Forecast, 1967–68).

1972: Mill Valley Bunch

Bloomfield was a member of the informal Mill Valley Bunch collective (Discogs ID: 1916410), a loose Bay Area aggregation also including Barry Goldberg, Nick Gravenites, John Kahn, Mark Naftalin, Michael Shrieve, Pete Sears, Spencer Dryden, and Reinol Andino, among others. The group released Mill Valley Jam Sessions (Polydor, 1972).

1973: Triumvirate

Triumvirate (Columbia, 1973) was recorded with John Paul Hammond (lead vocals, guitar, harmonica) and Dr. John (piano, organ, guitar, banjo, percussion, arrangement). Thomas Jefferson Kaye produced. Session players included Chris Ethridge (bass), Jim Gordon (baritone sax), Blue Mitchell (trumpet), Jerry Jumonville (reeds), and backing vocalists Robbie Montgomery and Jessie Smith (Jessica Smith). Roger Nichols and Pete Weiss engineered.

1975–1976: KGB (7)

Bloomfield joined the supergroup KGB (7) (Discogs ID: 595176) alongside Barry Goldberg, Carmine Appice (drums), Ray Kennedy (vocals), and Rick Grech (bass). Their self-titled album (MCA Records, 1976) was produced and engineered by Jim Price.

1976: Guitar Player Records

If You Love These Blues, Play ‘Em As You Please (Guitar Player Records, 1976) — a solo instructional/performance album — was produced by Bloomfield himself, with sleeve notes by him and cover art by David Blossom.

1977–1980: Solo on Takoma and Waterhouse

The Takoma period yielded Analine (1977), Michael Bloomfield (1978; produced by Norman Dayron), and Between The Hard Place & The Ground (1979). All three were produced by or with Dayron and recorded in Northern California. Analine featured Roger Troy (bass) and Barry Goldberg (keyboards). Living In The Fast Lane (Waterhouse Records, 1980) was his last studio album before his death, with Bloomfield playing guitar, piano, organ, bass, slide, and percussion throughout.

1979–1981: Late recordings

Bloomfield Harris (Kicking Mule / Sonet, 1979) was a duo acoustic album with guitarist Woody Harris. Cruisin’ For A Bruisin’ (Sonet, 1981) and Red Hot & Blue (United Distributors Lyrics, Ltd., 1981) appeared just before and after his death. I’m With You Always (Demon Records, 1987) and Junko Partner (Intermedia/Quicksilver, 1982) were posthumous compilations.

Posthumous Compilations

Bloomfield: A Retrospective (Columbia, 1983) — the primary career overview on his main label — and Don’t Say That I Ain’t Your Man! Essential Blues 1964–1969 (Columbia/Legacy, 1994) are the key compiled overviews. From His Head To His Heart To His Hands (Columbia/Legacy, 2014) is a later four-disc box.
