Overview

Jean-Pierre Turmel is a French graphic artist and co-founder of Sordide Sentimental, the label he established in 1979 that became a key conduit between the UK post-punk underground and French audiences. His 67 Discogs credits are almost entirely in visual and production roles — recorded by, text, cinematography, and maquette work — spanning from 1979 through at least 2025. His closest collaborators by shared release count are Joy Division and Martin Hannett (15 each), followed by Genesis P-Orridge and Psychic TV (10–11), positioning Turmel at the intersection of Factory-adjacent post-punk and the industrial avant-garde.

Sordide Sentimental and Joy Division: How the Collaboration Began

The account below is drawn primarily from an interview with Turmel published in Rébellion magazine (issue 31, July/August 2008; republished online April 2021): rebellion-sre.fr. No primary source account from Martin Hannett, Tony Wilson, or Joy Division members has been located that corroborates or contradicts this account.

Initial contact with Factory Records (early 1979). Turmel discovered Joy Division through a Factory Records compilation in early 1979 and immediately wrote to Tony Wilson to express his enthusiasm and note the affinities between Sordide Sentimental and Factory Records (the fanzine was already being published and the label was preparing its first 45). According to Turmel, Wilson later told him in a phone conversation that “Sordide Sentimental had been the very first independent label to contact him” (“Sordide Sentimental avait été le tout premier label indépendant à l’avoir contacté”).

Genesis P-Orridge’s role as intermediary. When Throbbing Gristle performed in Manchester a few months later, Genesis P-Orridge — who had a genuine friendship with Ian Curtis — showed Curtis the Sordide Sentimental/Throbbing Gristle 45 that the label had just published. According to Turmel, this “aroused vivid interest with Ian Curtis to the point of him suggesting the idea of making a record with Sordide Sentimental in the same spirit (a record-object with texts and illustrations).”

Rob Gretton and the text proposal. Rob Gretton (Joy Division’s manager) subsequently sent Turmel a copy of Unknown Pleasures immediately upon its release. Turmel had already prepared a text on the theme of light, originally intended for an American group that had changed direction; he recognized it “could perfectly suit Joy Division with some additional developments.” He proposed the collaborative project, which the band accepted.

The artwork and meeting. Turmel commissioned the cover painting from his mentor Jean-François Jamoul, a painter influenced by German Romanticism. The horizontal landscape painting was printed in full format despite increased costs, with the left portion forming the back cover, and no text was placed on the composition — Turmel felt this enigmatic approach suited both the band and his accompanying prose. The two parties met on 18 December 1979 at the Bains Douches concert in Paris; Turmel recalls Curtis being “visibly astonished” by the painting and expressing a desire to purchase it. Despite this meeting and the warm connection, Turmel notes they “barely communicated” in the end (“nous n’avons toujours que très peu communiqué”).

Martin Hannett’s role. No source — including Turmel’s own account — places Hannett in a brokering role. He recorded “Atmosphere” and “Dead Souls” with Joy Division at Cargo Studios, Rochdale, in October 1979 (the sessions that produced the tracks ultimately released on Licht und Blindheit), but the collaboration with Sordide Sentimental was initiated and arranged directly between Turmel, Tony Wilson/Factory Records, and Rob Gretton, with Genesis P-Orridge acting as the catalyst who showed Curtis the label’s work. Hannett is credited as producer on the release. No source consulted attributes to him any role in brokering the label relationship.

Conflict note. The Discogs profile and general discographic sources record the recordings as made for the Sordide Sentimental release in October 1979. Some secondary sources describe the tracks as “from the Closer sessions,” which is technically correct by chronology but misleading — they were recorded by Hannett at Cargo Studios in October 1979, several months before the Closer sessions at Britannia Row (March 1980). No conflict with the vault’s existing Discogs data has been identified on factual release details.
