Julian Priester

American jazz trombonist, born June 29, 1935, in Chicago, Illinois. Full name: Julian Anthony Priester. One of the most versatile brass voices of the post-bop and early fusion eras, Priester is best known for his role in Herbie Hancock’s Mwandishi sextet (1971–73), where he appeared under the Swahili name Pepo Mtoto (and later just Pepo). His career spans from Sun Ra’s Arkestra in the mid-1950s through a long run as a sideman for Max Roach, a turn as a solo ECM artist in the 1970s, and sustained activity into the 2010s and 2020s.

Discogs artist IDs: 264871 (Julian Priester), 320111 (Pepo (2) alias)

Career

Early Years and Chicago (1956–1959)

Priester first appears on record in 1956 with Sun Ra’s Arkestra — the 7-inch “Super Blonde / Soft Talk” on El Saturn Records and the LPs Jazz By Sun Ra Vol. 1 (Transition, 1957) and Super-Sonic Jazz (El Saturn, 1957). He continued appearing on Sun Ra Arkestra reissues through the 1960s, including Angels and Demons at Play (El Saturn, 1967) and We Travel the Space Ways (El Saturn, 1967), and compilations long afterward.

From Chicago, Priester moved into the broader hard bop circuit. He contributed to sessions on Riverside, Mercury, and Emarcy, including Eddie “Lockjaw” Davis’s Blues For Dracula (Riverside, 1958), Abbey Lincoln’s Abbey Is Blue (Riverside, 1959), and Wes Montgomery’s The Little Giant (Riverside, 1959).

Max Roach and Hard Bop Prominence (1959–1965)

The defining partnership of Priester’s early career was with Max Roach. He appears on the landmark We Insist! Max Roach’s Freedom Now Suite (Candid, 1961), one of the most important protest recordings in jazz history, alongside vocalist Abbey Lincoln. He also played on Percussion Bitter Sweet (Impulse!, 1961) and It’s Time (Impulse!, 1962), as a member of the Max Roach Plus Four / Quintet.

During this period Priester was also first-call for sessions at Candid Records — appearing on Booker Little’s Out Front (1961) and Abbey Lincoln’s Straight Ahead (1961, for which he also contributed arrangements). He appeared on the Newport Rebels session (Candid, 1961) alongside Mingus and Roach, and on sessions for Blue Note (Blue Mitchell’s Hub Cap, 1961) and Impulse!

His first album as a leader, Keep Swingin’ (Riverside, 1960), featured Sam Jones, Jimmy Heath, Elvin Jones, and Tommy Flanagan — a top-tier Riverside hard bop lineup.

Duke Ellington, Duke Pearson, and the Late 1960s Blue Note Circle (1965–1970)

Priester joined Duke Ellington’s orchestra and appears on the New Orleans Suite (Atlantic, 1971; recorded 1970) and The Private Collection: Volume Five (LMR, 1987; recorded 1968–70). He also worked with Duke Pearson’s Big Band on Introducing Duke Pearson’s Big Band (Blue Note, 1968) and Now Hear This / related sessions.

Through the late 1960s he was a regular Blue Note session player: Joe Henderson’s Tender Moments (1968), Lee Morgan’s The Spoiler (1967) and Heads Up! (1968), and Hank Mobley’s Turning Point (1969) and Fancy Free (1969).

Herbie Hancock’s Mwandishi Sextet (1971–1973)

Priester joined the nascent Mwandishi band for Mwandishi (Warner Bros., 1971), adopting the Swahili name Pepo Mtoto. The other core members were Bennie Maupin (reeds), Eddie Henderson (trumpet), Buster Williams (bass), Billy Hart (drums), and Leon Ndugu Chancler (percussion), all using Swahili names on the sleeve. The record was produced by David Rubinson.

Crossings (Warner Bros., 1972) deepened the fusion of jazz improvisation and electronic textures, with Priester now credited on bass trombone, tenor trombone, alto trombone, and percussion — and with Patrick Gleeson on synthesizer. Sextant (Columbia, 1973) was the most exploratory of the three, with Priester credited as Pepo (Julian Priester) alongside further synthesizer work from Gleeson.

The Mwandishi band dissolved after Sextant, as Hancock pivoted toward funk and the Headhunters.

Solo Work on ECM (1974, 1977)

Directly following Mwandishi, Priester recorded Love, Love (ECM, 1974) as a leader — credited as “Julian Priester Pepo Mtoto.” He produced and mixed the album himself, performing on an extraordinary range of instruments: bass trombone, tenor trombone, alto trombone, baritone horn, post horn, flute, cowbell, percussion, and ARP 2600 and prototype ARP string synthesizer. Patrick Gleeson appeared on synthesizer; the rhythm section featured Leon Ndugu Chancler (drums) and Henry Franklin (bass). The album is both his most ambitious solo statement and one of the more experimental ECM albums of its era.

His second ECM date, Polarization (ECM, 1977), was credited to “Julian Priester and Marine Intrusion” — Marine Intrusion being his working band at the time. The band included Curtis Clark (piano), Ron Stallings (tenor/soprano saxophone), Ray Obiedo (guitar), Heshima Mark Williams (bass), and Augusta Lee Collins (drums). Produced by Manfred Eicher.

V.S.O.P. Mwandishi Reunion (1977)

Priester appears on the third disc of The V.S.O.P. Quintet’s V.S.O.P. (Columbia, 1977), the portion that reassembled the Mwandishi band in a live Montreux setting. He is credited as “Pepo Julian Priester,” playing tenor trombone and bass trombone on sides C1–C3, alongside Maupin, Henderson, Williams, and Hart.

Session Work and Later Career (1975–2000s)

After Mwandishi, Priester remained a highly active sideman. Notable credits include:

  • Freddie Hubbard: Sunburst (Blue Note, 1975), Gears (Milestone, 1975), Fancy Dancer (Blue Note, 1975), Comin’ Through (Capitol, 1977)
  • Bobby Hutcherson: Montara / Now! era work via Blue Note
  • Sam Rivers: Various sessions; he is a member of the Sam Rivers Sextet
  • Dave Holland Quintet: Multiple ECM records — Jumpin’ In (ECM, 1984), Seeds of Time (ECM, 1985), The Razor’s Edge sessions; also Theatre (ECM, 1984, with Anthony Braxton)
  • George Gruntz Concert Jazz Band: Member; European Tour (Denon, 1985)
  • Clifford Jordan: These Are My Roots (Atlantic, 1965), Clifford Jordan In the World (Strata-East, 1972), Masters From Different Worlds (Mapleshade, 1994, co-leading with Jordan and Ran Blake)
  • Ray Charles: Live In Concert (documented)
  • Duke Ellington: New Orleans Suite (Atlantic, 1971), Up In Duke’s Workshop (Pablo, 1979)
  • John Coltrane sessions: Appeared on Coltrane’s Africa / Brass sessions on euphonium (Impulse!, 1961)
  • Andrew Cyrille / John Cage: Atlas Eclipticalis With Winter Music (Mode, 1986)
  • Dave Haney: Active collaborator on later records including Ota Benga of the Batwa and Signals from the Mind (with David Haney Quartet/Trio)

As a leader, Out of This World (Milestone, 2001) appeared in his later catalog.

In 2011, he contributed to the Kannon (Southern Lord, 2015; recorded earlier) project with Stephen O’Malley and others — appearing on conch. He appeared as a guest trombonist on We Insist 2025! (Candid, 2025), a tribute to the Freedom Now Suite.
