From Discogs

Born Gary McDaniel, Chuck Dukowski was the founding bass player and a songwriter for Black Flag, for whom he played with from 1978 until 1983. Prior to his tenure in Black Flag, he founded the band Würm which he resurrected in 1982 while Black Flag was involved in a lawsuit with Unicorn Records which barred Black Flag from recording. In 1985, Chuck formed SWA, who existed until 1992. Chuck was also co-owner of SST Records until late 1989 and continued to work for the label until 1997. Today, he leads the Chuck Dukowski Sextet, which also includes his wife, Lora Norton, and his oldest son, Milo Gonzalez.

Overview

Gary McDaniel (Chuck Dukowski) co-founded Black Flag in 1978 as its bassist and a core songwriter, departing in 1983 after five years at the heart of the Southern California hardcore scene. He had already founded Würm before Black Flag and briefly revived that band during the 1982 Unicorn Records lawsuit that stalled Black Flag’s recording output. After leaving Flag he formed SWA in 1985 (active until 1992) and served as co-owner of SST Records — the label he helped build — through late 1989, remaining a presence there until 1997. His deepest catalogue partnerships are with Greg Ginn and Dez Cadena; he currently leads the Chuck Dukowski Sextet, a family ensemble including his wife Lora Norton and son Milo Gonzalez.

Departure from Black Flag (1983)

The circumstances of Dukowski’s exit are documented in Michael Azerrad’s Our Band Could Be Your Life (pp. 40–41), which underlies the two main Wikipedia accounts. The two Wikipedia pages give slightly different but complementary framings:

Ginn’s ultimatum (My War article): Greg Ginn had grown frustrated with Dukowski’s sense of rhythm. During a 1983 European tour in Germany, Ginn gave Dukowski an ultimatum: quit the band, or Ginn himself would leave. Dukowski accepted the terms and departed. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_War, citing Azerrad pp. 40–41, as of 2026-10-10)

Rollins delivering the news (List of Black Flag band members article): The same source adds that Ginn was attempting to “edge out Dukowski, desiring a new musical direction,” and that in the fall of 1983 Henry Rollins took it upon himself to fire Dukowski in order to resolve the situation — i.e. Rollins delivered the actual dismissal even though the impetus came from Ginn. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Black_Flag_band_members, citing Azerrad pp. 40–41, as of 2026-10-10)

Dukowski’s own words: In a Perfect Sound Forever interview, Dukowski described the departure only obliquely: “It’s a whole big complicated thing, I’ll always be grateful to Henry for reaching out to me at that time, it was tough. It’s sad because I’d just hit my stride with Henry. I was having a lot of success putting songs together for Henry and with him.” He did not characterise it as a firing or voluntary quit. (furious.com/perfect/chuckdukowski.html)

Transition to manager: Despite being fired/forced out, Dukowski did not sever ties entirely — he stayed on to co-run SST Records and moved into a manager and booking-agent role for Black Flag, booking tours until 1986. The manager role appears to have been a pragmatic arrangement rather than a planned consolation: Dukowski retained his SST co-ownership stake and the band still needed his organisational skills. No source suggests the manager transition was pre-planned or negotiated as part of a graceful exit.

Bass credit on My War: With no bassist, Ginn recorded the bass parts himself under the pseudonym “Dale Nixon.” Dukowski is still credited as songwriter on two My War tracks: “My War” and “I Love You.”

Summary of what’s established vs. uncertain

  • Established: Ginn wanted him out for musical/rhythmic reasons; the ultimatum was issued in Germany during the 1983 European tour; Rollins delivered or at least communicated the firing; Dukowski stayed on at SST and as manager.
  • Uncertain / conflicting: Whether the European-tour ultimatum and the fall-1983 Rollins firing are two stages of the same process, or whether one account is imprecise. Both Wikipedia pages cite Azerrad pp. 40–41, so the book likely contains the clearest resolution — but the book text itself was not accessible for this research.
  • No direct Ginn quote on the reasons has been found in any accessible web source.
