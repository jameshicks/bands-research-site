Walter Gibbons

Walter Gibbons

Aug 12, 20268 min read

Walter Gibbons

Discogs ID: 23523

New York DJ and remixer; one of the pioneers of the extended 12-inch remix in disco. Known for his work at Galaxy 21 in New York and for early influential remixes including “Ten Percent” by Double Exposure (Salsoul, 1976) — often cited as the first commercially released 12-inch single.

Credited on Bettye LaVette’s “Doin’ The Best That I Can” (West End Records, WES 22113-X, 1978) — remix and mix credit on one of West End’s earliest soul crossover 12”s.

Credited on Arthur Russell’s Calling Out Of Context (Rough Trade / Audika Records, 2004) for the track “Calling All Kids (Walter Gibbons Remix)” — indicating an earlier remix that Russell had commissioned or collaborated on.

Gospel Period (1980–1986)

Transition timeline

The shift away from secular work unfolded in two stages:

Early 1980 — gospel enters the club sets. In early 1980 Gibbons was offered weekday slots at the Xenon nightclub. He began mixing in back-to-back gospel records alongside his Salsoul obscurities. The gospel programming cleared the dancefloor. After this Xenon episode his club engagements dried up and his remixing work stopped completely — a hiatus that lasted until 1984. (Wikipedia, Walter Gibbons, as of 2026-07-04; 5mag.net, “Mixed With Love”)

Early 1980s — born-again conversion. At some point in the early 1980s (no source pins an exact year) Gibbons became a born-again Christian. His conversion shifted his DJ sets toward gospel-heavy programming and shaped his label and production choices for the rest of his career. He began stacking a Bible next to the turntables and dropped records whose titles or lyrics he considered ungodly — e.g. C.J. & Co.’s “Devil’s Gun” and Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes’ “Bad Luck”. (Wikipedia, Walter Gibbons, as of 2026-07-04; 5mag.net, “Mixed With Love”)

1984–1985 — partial return, Jus Born Records. Gibbons returned to production in 1984 with the Strafe “Set It Off” mix and his Jus Born label. He continued to work within dance music forms but filtered material through his religious commitments.

~1986 — production stops. By 1986, Gibbons had stopped producing entirely. He continued to DJ but with a much heavier gospel slant. (Initial web search synthesis; consistent with his last documented secular remix credit being the Clark Sisters “Time Out” remix for Rejoice Records/A&M, 1986.)

He amassed a 5,000-record gospel collection, purchasing records directly from church congregations across New York. His Jus Born studio was located near a church in Crown Heights, Brooklyn.

Jus Born Records (co-owned by Gibbons, 1984–1985)

Gibbons co-owned Jus Born Records, a label whose name referenced his religious affiliation. The label had at least three releases, all dance records produced during his gospel period:

Rejoice Records / A&M (1986)

Gibbons remixed the Clark Sisters’ “Time Out” for its 1986 12-inch release on Rejoice Records (a gospel imprint distributed by A&M). This is the clearest documented case of Gibbons working for an established gospel label rather than his own imprint. The 12-inch included multiple Gibbons mixes: Detroit Mix, Instrumental, A Capella, Sax Dub, Cornerstone Vocals, Cornerstone Mix. The album Heart & Soul from which the single drew was produced by Norbert Putnam.

Sources: Root Down Records listing; Tim Lawrence, “Disco Madness"

"Stand On The Word” / Celestial Choir (independent, 1982)

Gibbons was an early champion of “Stand On The Word” by the Celestial Choir, recorded at the First Baptist Church of Crown Heights in 1982 and initially pressed as an independent church production (a few hundred copies sold at the congregation). Gibbons purchased copies and began playing the track at clubs, helping break it to the NYC underground scene. He did not produce or remix this record; his role was as DJ/tastemaker. (The 1985 commercial re-recording as the Joubert Singers was released on Next Plateau Records and was not a Gibbons production.)

Sources: Red Bull Music Academy, “Stand On The Word”; 5mag.net, “Mixed With Love”

Arthur Russell Collaboration

Gibbons and Arthur Russell developed what contemporaries described as a “dependable (if occasionally tempestuous) working relationship.” By the time they began working together, Gibbons had renounced his club career for evangelical Christianity and was employed as a buyer at Rock and Soul Records in Midtown Manhattan; Russell was one of the few figures who continued commissioning him for remix work.

Chronology of collaborations

“Go Bang” (Dinosaur L) — unreleased mix, ca. early 1980s. Gibbons remixed “Go Bang” (the track later known as “Go Bang #5”) at Blank Tapes studio. The result was described as retaining “the freestyle spirit of the original sessions” — longer and more unstructured than François Kevorkian’s celebrated 12-inch mix. The Gibbons version remained unreleased until a 2016 reissue on Get On Down paired it with the Kevorkian mix. Exact recording date is unconfirmed; the Kevorkian 12-inch was released on Sleeping Bag Records in 1982, suggesting the Gibbons session may have been roughly contemporaneous. (Fact Magazine, April 2016)

“Schoolbell/Treehouse” (Indian Ocean) — Sleeping Bag Records, 1986. Russell loved “Set It Off” and approached Gibbons to mix “Schoolbell/Treehouse.” The Gibbons mix featured “a spaced-out array of bongos, piercing hi-hats, discordant synth stabs, scratchy cello solos.” Released on Sleeping Bag Records (Russell’s label) in 1986; later compiled on The Sleeping Bag Sessions. (Tim Lawrence, “Mixed With Love: The Walter Gibbons Salsoul Anthology,” Suss’d Records, 2004; Wikipedia, Walter Gibbons, as of 2026-07-13)

“Let’s Go Swimming” (Coastal Dub) — Logarhythm/Rough Trade, September 22, 1986. Russell invited Gibbons to develop a mix of “Let’s Go Swimming.” The resulting Coastal Dub — credited on the vinyl as “Mixed with love by Walter Gibbons” — was described by producer Gary Lucas as “a visionary, psychedelic soundscape.” The bongo-driven dub “lurched from beat to beat.” Released as side B of the 12-inch single (Logarhythm LR-1002-1). (Discogs master 48546; last.fm)

“Calling All Kids” (Walter Gibbons Remix) — recorded ca. 1985–1990, released 2004. Russell commissioned Gibbons to remix “Calling All Kids,” a track recorded at home and in various studios during 1985–1990 as part of a projected Rough Trade album. The album was abandoned when Russell fell ill (he died April 4, 1992). The Gibbons remix was not released in Gibbons’s lifetime. It surfaced posthumously as track 12 on Calling Out of Context (Audika AU-1001-2 / Rough Trade RTRADLP161), released March 16, 2004 — the first Audika Records release, compiled by Melissa Jones, Steve Knutson, and Tom “Sisu” Lee. The remix runs 7:15 and closes the album. (Wikipedia, Calling Out of Context, as of 2026-07-13; Audika/Bandcamp)

Note on the Strut compilation title. The 2010 Strut retrospective Jungle Music (Mixed With Love: Essential & Unreleased Remixes 1976–1986) — the subtitle uses 1986 as the endpoint — implicitly brackets his active remix period. The Russell “Calling All Kids” remix falls within or just after this window but remained unreleased and is not on the Strut set.

Death

Walter Gibbons died on September 23, 1994 from AIDS-related complications, aged 40 (born April 2, 1954). He spent his final weeks living alone in a YMCA. One of his final acts was to donate his record collection to an AIDS charity based in San Francisco. There were few obituaries at the time of his death. He had toured Japan in 1993, where he was warmly received. In the final years before his death he played occasional events in New York and Japan, staying focused on gospel music and his faith.

Sources: Wikipedia, Walter Gibbons (as of 2026-07-13); The AIDS Memorial, Facebook; 5mag.net, “Mixed With Love”; 5mag.net, “PRAISE: A tribute to Walter Gibbons” (2024)
