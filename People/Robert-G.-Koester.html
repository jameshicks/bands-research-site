Robert G. Koester

Born Robert Gregg Koester, October 30, 1932, Wichita, Kansas. Died May 12, 2021, Chicago, Illinois, age 88. American record store owner, independent label founder, producer, and blues/jazz documentarian. Known universally as Bob Koester. Founder and original owner of Delmark Records and operator of the Jazz Record Mart in Chicago. Discogs artist ID: 319451. Name variations on Discogs include: Bob Koester, Bob G. Koester, G. Koester, RGK, Robert Koester, Mr. Robert G. Koester, Robert J. Koester.

Early Life and St. Louis, 1953–1957

Koester grew up in Wichita and developed an early obsession with jazz and blues records. In 1953, while still a student, he opened a record store on Delmar Boulevard in St. Louis, Missouri. The same year he started the Delmar label — named after the street — to document regional jazz and blues he couldn’t find on existing commercial labels. His earliest productions include Wake The Levee! (1956, The Dixie Stompers) and On Parade (1958, George Lewis’ Ragtime Band), both on Delmar Records. From the outset the label’s posture was archival and documentation-first: Koester was recording artists he valued rather than chasing commercial potential.

Chicago, the Jazz Record Mart, and Renaming Delmark, 1958–1963

Koester relocated to Chicago in 1958 and opened the first Jazz Record Mart — a specialty record shop that would become a Chicago institution for collectors, musicians, and journalists for the next six decades. The store’s physical location on North Lincoln Avenue served as an informal hub connecting the city’s blues and jazz communities: musicians could be found there, collectors could find rare pressings, and Koester himself maintained relationships across the South and West Side blues scenes and the emerging avant-garde.

In 1963 the label was renamed Delmark Records after a legal dispute over the Delmar name. The Discogs label entry for Delmark (ID: 33470) carries credits stretching from 1953 through the present.

Blues Documentation, 1963–1969

The mid-1960s were Delmark’s most concentrated period of Chicago electric blues documentation. Koester produced prolifically across this era, recording artists who were working the South and West Side club circuit but had minimal commercial visibility outside Chicago.

Sleepy John Estes was among the first major artists Koester put on record with any systematic intent: The Legend Of Sleepy John Estes (1963, DL-603) was followed by Brownsville Blues (1965) and Sleepy John Estes In Europe (1966). Big Joe Williams appeared on Piney Woods Blues (1961, Delmar) and Mississippi’s Big Joe Williams And His Nine String Guitar (1962, Folkways — Koester credited as Recorded By and Supervised By). Speckled Red was documented with The Dirty Dozens (1961). Folk and country blues were treated as seriously as the amplified Chicago sound.

The label’s flagship blues release and one of the most celebrated recordings in the Chicago electric blues tradition is Junior Wells’ Hoodoo Man Blues (1965, DL-612). Koester produced and handled photography on the session; Buddy Guy played guitar uncredited on the sleeve. The record is routinely cited as a defining document of the Chicago blues style. Koester’s production approach on this and similar sessions was minimal-intervention: capture the performance, trust the artist.

Magic Sam (Samuel Gene Maghett) became one of Delmark’s most important artists in the late 1960s. West Side Soul (1968, DS-615) and Black Magic (1969, DS-620) are the two landmarks; Koester produced both. Magic Sam died in December 1969 at 32, and the label subsequently assembled posthumous documentation including The Magic Sam Legacy (1989). See Magic Sam for the full West Side blues context.

Other significant blues productions from this period include Carey Bell’s Blues Harp (1969), produced by Koester and supervised; Buddy Guy’s Left My Blues In San Francisco (not a Delmark album — Guy was on Chess — but Koester and Guy overlapped through the Wells sessions); Eddie Boyd’s Southside Blues Jam (1970), a session with Buddy Guy and Otis Spann; and extended work with Jimmy Dawkins, including All For Business (1973) and Blisterstring later.

The AACM Partnership, 1966–1971

The relationship between Koester and the AACM is one of the most consequential in the history of American avant-garde music. Beginning in 1966, Delmark became the primary commercial outlet for a group of composers and improvisers who had almost no access to major label infrastructure. Koester’s willingness to record and release their work — at a time when these recordings had limited commercial prospects — preserved music that is now considered foundational.

Koester’s role in the AACM recordings was as facilitator and producer rather than artistic director. Roscoe Mitchell, Joseph Jarman, Muhal Richard Abrams, and Anthony Braxton brought their own visions fully formed; Koester provided studio access, label infrastructure, and in most cases liner notes or supervision credits. The 1971 For Alto remains Braxton’s most iconic early statement and one of the most influential recordings of the entire free jazz period.

The Sun Ra relationship was complicated by Ra’s own label El Saturn Records, which had issued recordings independently. The Delmark releases (Sun Song DL-411 and Sound Of Joy DS-414) drew from recordings made earlier by Ra’s Arkestra; Koester produced them for broader distribution.

1970s: Continued Production and Consolidation

The pace of production continued through the 1970s, though the AACM burst had passed its peak. Key productions:

  • Southside Blues Jam (1970, Buddy Guy, Otis Spann, Junior Wells) — ensemble blues session
  • Things To Come From Those Now Gone (1975, Muhal Richard Abrams)
  • Cold Day In Hell (1975, Luther Allison) — Koester as executive producer
  • Live At Mandel Hall (1974, The Art Ensemble Of Chicago) — documenting AEC live; Trio Records co-release for Japan market

Koester also produced for and supervised releases on Pearl Records (5), a Delmark sublabel used for blues archival material, and occasionally licensed material to the UK label Esquire and French label Goody.

Jazz Record Mart: the Store as Community Infrastructure

The Jazz Record Mart was more than a retail operation. As one of the few stores specializing in blues and jazz in Chicago across the 1960s–80s, it functioned as a meeting point between musicians, researchers, critics, and collectors. Koester’s floor-level knowledge of the catalog — he could locate obscure pressings, had personal acquaintance with many of the artists whose records he stocked, and maintained informal archival practices — made the store an institution distinct from its retail function. Koester sold the Jazz Record Mart in 2016 and subsequently opened the smaller Bob’s Blues & Jazz Mart in its place; he continued working until close to his death.

Later Decades: 1980s–2018

Production continued without interruption. Koester’s credits extend from 1956 through 2018, with his role shifting across decades — from direct producer to supervising producer to production manager as Delmark brought on additional staff including Steve Wagner (who became the primary in-studio producer from roughly the 1990s onward) and Stu Black (engineer on many 1960s–70s sessions). Koester retained overall curatorial authority.

The 2003 Four Compositions (GTM) 2000 marked Anthony Braxton’s return to Delmark more than three decades after his debut there — Koester produced and supervised. The relationship between Koester and Braxton thus spans 1968 to 2003, an unusual continuity across the full arc of Braxton’s career.

Scale and Scope of Production

Koester’s Discogs credits — across producer, supervised by, executive producer, recording supervisor, liner notes, photography, and compilation producer roles — span approximately 200 unique master releases, with pressings counts that collectively represent roughly 869 separate release entries. This figure reflects the extraordinary span of Delmark’s catalog (1953–2021) and Koester’s near-total personal involvement across it. No other independent American jazz or blues label has a comparable example of single-individual curatorial continuity over a seven-decade span.

Beyond Delmark proper, Koester’s credits appear on: Pearl Records (5) (blues and jazz archival sublabel under Delmark), Sackville Recordings (Canadian jazz label distributed through Delmark), P-Vine Records (Japanese label that issued Delmark anniversary compilations), Testament Records (Koester credited on tape editing for Peg Leg Howell, 1964), Folkways Records (supervision credit on Big Joe Williams, 1962), Earwig Music (special production assistance on a 1979 release), and minor credits on UK labels Esquire and 77 Records.

His alias on Discogs, “Greg Roberts (4),” is listed but appears to be a minor name variation entry rather than a significant alternate identity.

Death and Legacy

Koester died May 12, 2021, in Chicago. He was 88. Delmark Records continued operating after his death. The label he founded in 1953 remains active — one of the longest-running independent American jazz and blues labels.

The depth of his contribution is most visible in what would not exist without him: the first commercially available recordings of Roscoe Mitchell, Anthony Braxton, Joseph Jarman, and Muhal Richard Abrams as leader; the canonical Chicago electric blues documents by Junior Wells and Magic Sam; and Sun Ra’s earliest wide-distribution releases. These are not peripheral items — they are central documents of twentieth-century American music.
