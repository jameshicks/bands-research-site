Tim Friese-Greene

Producer, musician. Born Timothy Alan Friese-Greene. English. Discogs ID: 60622.

Grandson of filmmaker Claude Friese-Greene; great-grandson of photographer and inventor William Friese-Greene. De facto fourth member of Talk Talk from 1983 until the band’s dissolution in 1991–92; co-wrote all songs on The Colour of Spring, Spirit of Eden, and Laughing Stock with Mark Hollis. Alias: Heligoland (2). Also records with his wife Lee Friese-Greene under the name Short-Haired Domestic.

Career

Early Engineering (1975–1981)

Friese-Greene worked as a staff engineer at Wessex Studios, London through the second half of the 1970s, accumulating a wide range of recording credits before moving into production. Verified engineering credits from this period include the Boomtown Rats’ A Tonic For The Troops (Mercury, 1978), Art Garfunkel’s Fate For Breakfast (CBS, 1979), and Five Live Kursaals (CBS, 1977). He had no prior experimental music background when he joined Talk Talk.

Production (1979–1983)

Friese-Greene began producing in parallel with his engineering work. Early production credits include The Records (Virgin, 1979), Tight Fit (Jive, 1982) — for which he also arranged — and Thomas Dolby’s debut album The Golden Age Of Wireless (Venice In Peril / EMI, 1982) and its single “She Blinded Me With Science.” He also produced Huang Chung’s self-titled debut (Arista, 1982).

Talk Talk (1984–1991)

He was brought in to assist on Talk Talk’s second album It’s My Life (1984) and immediately became Mark Hollis’s primary songwriting collaborator. From The Colour of Spring (1986) onward, all Talk Talk compositions carry Hollis / Friese-Greene credits. He played keyboards — piano, organ, harmonium, mellotron, variophon — on the studio recordings, but did not tour with the band and was absent from their publicity.

On Spirit of Eden (1988) and Laughing Stock (1991) he is credited as co-producer alongside Hollis, playing piano, organ, and harmonium in the improvised ensemble sessions that formed the raw material for both records. On Laughing Stock he is explicitly credited: “Piano, Organ, Written-By; Producer, Harmonium.”

Post-Talk Talk Production (1992–present)

Friese-Greene’s longest sustained production relationship post-Talk Talk was with Catherine Wheel, for whom he produced six albums across 1992–2016 and contributed as a performer (Hammond organ on Chrome, electric organ and keyboards on Happy Days, organ and piano on Adam And Eve). Family background

William Friese-Greene (born William Edward Green, 7 September 1855 – 5 May 1921) was a Bristol-born photographer and prolific inventor. He married Helena Friese in 1874 and hyphenated her surname onto his own. He set up his own studio in Bath in 1876 and expanded to Bath, Bristol, and Plymouth by 1881. He held over 70 patents; his most significant was patent no. 10131 (21 June 1889), a motion-picture camera co-developed with engineer Mortimer Evans, which put him among the earliest claimants to the invention of cinematography. He also patented an early two-colour film process in 1905. He spent his money on inventing, went bankrupt three times, was once jailed, and died in poverty — collapsing at a cinema trade meeting at the Connaught Rooms, London, on 5 May 1921.

Claude Friese-Greene (3 May 1898 – 6 January 1943) was William’s son, and Tim Friese-Greene’s grandfather (confirmed by the Claude Friese-Greene Wikipedia article: “he was the grandfather of musician and music producer Tim Friese-Greene”). After William’s death Claude continued developing his father’s additive colour-film system, eventually renaming it the Spectrum Colour Film process (originally called Biocolour). His most celebrated work was The Open Road (1926), a road-trip documentary shot in Spectrum Colour from Land’s End to John o’ Groats — restored by the BBC in 2005. He worked as cinematographer on more than 60 films between 1923 and 1943 and was one of the first in Britain to shoot in Technicolor. He died as the result of an accident while filming at Denham Film Studios in January 1943. The BBC ran a series on the Friese-Greene legacy in 2006 presented by Dan Cruickshank.

Wessex Studios career — confirmed origin story

Confirmed: Friese-Greene started as a tape operator at Wessex Sound Studios, London, in the mid-1970s. A friend who worked at Air Studios as a tape operator took him to visit shortly before he left school; he became “totally smitten with the whole idea of working in a studio” (his phrase, quoted in multiple secondary sources drawing on the Penny Black Music interview). He moved from tape operator to full engineer when two staff engineers left Wessex virtually simultaneously; he underwent a brief in-house apprenticeship and filled the gap. Early engineering credits from this period include sessions with Hawkwind and The Rumour, alongside the credits documented via Discogs (Boomtown Rats, Art Garfunkel, Five Live Kursaals).

How he joined Talk Talk

Wikipedia states he was “brought into Talk Talk to assist in the remixing of It’s My Life (1984).” This is the original point of contact — remixing the title single — rather than being hired as full producer from the outset. He subsequently co-produced the album and became a permanent creative partner.

Spirit of Eden recording sessions

Recording commenced 11 May 1987 at Wessex Studios and continued through May–July 1987, with instrumental overdubs resuming 19 October 1987. The album was completed 11 March 1988 but withheld by EMI until 12 September 1988. Sessions ran five-day weeks from 11am to midnight in deliberate near-darkness: oil projector, gentle strobe lighting, three Anglepoise lamps. TFG stated in interviews that “Spirit of Eden was largely written on a drum machine,” distinguishing it from Laughing Stock, where starting points came from Lee Harris playing drum patterns in the rehearsal room. He also stated that Spirit of Eden was the one album he had the most difficulty with — a reaction that surprised one interviewer. Source: Moving The River blog (2022), Penny Black Music interview (2006, referenced widely but page currently returning 403).

Laughing Stock sessions

Approximately 50 session musicians were assembled; only 18 appear on the final record. The sessions were heavily improvised; TFG used digital editing to assemble arrangements from hours of recordings, discarding an estimated 80–90% of the raw material.

Tinnitus and the abandoned 2014/15 folk-funk project

The 2015 Bandcamp single I Would Change None For You (2015 A/B) (Calcium Chloride; two digital tracks, accordion) is a release under his own name (not the Heligoland alias). The B-side “All I Hear Is Foley” was to be part of a “folk-funk” album pairing accordion with Fender Rhodes electric piano, but the project was abandoned due to ongoing tinnitus problems. TFG’s note on the release: “I remain fond of both these songs, even though the renditions are a bit skanky.” This is the only explicit public mention of tinnitus in the available record and helps explain the sparse release schedule post-2005.

Melodic Apoptosis (2022)

Released 22 July 2022 on Calcium Chloride, under his own name. 8 tracks; instruments are melodica, vibraphone, drums, and string section. Recorded “in the roof of a deserted language school during the first lockdown in 2020.” Limited to 350 copies on 180g vinyl, cut from 96k digital masters at Gearbox Records, pressed at Optimal, matt varnished reverse board sleeve. Personnel beyond TFG: Parallax Orchestra, Jack Ross, John Barber; artwork by Nathan Carter (same as Heligoland LP, 2000). This is his most recent known solo release.

Short-Haired Domestic — release date

The Short-Haired Domestic album (already documented via Discogs) was released 21 August 2020, per press coverage. The album concept — each track sung in a different language — was described as coming to TFG “piecemeal over a couple of years.”
