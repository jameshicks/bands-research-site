Rob Chapman

Vocalist, lyricist, music journalist. Bristol, UK. Discogs ID: 652161.

Vocalist and sole consistent songwriter of Glaxo Babies across all three phases (1977–1980, 1985–1990, 2015 reunion). All written-by credits on “Put Me On The Guest List” go to Chapman; Phase Two lyrics credited to him throughout The Porlock Factor. After Glaxo Babies he joined The Transmitters. Also active as a music journalist and author (website: rob-chapman.com; credited on Rocks Back Pages).

”Dreams Interrupted” Liner Notes (2006) — Notes (web)

The sleeve notes to “Dreams Interrupted: The Bewilderbeat Years 1978–1980” (Cherry Red, 2006) were written by Chapman in January 2006 and published in full on bristolarchiverecords.com. They constitute Chapman’s first-person account of Glaxo Babies’ Phase One (1977–1979). Key points documented:

Formation (Autumn 1977): Chapman placed “Musicians wanted” ads in both Sounds and NME reading “Musicians wanted to take over where the Velvet Underground left off.” Tom Nichols responded by letter, inviting Chapman to a rehearsal at the Dockland Settlement, City Road, St Pauls — five minutes’ walk from Chapman’s home in Montpelier. He met Nichols and Dan Catsis there on a cold November night; no drummer present. After hearing their “angular little sketches” (some lasting 30 seconds, some up to four minutes, half sounding like Trout Mask Replica), Chapman immediately committed. “I thought they were fucking magnificent.”

Band name origin: Dan Catsis explained it at the bar that same evening. Glaxo manufactured baby milk powder; Catsis had xeroxed a Glaxo baby food ad repeatedly at art college until the image of a smiling child became grotesque, but the caption still read “smiling one year old Gl*xo Baby.” Chapman found the juxtaposition immediately appealing.

First gig (December 1977): A Christmas party at the Dockland Settlement — three weeks after the first rehearsal. The Sex Pistols were rumoured to be playing the Bamboo Club in St Pauls that same night under a pseudonym, but the club burned down the night before. The Glaxo Babies assembled eight songs including Beatles and Stones covers; Tom’s mother had sewn the band name into the back of his jacket.

Early reception: Their second gig at the “BQ club” (a hardcore punk venue) was met with near-silence. A year later audience members would tell Chapman they’d seen that gig and thought the band were “really heavy. Really intense.” Chapman notes the band were initially isolated from the Bristol scene — they “didn’t really know anyone” — and a rumour circulated that “the Gl*xo Babies hate everybody.” Chapman writes: “We didn’t, we just didn’t know anyone, but we were happy to indulge the myth.”

Third gig (March 1978): At Redland Teacher Training College, supporting The Only Ones. Only Ones guitarist John Perry told them he thought they were really good: “That gave us a kind of validation and confidence.”

Cave rehearsal space: Around this time Tom Nichols found a rehearsal space in a cave cut into the Avon gorge, accessible through unlit tunnels. They rehearsed there three nights a week: “That’s when we started to get really fucking good.”

Rock Garden gig (summer 1978): Their first London gig, supporting a group called Resistance. Three members took too much amphetamine sulphate beforehand; Chapman and Nichols spent most of the set rigid and with their backs to the audience. Dan Catsis was embarrassed because art school friends had come to see them. The band assumed they would not be asked back for a second planned gig — but weeks later Resistance phoned to ask why they hadn’t shown up for the return date. “They thought we were great.”

Ashton Court Free Festival (summer 1978): First on the bill at a Saturday lunchtime, playing to “a field full of disinterested hippies.” That evening they played a pub gig in Kingswood arranged by drummer Geoff Alsopp, at which Bristol Rovers football supporters hassled their girlfriends and threw beer at them. The band eventually placated the crowd by bluffing through White Riot, Pretty Vacant, and Janie Jones.

Clifton scene distance: Chapman describes the Glaxo Babies as standing apart from the Clifton art-school scene (The Pop Group, Essential Bop, Art Objects, the “Fried Egg bands”). He writes that their outsider status was their strength: “We weren’t part of any clique. That’s why we didn’t sound like anybody else.” Specific anecdotes: discovering that Dan Catsis was listening to The Edgar Broughton Band in 1978 (“Far out” — “We meant everything we said, maaaan!”); a hushed pub conversation about the virtues of Chic’s “Le Freak,” kept covert lest “some mohair-clad twat might overhear us.”

First single and rapid rise: Simon Edwards approached them in December 1978 to demo tracks; they ran through the four songs that would appear on “This Is Your Life” EP plus “Avoiding the Issue.” They recorded at Crescent Studios in Bath. By March 1979 Chapman claims they had an indie chart number one — “although this was in the days before there was proper documentation of such things so you won’t find verification of that fact in any punk encyclopedia.” The band subsequently played the John Peel roadshow at Bristol Polytechnic (which Chapman calls “possibly our best ever gig”), the Anson Rooms (opening for the Human League), and Salisbury Art College (opening for Adam and the Ants, of whom Chapman is withering: “Adam was already a star in his own head”).

Drummer change: Geoff Alsopp was sacked — “not so much for musical differences as personal ones” — chiefly because his friends were trouble on the road. The final straw was a van journey back from a Dingwalls gig during which they kept stopping for a friend of Alsopp’s to be sick. Charles (“Charlie”) Llewellyn joined on drums; at his first rehearsal the band asked him to “play like Neu” and he locked effortlessly into a “primitive motorik pulse.”

Tony Wrafter joins: Wrafter had previously been in a band called Peru, which had pulled rank on the Glaxo Babies at a double-booking at the Stonehouse (“We’ve played Bath”). The Glaxo Babies blew them off stage. Chapman finished that gig covered in blood after inadvertently smashing a wine glass while performing, too numbed by amphetamines to notice.

Ideological split with Tom Nichols: Chapman describes a “massive ideological rift” in which Nichols grew “increasingly disenchanted” with what he saw as the nihilistic content of some of Chapman’s songs. For his part Chapman “perceived a certain ‘sniff of the barmaids apron’ approach to jazz developing within the unit” and was “vehemently opposed to this tendency.” He quotes a fanzine called Allied Propaganda (found in Rough Trade on Portobello Road), in which Nichols said of Chapman: “Rob was a good guy, but he was into ‘songs’ and we weren’t” — the word “songs” in inverted commas.

Final session (25 June 1979): At Crescent Studios for what should have been the debut LP. Chapman describes the session as “as depressing an occasion as I can ever remember” — improvising while Chapman sat “like a spare part.” A version of “Christine Keeler” was recorded as a token gesture; it was later Record Mirror’s Single of the Week, but “by then there wasn’t a band to promote it.” The day after, Chapman phoned Nichols “in vaguely conciliatory mood” and cited early Pink Floyd as a model. Nichols’s reply: “We’re not compromising.” Chapman left the band.

Penultimate gig: The Glaxo Babies played with the Cure in Cheltenham shortly before the split. Chapman and Nichols argued in the van on the way there. Two “unassuming young guys dressed in black” who came to their dressing room to say they were great turned out to be Robert Smith and Lol Tolhurst.

Title of “Put Me On The Guest List”: Chapman explains in the notes that a 1980 compilation of early Heartbeat demos was named after an incident at a Tiffany’s gig in Bristol; disgusted by the room full of freeloaders, he changed a lyric of “Who Killed Bruce Lee” from “put me in the picture” to “put me on the guest list.”

Date signed: January 2006.
