Overview

Gary Marx (born Mark Frederick Pearman) co-founded The Sisters of Mercy with Andrew Eldritch around 1979–1980, playing lead guitar and contributing songwriting through the band’s formative Merciful Release years alongside Craig Adams, Wayne Hussey, and drummer-machine Doktor Avalanche. He departed in 1985 and immediately formed Ghost Dance with vocalist Anne-Marie Hurst, releasing on Karbon and briefly Chrysalis before that band ran its course. After a lengthy absence from recording, he began releasing solo material from 2004 onward, including tracks originally composed for a mid-1990s Sisters session that Eldritch never put out.

Notes (web)

The 1995 Sisters of Mercy material and Nineteen Ninety Five and Nowhere (2007)

In 1995 Andrew Eldritch invited Marx back to contribute to what would have been a fourth Sisters of Mercy studio album. Marx wrote eleven backing tracks in a couple of weeks and delivered them to Eldritch. Eldritch then went silent without explanation. As Marx described it in interviews: “Once Andrew fell silent the tracks were shelved — I had no intention of doing anything with them. This wasn’t because I thought the music wasn’t up to the mark, it just wasn’t the music I was interested in making for myself at that time.” A second characterisation from the same period of interviews states that Eldritch “backed out of the project without uttering a single word” after Marx had delivered the eleven tracks. These quotes circulate via SistersWiki (sisterswiki.org/Gary_Marx — blocked 403, seen only in search snippets) and the Louder Than War interview (louderthanwar.com/first-and-last-and-always-an-interview-with-gary-marx-the-sisters-of-mercy/ — article text truncated before reaching this section in the fetched version); they cannot be verified against the primary source text directly.

Marx eventually completed the material himself — writing lyrics and adding vocals — and released it across releases from 2004 onward, culminating in the album Nineteen Ninety Five and Nowhere (D-Monic, 2007). The album title makes the provenance explicit.

Eldritch’s public comment. The only known instance of Andrew Eldritch addressing this episode publicly is in a November 2011 interview with The Quietus (“Off To Never Land: The Sisters Of Mercy Interviewed”, thequietus.com/interviews/sisters-of-mercy-interview/, published 2011-11-12). The interviewer put it to Eldritch that “in the mid-nineties, you apparently approached Gary Marx to renew your old songwriting partnership; he wrote some music, but you supposedly then backed out of the project without explanation.” Eldritch’s complete response was: “That is not how it happened.” He said nothing further on the subject. The exchange was then interrupted by a statement from someone identifying as Marx’s manager (“Mr J Hossain, manager of Mr G Marx”), accusing the interviewer of unrelated conduct at a Sisters anniversary show. Eldritch never offered his version of events in that interview or, as far as searches can establish, elsewhere.

What is not established. No source explains why Eldritch did not pursue the material — whether it was rejected on creative grounds, whether contractual or label pressures were involved, or what specifically he disputes in the standard account. His “That is not how it happened” directly contests the framing that he backed out without explanation, but he does not say what did happen.

Louder Than War interview — full 1995 section (primary source, obtained via agent-browser 2026-11-12)

The article does contain Marx’s direct account of the 1995 collaboration in full. The relevant exchange, verbatim:

MA: In 1995, you demoed tracks for Andrew Eldritch for a potential fourth Sisters album. Were old wounds opened?

GM: I ended up out in Leeds one night and got talking to someone who worked with Andrew. I didn’t know this person well but he was a good mate of (long-serving Sisters roadie and ‘family’ member) Jez Webb’s, which struck me as recommendation enough. I let my guard down and got talking to him about Andrew’s lack of activity and recorded output. I probably said something like, “If he’s short of songs I can give him some tomorrow.”

Quite possibly as soon as the next day Andrew rang me – not sure how long it had been prior to that since we’d spoken, but years rather than months, quite possibly nine or ten. We arranged to meet at The Faversham (pub) in Leeds. On the surface everything was fine between us. We seemed to agree that the timing was right and that it would mean a lot to people who loved the band to see a new Eldritch/Marx credit on a record sleeve. It was my idea rather than his and I was the one initially laying out the parameters, choosing the safe word: ‘songwriting.’ I had no interest in recording or playing live with him whatsoever and purely wanted to focus on writing an album’s worth of songs for whatever edition of The Sisters he was up to by then. I’d made that clear from the first minute of our phone conversation – ideally I wanted this process to be speedy (not in his more commonly understood interpretation of the word.) Yes, we were back laughing at the bar in The Faversham, but I had no desire to spend a massive amount of time in his company. He was living in Hamburg and I said I was prepared to go out there to write with him, but that’s where he imposed his one condition: no face-to-face collaborations; send me complete backing tracks.

We never ever got to comparing scar tissue – I sent him the tracks, the line went dead. I wasn’t in the mood for games so that’s where we left it.

A second exchange covers the 2004–2007 release of that material:

MA: You revived and released the 1995 demos made for Eldritch between 2004 and 2007 as Nineteen Ninety Five and Nowhere.

GM: Once Andrew fell silent the tracks were shelved, I had no intention of doing anything with them – this wasn’t because I thought the music wasn’t up to the mark, it just wasn’t the music I was interested in making for myself at that time.

Source: First and Last and Always: An Interview with Gary Marx (the Sisters Of Mercy) — Louder Than War, December 2021 (article-browser snapshot obtained 2026-11-12).

What the interview establishes (primary source, now confirmed):

  • The collaboration was Marx’s idea, not Eldritch’s.
  • It was Marx who set the terms (songwriting only, no live work, no recording together).
  • Eldritch’s sole condition was no face-to-face — Marx should send complete backing tracks.
  • Marx sent the tracks; Eldritch did not respond. Marx describes it: “the line went dead.”
  • Marx offers no explanation for why Eldritch went silent — he records the fact of the silence but does not speculate on Eldritch’s reason.
  • The phrase “fell silent” appears in a later exchange about reviving the material, used by Marx as a descriptor of the outcome, not an explanation.
  • The interview gives no hint that Eldritch gave any reason, privately or publicly.

What the interview does NOT establish: Why Eldritch did not respond. No creative, contractual, or personal explanation appears anywhere in the article.

Conflict note: Earlier notes in this file, citing search snippets attributed to SistersWiki and other secondary sources, described the collaboration as Eldritch inviting Marx back and stated he “backed out of the project without uttering a single word.” The primary source is more nuanced: Marx makes clear it was his own idea, and the framing “backed out” does not appear in the article — the article only records that Eldritch went silent after receiving the tracks. The “backed out without a single word” phrasing (also challenged by Eldritch in The Quietus 2011) may originate from secondary paraphrasing rather than Marx’s own words.
