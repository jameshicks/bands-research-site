Chris Lombardi

Oct 15, 202615 min read

Chris Lombardi

Founder, Matador Records. New York City (later Los Angeles). Discogs ID: 2528764.

Founded Matador in 1989, running it from his NYC apartment. The first release (OLE 001, H.P. Zinker) was recorded at Wharton Tiers’s Fun City studio. Brought in Gerard Cosloy as co-owner in 1990. In 1999 Lombardi and Cosloy bought back Capitol Records’ 49% stake in the label. Continued operating Matador after the 2002 Beggars Group acquisition.

Background Before Matador (pre-1989)

Early musical formation

As a pre-teen and teenager Lombardi was drawn to classic rock, attending shows by Queen, Led Zeppelin, and Kiss. He became a regular at Bleecker Bob’s record store in Greenwich Village, where he discovered British post-punk and indie music through UK imports and magazines — principally Melody Maker and NME. He attended shows at CBGB’s and was embedded in the New York scene well before starting a label. [source: https://www.loudandquiet.com/interview/chris-lombardi/]

Education — Bennington College

Lombardi attended Bennington College in Vermont. The college lists him as class of ‘90 (Christopher P. Lombardi ‘90), but the Loud and Quiet interview headline states he “dropped out of college in 1989 to start Matador Records in his apartment.” These two facts are consistent: enrolled likely ca. 1986–88, left without graduating to launch the label. [source: https://www.bennington.edu/bennington-network/outsized-impact/christopher-p-lombardi; https://www.loudandquiet.com/interview/chris-lombardi/]

Dutch East India Trading

Before founding Matador, Lombardi worked at Dutch East India Trading, the Long Island-based distribution company that handled Homestead Records (among other indie labels). The role gave him contacts at record stores across the country and an inside view of how independent labels operated. It is also where he encountered both Gerard Cosloy (who ran Homestead) and Patrick Amory, his future Matador partners. [source: https://artists.spotify.com/en/blog/label-spotlight-matador-records; https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2017/01/we-sign-music-we-love-the-matador-origin-story-interview.html]

Source conflict note: The Loud and Quiet interview (direct Lombardi quotes) describes his pre-Matador employer as “Homestead Records” itself (“I knew record stores up and down the country through my job at Homestead Records”). The Spotify for Artists label spotlight and the Hypebot/PIAS accounts are more precise, describing Dutch East India Trading as Homestead’s distributor and Lombardi’s actual employer. These are not necessarily contradictory — he may have thought of the work as “at Homestead” while technically employed by the distributor — but the DEIT framing appears in multiple secondary accounts and is likely the more accurate description of his employer. The Spotify piece states explicitly: “Lombardi worked for Homestead’s distributor, Dutch East India Trading.”

Motivation for starting the label

Lombardi described founding Matador as initially a hobby: “to document some bands playing in New York at the time.” He entered the music business “purely by accident,” driven by enthusiasm for the local scene rather than a planned business strategy. [source: https://www.loudandquiet.com/interview/chris-lombardi/]

Current Role at Matador

Matador is co-owned by Lombardi, Gerard Cosloy, and Patrick Amory, with the other half held by Beggars Group. The label’s official description (matadorrecords.com/pages/info) presents the three as operating collectively: “The label continues to be run by Patrick Amory, Chris Lombardi and Gerard Cosloy,” focused on independent A&R, art, production, and direct-store distribution. The catalog and release schedule are described as reflecting the “tastes of the company’s owners.”

No public source provides a granular functional division of labour among the three. What is available:

  • Patrick Amory holds the formal title of President & Co-Owner (joined 1994 as label/general manager, later became partner). The Org lists him with 39 direct reports, indicating he runs operational day-to-day management. theorg.com; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Amory
  • Chris Lombardi carries the title of co-founder/co-owner with no distinct operational title in any available source. A Loud and Quiet interview (date unlisted, but post-2010) quotes him saying he has relocated to Los Angeles and uses it as “a more meditative place” to “think about the big picture shit,” suggesting a strategic rather than operational day-to-day role. He also mentions still sending out packages personally (“It’s hard to do a big mail out as it’s on a much bigger scale”). loudandquiet.com/interview/chris-lombardi
  • Gerard Cosloy is listed as “label co-owner” (LinkedIn). Press routinely describes him as the A&R voice; he has said he signs bands he personally likes. No formal operational title found in any source. linkedin.com/in/gerard-cosloy-8b38481a2

The three also co-own True Panther Sounds in partnership with Dean Bein. matadorrecords.com/pages/info

A&R Signing Attribution: Lombardi vs. Cosloy

No public source documents a formal division of A&R duties between Lombardi and Cosloy. Both have consistently described Matador’s signings as reflecting the collective taste of the label’s owners rather than individual department assignments. Cosloy stated in an interview at vacant.org.uk: “I co-own and manage Matador with the label’s founder Chris Lombardi,” and described their shared hands-off philosophy without delineating personal signing credits.

The clearest attribution signal available from sourced accounts:

Pre-Cosloy (Lombardi solo, 1989–early 1990):

  • H.P. Zinker — OLE 001. Lombardi attended a show that Cosloy put on (before Cosloy joined). Per search synthesis of the Hypebot 2017 interview, Cosloy recommended Lombardi go see them; Lombardi then put them in the studio. Source: hypebot.com (2017); also referenced via Wikipedia’s Gerard Cosloy article.
  • Dustdevils — early Matador release, Lombardi era.
  • Railroad Jerk — early Matador release, Lombardi era.
  • Superchunk — arrived around the time Cosloy joined; Wikipedia credits these four (H.P. Zinker, Dustdevils, Railroad Jerk, Superchunk) to Lombardi’s pre-Cosloy period.

Cosloy-driven signing with documented sourcing:

  • Teenage Fanclub — A Catholic Education (1990, OLE 012). Gerard Cosloy received the cassette directly from Stephen McRobbie of the Pastels. Cosloy had considered releasing it on Homestead but was leaving that label; the tape became a shared favourite and was released through the new Matador partnership. Cosloy is named as the person who received and championed the tape. Source: Cosloy quoted at rockandrollglobe.com, critical-mass-a-catholic-education-at-30 (accessed 2026-07-27). This signing is also described at search-result level as Matador’s “first taste of crazed media attention & big sales.”

  • Pavement — Cosloy first encountered Pavement when Scott Kannberg sent his fanzine Conflict the band’s first studio recording, Slay Tracks. Wikipedia (Gerard Cosloy article, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Cosloy, as of 2026-07-27) attributes the initial discovery to this fanzine connection. Subsequent accounts describe “Cosloy and Lombardi” hearing the demo together before signing. No single-person A&R credit is explicitly asserted in any source, but the fanzine origin points to Cosloy as the entry point.

  • Liz Phair — The discovery path ran through the Girly-Sound tapes being circulated. Dedicated web research (2026-10-13) found the following:

    “Tom Brokaw” — not a participant. No web source connects Tom Brokaw (the NBC newscaster) to Liz Phair’s signing in any way. The name “Chris Brokaw” (guitarist of Come and Codeine) is the person referenced in tape-circulation accounts; prior research may have garbled this name.

    What the Wikipedia Girly-Sound article actually says (checked 2026-10-13): Copies of the Girly-Sound tapes were “initially given by Phair to only two people in 1991: friends and fellow musicians Chris Brokaw and Tae Won Yu.” The article does NOT state that Brokaw passed tapes to Cosloy. For the signing itself the article says only: “In 1992, Phair signed a deal with Matador Records on the strength of a demo tape she had sent them of six Girly-Sound songs.” Cosloy is not named in the Girly-Sound Wikipedia article. en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girly-Sound (as of 2026-10-13)

    What the Newsweek 2018 article says (Cosloy quoted directly): Cosloy describes learning of Phair through a Chemical Imbalance fanzine review of a Girly-Sound cassette written by Tae Won Yu. He says: “I get a lot of silly, audacious calls. But the day before, I’d read a review of a Girly-Sound cassette in Chemical Imbalance.” Phair then called Matador herself and Cosloy agreed to hear more. No mention of Chris Brokaw as a go-between in this account. Source: newsweek.com/2018/06/08/liz-phair-girly-sound-guyville-tapes-matador-908739 (accessed 2026-10-13)

    SPIN 2013 oral history (Girly Show: The Oral History of Liz Phair’s ‘Exile In Guyville’): Brad Wood is quoted saying a few days after Matador heard a FedEx’d cassette of their recordings, “they faxed me a two-page contract.” Cosloy is quoted as saying: “We usually don’t sign people we haven’t met, or heard other records by, or seen as performers. But I had a hunch, and I called her back and said okay.” No Brokaw-as-intermediary in the accessed excerpts. Source: spin.com/2013/06/liz-phair-exile-in-guyville-oral-history-best-1993/2/ (page partially inaccessible — JS-only render; cited at search-result level)

    “Brokaw gave tapes to Cosloy” — origin of the claim: AI search summaries of the Wikipedia Girly-Sound and Liz Phair articles assert this chain, but the underlying Wikipedia article text does not say it. The claim may originate in the 2018 25th Anniversary boxset oral history (Jason Cohen, for Matador), which directly interviews both Chris Brokaw and Gerard Cosloy (confirmed by New Noise Magazine’s boxset announcement at newnoisemagazine.com/matador-records-announce-liz-phair-girly-sound-guyville-25th-anniversary/). That oral history’s full text exists only in the physical book accompanying the boxset and has not been reproduced in any fetchable web page as of 2026-10-13. The Rock and Roll Globe 30th anniversary piece (2023) says only that “eventually, songs from them wound up in the hands of Gerald Cosloy at Matador” — no named intermediary. Source: rockandrollglobe.com/rock/never-said-liz-phairs-exile-in-guyville-at-30/ (accessed 2026-10-13)

    Summary: The Brokaw-to-Cosloy chain is plausible (Brokaw received early tapes; the boxset oral history interviews both and likely documents this), but no accessible primary interview text confirms it. The Cosloy accounts available online (Newsweek 2018, SPIN 2013) describe the route as: Tae Won Yu fanzine review → Phair called Cosloy directly. These accounts are not necessarily contradictory — tapes could have circulated via multiple routes — but the specific claim that Brokaw handed tapes to Cosloy has not been confirmed in any primary-source text available on the open web.

No individual attribution found:

  • Yo La Tengo, Cat Power, Mogwai — multiple searches and page fetches of all available Matador histories, Spotify for Artists label spotlight, Las Vegas Sun 2010 Cosloy interview, vacant.org.uk interview, and Wikipedia articles on the individual artists found no source naming either Lombardi or Cosloy specifically as the A&R contact for these acts. All sources describe collective decision-making. Cat Power signed 1996; both post-Patrick Amory joining (1994).

Interpol (2002 signing) — partial attribution found:

Multiple retrospective sources covering the making of Turn On the Bright Lights provide the following picture:

  • Daniel Kessler had maintained contact with both Cosloy and Lombardi while working for Domino Recording Company, repeatedly sending them new Interpol recordings. Matador had already rejected the band twice. Stereogum, 2022
  • Lombardi is cited as “converted first”: “Lombardi bought in first, converted by a brief glimpse at the live show and a transcendent drive through the mountains of France with Interpol’s recently recorded Peel Session.” Stereogum, 2022
  • Drummer Sam Fogarino (in Rolling Stone) on Lombardi: “Word is Lombardi was listening [to the songs] while driving through the Alps in his BMW, going really, really fast. And he went, ‘Yeah, this will work.‘” Source cited as Rolling Stone “Princes of Darkness” interview; musicianguide.com relays the attribution.
  • Cosloy made the formal outreach: after the third-demo/Peel Session, “Matador head Gerard Cosloy emailed Kessler, saying he and his partner, Chris Lombardi, wanted to meet the band.” Multiple search-result summaries citing contemporaneous accounts; appears to originate in the Pitchfork 2012 oral history or Paste 2012 oral history (see below).
  • At the signing meeting: “They were all wearing suits and were considerably better dressed than we were. Their shoes were shined. It was like meeting with a bunch of young lawyers. They were fans of the label, had great taste and a very clear idea of what they wanted to do after recording.” — attributed to Chris Lombardi; source appears to be the Pitchfork 2012 oral history (see below). musicianguide.com relays this quote.

Primary sources not yet directly confirmed (paywalled/403 on fetch):

  • Pitchfork 2012 oral history of Turn On the Bright Lights (features Paul Banks, Carlos Dengler, Sam Fogarino, Daniel Kessler, and Chris Lombardi). Announced at news.matadorrecords.com (2012-11-29). Direct fetch returned 403; Pitchfork URL structure unclear. This is the most likely primary source for the Lombardi meeting quote.
  • Paste Magazine 2012 oral history (features Gerard Cosloy but not Carlos D). URL: https://www.pastemagazine.com/music/interpols-turn-on-the-bright-lights-at-10-an-oral (403 on fetch).
  • Magnet Magazine 2017: “MAGNET Classics: The Making Of Interpol’s ‘Turn On The Bright Lights’” — https://magnetmagazine.com/2017/05/29/magnet-classics-the-making-of-interpols-turn-on-the-bright-lights/ (429 on fetch).
  • Rolling Stone “Princes of Darkness” (date not confirmed; paywalled via tollbit redirect).

Summary: Both Lombardi and Cosloy were involved in the Interpol signing. Lombardi is the named person who was “converted first” by hearing the Peel Session; Cosloy made the formal contact email. Patrick Amory is not named in any source as involved in this signing. The Lombardi meeting quote appears to be primary-sourced in the Pitchfork 2012 oral history, which directly interviews him alongside the band.

The label’s stated philosophy: Lombardi told Loud and Quiet: “We have never signed a band for commercial reasons.” Cosloy told the Las Vegas Sun (2010): “We try to concentrate on what we like, as opposed to what we think the rest of the world is gonna like.” Both consistently frame signing decisions as a collective reflection of the owners’ taste rather than an individual A&R function. Sources: loudandquiet.com/interview/chris-lombardi; lasvegassun.com/news/2010/sep/30/matadors-manifesto/ (accessed 2026-07-27).
