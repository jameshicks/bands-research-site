Olu Dara

Aug 03, 20269 min read

Olu Dara

Cornetist, trumpeter, guitarist, and vocalist. Born Charles Jones III, January 12, 1941, in Natchez (some sources say Louisville), Mississippi. Moved to New York City in 1963. Discogs artist ID: 174747. Father of rapper Nas.

Also documented under name variations: Dara, O. Dara, Odra, OluDara, Olu Dara aka Charles Jones III.

Biography

Mississippi Origins and New York Arrival (pre-1970s)

Charles Jones III grew up in Natchez, Mississippi, absorbing the blues and gospel traditions of the Deep South before relocating to New York City in 1963. He adopted the name Olu Dara — drawn from Yoruba culture — as part of a broader engagement with African musical traditions that would shape his entire career. In New York he plugged into the Lower East Side and loft jazz scenes that were incubating much of the era’s most radical improvised music.

Loft Jazz and Downtown Avant-Garde (mid-1970s–mid-1980s)

Dara became one of the most recognizable voices in the New York free jazz scene during the mid-1970s and through the 1980s. His cornet and trumpet work appeared on foundational records for India Navigation, Black Saint, and Hat Hut Records, alongside some of the most significant players of the era.

His 1976 appearances stand as defining early credits: trumpet on David Murray’s Flowers For Albert (India Navigation, with Phillip Wilson on drums and Fred Hopkins on bass), and on Hamiet Bluiett’s Endangered Species (India Navigation, same year, same rhythm section). The two records document a tightly knit circle of musicians building what would become the loft jazz canon.

Through the late 1970s he expanded his reach: Esoteric (1979, Hat Hut Records) with Phillip Wilson’s quartet, where he played both serpent and trumpet — a rare credit that speaks to his interest in historical and non-Western brass instruments. He appeared on James Blood Ulmer’s Are You Glad To Be In America? (Rough Trade, 1980) alongside David Murray, Oliver Lake, and Ronald Shannon Jackson.

The early 1980s saw Dara as a central figure in David Murray’s expanding ensembles. He played trumpet on the David Murray Octet’s Ming (Black Saint, 1980) — a landmark session alongside Henry Threadgill, George Lewis, Butch Morris, Anthony Davis, and Steve McCall — and cornet on the David Murray Big Band’s Live At “Sweet Basil” Vol. 1 (Black Saint, 1985), with Butch Morris conducting. His cornet also appears on Murray’s Home (Black Saint, 1982) and the two Sweet Basil live volumes.

He became a regular member of Bill Laswell’s Material orbit. His trumpet is on Memory Serves (Celluloid, 1981) — a session that also brought together Henry Threadgill, Sonny Sharrock, Fred Frith, George Lewis, Billy Bang, Fred Maher, Michael Beinhorn, and Martin Bisi. He returned for Material’s The Third Power (Axiom, 1991) and Reality (Axiom, 1993), both Laswell productions with enormous rosters including Bootsy Collins, Bernie Worrell, Henry Threadgill, Herbie Hancock, and Nicky Skopelitis.

Dara’s Africa-facing interests converged sharply with Laswell’s through the Deadline project. Makosaa Rock (Celluloid, 1985) features Dara on cornet and “wood trumpet” alongside Jaco Pastorius (bass), Manu Dibango (saxophone), Steve Turre (didgeridoo), Paul Butterfield (harmonica), and Bernie Worrell (synthesizer) — Laswell and Phillip Wilson co-producing. The same year he appeared on Manu Dibango’s Makosaa Rock (Celluloid) single and on Henry Threadgill’s Henry Threadgill Sextett records.

Other significant mid-decade credits include: Jamaaladeen Tacuma’s Show Stopper (Gramavision, 1983), cornet on track 6 alongside Julius Hemphill; the Shango project Zulu Groove (Celluloid, 1983) produced by Afrika Bambaataa and Material; Cecil McBee’s Flying Out (India Navigation, 1982) with Billy Hart; and Conjure’s Cab Calloway Stands In For The Moon (American Clavé, 1988), Kip Hanrahan’s literary-jazz ensemble setting the poetry of Ishmael Reed, where Dara played trumpet, vocals, and harmonica alongside David Murray, Don Pullen, Hamiet Bluiett, and Bobby Womack.

He appears on the Easily Slip Into Another World album (Novus/RCA, 1988), credited as Composed By — indicating he contributed original compositions to that project.

African Diaspora and Broader Contexts (late 1980s–1990s)

Dara continued as a working musician across African-influenced and world jazz contexts. He played cornet on the Kansas City film soundtrack (Verve, 1996) — Robert Altman’s period jazz film — and on its companion KC After Dark (Verve, 1997). He appeared as a guest cornetist on Jean-Marc Padovani’s Trance Atlantic (Boom Bop II) (Double Moon, 2001) and on various other European projects.

His son Nas credited him with trumpet on the 1994 Illmatic (Columbia) — a direct lineage connection between the two worlds Dara inhabited. Nas later formalized this on “Bridging The Gap” from Street’s Disciple (Columbia, 2004), where Dara played lead guitar, trumpet, harmonica, and sang — an explicit blues-to-hip-hop generational statement that he co-wrote.

Solo Career and Return to Blues Roots (1998–)

In 1998 Dara released his debut solo album In The World — From Natchez To New York (Atlantic), a major artistic statement that repositioned him as a blues and African roots artist rather than strictly a jazz sideman. The record draws on Delta blues, New Orleans second-line, West African rhythms, and jazz improvisation, and received widespread critical attention. A follow-up, Neighborhoods (Atlantic, 2001), continued in the same direction.

Both albums reflect his stated belief that the blues and African traditional music are not separate traditions, and that his career from the loft jazz era through the blues revival was a continuous arc rooted in Mississippi.
