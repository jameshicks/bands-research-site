Peter Jenner

Peter Jenner

Oct 29, 202610 min read

Peter Jenner

Discogs ID: 260403 Active: 1960s–present Origin: UK

British music manager and producer. One of the original producers on AMM’s 1966 recordings (AMMMusic 1966, ReR Megacorp, RēR AMMCD, 1989). Better known as manager/producer for Pink Floyd (early era), Billy Bragg, and The Clash.

AMM Involvement (1966)

DNA Productions

In spring 1966 Jenner co-founded a small production company called DNA Productions with associates from the London underground scene, including photographer and activist John “Hoppy” Hopkins, jazz critic Ronald Atkins, and cultural critic Alan Beckett (see note below on Beckett’s identity). DNA set up a studio in a former dairy at 46a Old Church Street, Chelsea, and secured funding from Elektra Records. (source: Barry Miles, “Early Days of the London Underground Scene”, lux.org.uk)

Alan Beckett — likely identity. The Alan Beckett credited as DNA Productions co-producer is almost certainly the same person as Alan Beckett, the British music and cultural critic who published 17 articles in New Left Review between 1964 and 1975. His NLR work ran an extended series on jazz and avant-garde music (Cecil Taylor, Ornette Coleman, John Coltrane, Eric Dolphy, Thelonious Monk, free jazz — NLR I/23 through I/36, 1964–1966), then pivoted to pop (NLR I/39, September–October 1966, engaging Adorno’s critique of popular music; NLR I/47, 1968, “Stones”, the first serious critical account of the Rolling Stones; NLR I/54, 1969, “Mapping Pop”). The chronology fits: his NLR jazz series was running precisely in 1964–1966 when DNA Productions was formed, and his interests — free jazz, avant-garde improvisation, pop criticism from a Marxist-inflected perspective — align closely with what AMM represented. His NLR article “ESP Music” (I/42, March–April 1967) reviews ESP-Disk free jazz releases, showing continued engagement with collective improvisation at exactly the moment the AMM album was being produced. No source found to date explicitly names the NLR Beckett as the DNA Productions Beckett, but the overlap of period, location (London), and musical focus is strong circumstantial evidence. (source: NLR author index, newleftreview.org/search?query%5Bauthor%5D=Alan+Beckett)

No other production or music-industry credits for Alan Beckett beyond the AMM/DNA Productions co-production have been found in web sources. His NLR contributions ceased after 1975 and no further career information is publicly documented online.

Joe Boyd’s role — conflict between sources, updated. Whether Joe Boyd was a formal member of DNA Productions or only the Elektra Records facilitator has now been substantially clarified by a 2026 primary-participant interview, though one secondary source still pulls the other way.

Evidence pointing to Boyd as outside facilitator (weight of evidence):

  • Eddie Prévost (primary participant, psychedelicbabymag.com, May 2026) names DNA’s four members as “Pete Jenner, John ‘Hoppy’ Hopkins, Ron Atkins and one other, I think Alan Bates” — Boyd is not in this list. Prévost adds: “Along the way, I think Joe Boyd also got involved.” The hesitant phrasing (“I think,” “along the way”) implies Boyd’s participation was secondary and consultative, not foundational. (source: psychedelicbabymag.com)
  • 500songs.com analysis (Episode 157, “See Emily Play”) likewise frames Boyd as Elektra UK head who was “another London Free School member” and secured the distribution agreement for DNA’s release — i.e. he facilitated the deal from the Elektra side, while DNA itself was the four-person collective of Jenner, Hopkins, Atkins, and a fourth member. (source: 500songs.com)
  • Discogs release credits (r128786, the ReR CD reissue) list producers as Jenner, Hopkins, Atkins, and Beckett — Boyd absent. (source: discogs.com/release/128786)
  • Boyd’s own memoir (White Bicycles, 2006) recounts the AMM record and describes Jenner giving it to Syd Barrett, but no available excerpt or review contains Boyd identifying himself as a DNA member. His production company was the later Witchseason Productions. Boyd also describes his Elektra role as a job he “neglected” and eventually lost — consistent with him acting as a label-side contact rather than an equity member of an independent production entity. (source: npr.org/2007/03/20/9007942; reynoldsretro.blogspot.com interview)
  • Boyd’s own self-description in interviews characterises his broader role in the 1966 London underground as an “eminence grise” — a behind-the-scenes catalyst — which fits a facilitation role rather than formal co-founding. (source: reynoldsretro.blogspot.com)

Evidence pointing the other way (one source):

  • Barry Miles (lux.org.uk) names Boyd explicitly as a co-member: “Jenner, Hoppy and Joe Boyd, the American record producer and concert promoter, and several others, had a production company called DNA and had already released an AMM record with Elektra, that was hardly a commercial success.” (source: lux.org.uk)

Resolution (current best assessment). The Prévost account is the only testimony from inside the recording project; it does not include Boyd in DNA’s membership list and describes his involvement with hedged, retrospective language. The 500songs analysis, Discogs production credits, and Boyd’s own memoir all support the same conclusion: Boyd was the Elektra UK contact who brokered the label funding and distribution for DNA’s project — close enough that Miles characterised him as part of the group in a colloquial retrospective, but not a formal co-member of DNA Productions itself. The formal production entity consisted of four people (Jenner, Hopkins, Atkins, and a fourth — probably Beckett per Discogs, though Prévost recalls “Alan Bates”). The primary source needed for a final resolution is the relevant chapter of White Bicycles itself, which is not available in full online.

DNA’s ambition was to run a forward-looking independent label. AMM — then an emerging free-improvisation collective built around Keith Rowe, Lou Gare, and Eddie Prévost — were among their first signings. (source: lux.org.uk, Barry Miles)

Recording sessions

AMM recorded AMMMusic on 8 and 27 June 1966 at Sound Techniques, Chelsea. The five-piece lineup for the sessions was Rowe, Gare, Prévost, Lawrence Sheaff, and radical composer Cornelius Cardew. Jenner, Hopkins, Atkins, and Beckett are all credited as producers on the release. (source: Discogs release credits, discogs.com/release/128786)

The first session ran into problems: studio engineers misunderstood AMM’s aesthetic (one attempted to modify Prévost’s bass drum by removing the front head and stuffing it with cushioning). The session was abandoned in “confused disarray.” The DNA producers then contacted Elektra’s Jac Holzman directly; after this intervention AMM returned to the studio with all parties receptive to their unorthodox approach. As Prévost recalled: “it was an AMM session like our usual weekly explorations. Nothing was prepared beforehand.” (source: Eddie Prévost interview, psychedelicbabymag.com, 2026)

The album was released by Elektra UK in 1967 (cat. EUK-256). It sold poorly and AMM were quickly dropped.

Why Jenner, and the Pink Floyd / AMM overlap

Jenner’s route to AMM ran through the same tight-knit London underground network that also produced Pink Floyd’s early scene:

  • Jenner was an LSE economics lecturer who drifted into the underground in 1966 and was already active in the London Free School, the Notting Hill community project co-founded by Hopkins. The LFS was a meeting point for avant-garde musicians, counterculture activists, and emerging rock acts. (source: London Free School Wikipedia, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Free_School)
  • Jenner first saw Pink Floyd in June 1966 at the Spontaneous Underground, signing them to Blackhill Enterprises (with Andrew King) in October 1966 — several months after the AMM sessions in June. The DNA/AMM work therefore preceded or ran in parallel with the formal Pink Floyd management deal, not after it. (source: Pink Floyd Wikipedia, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_Floyd)
  • AMM and Pink Floyd shared bills at the same venues. All Saints Church Hall (All Saints Hall, Notting Hill) LFS benefit concerts in autumn 1966 featured both bands on the same poster, alongside Ron Geesin and others. The two groups also co-billed at the UFO Club. (source: London Free School Wikipedia; pinkfloydarchives.com)
  • AMM guitarist Keith Rowe’s tabletop/prepared guitar techniques reportedly influenced Syd Barrett’s early playing. (source: reddybrownobjects.wordpress.com)
  • The AMM title “Later During a Flaming Riviera Sunset” (Side A of AMMmusic) is cited as the origin of the word “Flaming” in Pink Floyd’s “Astronomy Domine” / “Flaming” titles. (source: matchlessrecordings.com/review/ammmusic)

In short: Jenner’s AMM role was not incidental to his Pink Floyd work — both arose from the same London underground milieu, organised through the same network of people (Hopkins, Boyd, the LFS), at virtually the same moment in 1966.

Specific role

Jenner’s credit is producer (shared with Hopkins, Atkins, Beckett / collectively DNA). There is no evidence that he was the engineer (Sound Techniques had its own staff engineers) or a financier acting alone — the Elektra funding was brokered collectively via Joe Boyd. His role appears to have been that of a co-organiser and advocate: arranging the Elektra deal, liaising with the label when the first session collapsed, and shepherding the project to completion alongside the other DNA principals.
