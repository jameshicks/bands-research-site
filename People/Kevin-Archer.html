Kevin Archer

British guitarist and songwriter. Also known as Al Archer. Discogs artist ID: 383861.

Overview

Co-founder of Dexys Midnight Runners alongside Kevin Rowland; both had previously been in punk band The Killjoys. Archer served as co-leader and guitarist in Dexys’ first era (1978–1981) and was present for the recording and release of Searching For The Young Soul Rebels (July 1980), departing the band in early 1981 during the touring period that followed. After leaving Dexys he formed The Blue Ox Babes.

Connections

  • Dexys Midnight Runners — co-founder, guitar (Era 1)
  • The Killjoys — punk band with Kevin Rowland
  • The Blue Ox Babes — post-Dexys project

Departure from Dexys (early 1981)

Archer co-founded Dexys with Kevin Rowland in 1978 and was a central creative force throughout the debut album. He is credited on Searching For The Young Soul Rebels (July 1980) as guitarist, vocalist, and liner-notes writer, and co-wrote tracks including “Geno” and “Love Part One.” [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searching_for_the_Young_Soul_Rebels, as of 2025-07-14]

Note on the brief’s framing: the task brief states Archer departed “before the debut album was released,” but all available sources indicate he was present through the recording and release of the album (July 1980) and departed in early 1981 during the subsequent touring period. This is a factual discrepancy to flag.

Circumstances

After the debut album’s release, Dexys suffered internal conflict when the third single “Keep It Part Two (Inferiority Part One)” underperformed commercially. Five band members quit simultaneously, citing ongoing personality clashes with Rowland and his policy of refusing to speak to the music press; those departing members went on to form The Bureau. Archer initially remained with Rowland but ultimately departed in early 1981 as well, leaving only Rowland and trombonist “Big” Jim Paterson from the original lineup. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexys_Midnight_Runners, as of 2025-07-14]

Rowland’s management style was a central driver. He had already imposed the stage name “Al” on Archer (objecting to two band members sharing the name Kevin) and exercised extensive personal control over members’ appearances and conduct. Archer later described the culture of the band under Rowland as something he did not enjoy, while saying he valued the musical side. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Archer, as of 2025-07-14; 2009 BBC Radio WM interview cited therein]

The Record Collector article “A Tale of Two Kevins” characterises the atmosphere Archer endured as “claustrophobic” under Rowland’s dominant personality, and describes Archer as having reached “the end of his tether.” [source: https://recordcollectormag.com/articles/a-tale-of-two-kevins — article text not directly accessible; summary drawn from search-engine excerpts only; treat as secondary paraphrase]

The Blue Ox Babes and the Too-Rye-Ay controversy

After leaving Dexys, Archer formed The Blue Ox Babes in early 1981 with his girlfriend Yasmin Saleh, guitarist Nick Bache, former Dexys keyboard player Andy Leek, and fiddle player Helen Bevington (later renamed Helen O’Hara by Rowland). The Blue Ox Babes pursued a blend of soul and Celtic folk that Archer had been developing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Ox_Babes, as of 2025-07-14]

Archer shared Blue Ox Babes demos with Rowland. Rowland adopted the style — combining a Tamla-style beat with strings, and the technique of speeding up and slowing down — and recruited Bevington/O’Hara for Dexys’ second album Too-Rye-Ay (1982), on which “Come On Eileen” became a global hit. This effectively ended The Blue Ox Babes’ commercial prospects, as they were subsequently dismissed as imitators of Dexys. [source: https://nostalgiacentral.com/music/artists-a-to-k/artists-b/blue-ox-babes/; https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/come-on-eileen-dexy-midnight-runners-song/]

Rowland’s statements on the debt to Archer

Rowland’s public position on this shifted over time:

  • c. 1993: Rowland stated publicly: “After Searching For The Young Soul Rebels, when [Archer] left, we were both experimenting with strings. I wasn’t getting what I wanted: he found it and I stole it.” He also said: “Dexys had taken his sound and succeeded with it,” and reportedly paid Archer a share of Too-Rye-Ay royalties for a period. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexys_Midnight_Runners, as of 2025-07-14; search-engine excerpts of Record Collector article]

  • 2014, The Guardian: Rowland revisited and partially walked back the earlier statement: “Around 19 years ago I gave an interview about how I’d stolen ‘Come On Eileen’ from Kevin Archer. Some of it was true. Most of it was me punishing myself. I was in a dark place and thought it had all been him and I had no talent.” He clarified: “What actually happened was … he played me his demos and he was using a combination of a Tamla-style beat with violins, which I thought sounded better than what we were doing. So I nicked that style, and the idea of speeding up and slowing down. I didn’t steal one note, one chord, one melody.” [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_On_Eileen, citing The Guardian 2014, as of 2025-07-14]
