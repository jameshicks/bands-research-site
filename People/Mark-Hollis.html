Mark Hollis

Vocalist, songwriter, multi-instrumentalist. Born Mark David Hollis, 4 January 1955, Tottenham, London. Died 25 February 2019. Discogs ID: 47956.

Primary creative force behind Talk Talk. His brother Ed Hollis co-wrote the debut single “Talk Talk.” Hollis began the band in synth-pop mode and drove it progressively further from commercial music across five albums; after Talk Talk dissolved he released one solo album (1998) and essentially retired from music.

Biography

Hollis had an early credit as vocalist and guitarist on an Island Records session in 1978 (“I Can’t Resist,” release ID 1557319, with The Reaction). Talk Talk formed in 1981; by the second album he had established Tim Friese-Greene as co-writer and producer, and the two became the primary songwriting partnership for the rest of the band’s life. All songs on The Colour of Spring (1986), Spirit of Eden (1988), and Laughing Stock (1991) carry Hollis / Friese-Greene writing credits.

On the later albums Hollis played piano, organ, guitar, and various other instruments in addition to singing; he co-produced Spirit of Eden and Laughing Stock alongside Friese-Greene.

After Talk Talk dissolved, Hollis produced a session for an act called Smiling & Waving (Virgin, 2001 — uncredited producer credit). His solo album appeared in 1998.

Mark Hollis (1998)

Self-titled solo album. Polydor, cat. 537 688-2. Eight tracks. Produced by Hollis. Continuing the sparse, acoustic-oriented direction of Laughing Stock — chamber instruments, no drums on most tracks, extreme quietude.

Smiling & Waving detail

Smiling & Waving (Virgin, 2001) is the debut album by Norwegian vocalist Anja Garbarek. Hollis produced tracks 2 (“The Gown”) and 7 (“The Diver”) without public credit. The remaining tracks were co-produced by Garbarek and Steven Wilson. Robert Wyatt sings uncredited on track 7 alongside Hollis’s production. This is Hollis’s only documented production work outside Talk Talk and his own solo album.

Alias

Discogs records one alias name: John Cope (artist ID 612730). The John Cope entry has two releases under that name — A V 1 (1998, Resurgence, credited as “Musician”) and American Folk Horror (2001, Estrus Records, credited with photography/design). These do not appear connected to Mark Hollis musically and are almost certainly a different person sharing the name. The alias link in the Discogs entry appears to be an error or tagging artifact.

Biography — Confirmed Details

  • Born 4 January 1955, Tottenham, London. Attended Tollington School, a grammar school in Muswell Hill.
  • Studied child psychology at Sussex University; quit (date reported variously as 1975 or 1977) and returned to London to concentrate on songwriting. Met his future wife Felicity (“Flick”) Costello at Sussex.
  • Brother Ed Hollis managed punk band Eddie and the Hot Rods and introduced Mark to a broad musical diet including John Coltrane, Miles Davis, Béla Bartók, and Claude Debussy.
  • In 1977, The Reaction recorded a demo for Island Records that included an early Hollis song titled “Talk Talk,” which later appeared on the Beggars Banquet punk compilation Streets. The band disbanded after the 1978 single.
  • Talk Talk formed in 1981 with Paul Webb, Lee Harris, and Simon Brenner — described as acquaintances of his brother.
  • Later life: lived in Wimbledon with Flick and two sons; in 2017 moved to Heathfield, East Sussex, to a house known as “the Walled Garden.” Occasional golf and motorcycling.

Gap Years: 1991–1998

Hollis made no public statements about a formal retirement after Laughing Stock. The 1998 solo album appears to have fulfilled a contractual obligation to Polydor (he had negotiated a £2 million advance for a two-album contract with complete artistic freedom). He did give a small number of interviews around the solo album’s January 1998 release — among them a Music Minded interview and an NME interview — but these were rare. Selected quotes from around that period:

  • “I wanted to make a record where you can’t hear when it has been made.” (Music Minded)
  • “We only used two microphones. Recording in its purest form, really, like in the old days.” (Music Minded, on recording technique)
  • On “A Life (1895–1915)”: discussed fascination with “the very severe mood swings” between pre-war patriotism and post-war disillusionment. (NME)
  • Stated emphatically there would be no concerts: “this material isn’t suited to play live.”
  • On the decision to stop: “I choose for my family. Maybe others are capable of doing it, but I can’t go on tour and be a good dad at the same time.”
  • “All that matters are my records. I can’t live up to them, I can’t be as succinct and clear as they are.” (variant also reported as: “All that counts is my records. I can’t be at their level, I can’t be as brief and clear as they are. I can only harm them. If I had the choice, I would never speak to the Press.“)
  • “I have enough money to live on, which is great.” — characterised his post-1998 life as like “a student whose grant allows him to spend his time creatively.”

After 1998 he declined all further interviews and released nothing.

Anja Garbarek / Smiling & Waving — Production Story

Source: Innerviews.org interview with Anja Garbarek.

While in London during pre-production for her debut (c. 1998–99), Garbarek heard Hollis’s 1998 solo album in a record store and recognised its spirit as what she was aiming for. She contacted her label to enquire about working with him; they told her he had retired from the music industry. The label nevertheless arranged a meeting, they got on well, and he agreed to produce two tracks. Hollis produced “The Gown” (track 2) and “The Diver” (track 7). It was Hollis’s idea to have Robert Wyatt as a duet partner on “The Diver.” After those two tracks were completed, Garbarek needed a producer for the rest of the album; BMG organised meetings with candidates and she chose Steven Wilson. Garbarek’s comment after Hollis’s death: “He was such a fantastically-talented person. I feel extremely privileged that he said yes to my album and that I got to know him for a little while.”

Solo Album (1998) — Critical Reception

  • UK chart peak: No. 53, one week in the Top 75 (modest commercial performance).
  • NME: four stars.
  • The Guardian: four stars.
  • Melody Maker: “Open, restful and at times fantastically beautiful… a sound whose ambience is as natural as breathing.”
  • Cited influences: Miles Davis / Gil Evans’s Sketches of Spain and Porgy and Bess; minimalist composers.

Death

Mark Hollis died 25 February 2019, aged 64. His manager Keith Aspden confirmed death was due to “a short illness from which he never recovered.” The New Statesman profile (2022) states the cause was cancer, though this was not part of the official statement at the time. No further details were made public.

Legacy & Influence

Spirit of Eden (1988) and Laughing Stock (1991) are identified by critics as foundational to post-rock. The term “post-rock” was coined by Simon Reynolds in a March 1994 Mojo review of Bark Psychosis’s Hex, which he described as following in the footsteps of Spirit of Eden. AllMusic stated that “the musical foundation for post-rock crystallized in 1991, with the release of two very different landmarks: Talk Talk’s Laughing Stock and Slint’s Spiderland.”

Named artists with documented connections to Talk Talk’s influence:

  • Bark Psychosis — cited Talk Talk among early influences; Graham Sutton later worked extensively with Lee Harris and Paul Webb in .O.Rang. Reynolds’s coinage of “post-rock” was explicitly in the context of Talk Talk’s ambient experiments.
  • Elbow — Guy Garvey credited Talk Talk with shaping Elbow’s approach to volume dynamics: “Volume dynamics are an essential part of classical music, but a lost art with guitar music.”
  • Graham Coxon — cited Spirit of Eden as an influence (per Wikipedia’s Spirit of Eden article).
  • Doves — cited (per Wikipedia’s Spirit of Eden article); no specific quote documented.
  • Bedhead — cited (per Wikipedia’s Spirit of Eden article); no specific quote documented.
  • Sigur Rós, Godspeed You! Black Emperor, Low, latter-period Radiohead — grouped by critics (Andy Whitman, Paste) as heirs to the Talk Talk template; no direct quotes from these acts documented in sources consulted.
  • Spiritualized — linked by critics (Mark Lager, PopMatters) to Talk Talk’s influence alongside Bark Psychosis.
  • Tortoise — Simon Harper (Birmingham Post) argued they “would hardly sound the same” without Hollis and Friese-Greene.
  • Jenny Hval — stated Laughing Stock is “an incredibly intuitive and bare recording” and described listening to it after the 2011 Norway attacks.
  • King Creosote — called Laughing Stock “the wayward, grubby wee brother” of Spirit of Eden.
  • Wild Beasts (Tom Fleming) — praised Laughing Stock for its “kind of a dead end” minimalism and the album’s achievement in reduction.

Note: Mogwai’s documented influences (Wikipedia) do not include Talk Talk. Kid A’s documented influences do not include Talk Talk.

Missing Pieces (Pond Life, 2001) — Track Listing & Session Origins

Released 16 February 2001 on Pond Life. Tracks 1–6 are the A- and B-sides of the three CD singles released in 1991 to support Laughing Stock — all from the Laughing Stock sessions. Track 7 is a 1998 recording made under Hollis’s pseudonym John Cope (see Alias section) for a Dave Allinson / Phill Brown art exhibition; it appears twice consecutively on the CD, designed to cycle indefinitely.

Open Questions

  • John Cope alias — RESOLVED. Discogs ID 612730 is confirmed as a Hollis pseudonym. The Discogs entry states: “Pseudonym for former Talk Talk frontman Mark Hollis, derived from the B-side to [the 1988 ‘I Believe in You’ single].” The track “Piano” on Missing Pieces is the only known release under this name, recorded 1998 for an art exhibition by Dave Allinson and Phill Brown.
  • Missing Pieces sessions — RESOLVED. All six main tracks are from the Laughing Stock (1991) single sessions. Track 7 is a 1998 recording unconnected to the Talk Talk album sessions.
  • No session or guest credits found for Hollis outside The Reaction, Talk Talk, his solo album, and the Smiling & Waving production work — consistent with his known withdrawal from the industry.
