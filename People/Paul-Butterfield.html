Paul Butterfield

Blues harmonica player and vocalist. Born 17 December 1942, Chicago, Illinois; died 4 May 1987, North Hollywood, California. Discogs artist ID: 273348.

One of the first white musicians to master Chicago blues harmonica at source — playing alongside Muddy Waters and Howlin’ Wolf on the South Side before he was twenty. Leader of The Paul Butterfield Blues Band (1965–1971), whose integrated lineup and electric intensity helped channel Chicago blues into the emerging rock mainstream. Inducted into the Blues Hall of Fame in 2006.

Biography

Chicago Roots (1942–1964)

Butterfield grew up on the South Side of Chicago, began playing classical flute as a child, and switched to harmonica in his teens. He fell in with the Chess Records blues world, sitting in at clubs with Muddy Waters, Howlin’ Wolf, Little Walter (Walter Horton), and Otis Spann. At the University of Chicago he met guitarist Mike Bloomfield, and the two began forming the group that would become the Blues Band. Bassist Jerome Arnold and drummer Sam Lay were recruited from Howlin’ Wolf’s working band — a notable transfer of personnel from the first-generation Chicago blues world into what would become rock.

The Paul Butterfield Blues Band — Elektra Years (1965–1971)

Signed to Elektra Records by Jac Holzman; debut produced by Paul A. Rothchild.

The original lineup — Butterfield (vocals, harmonica), Mike Bloomfield (lead guitar), Elvin Bishop (rhythm guitar), Mark Naftalin (organ), Jerome Arnold (bass), Sam Lay / Billy Davenport (drums) — recorded six studio albums for Elektra and helped introduce integrated Chicago blues to rock audiences. The band’s 1965 Elektra debut and East-West (1966) are canonical documents of the mid-Sixties blues-rock moment; the nearly-thirteen-minute title track of East-West is one of the earliest extended rock improvisations on record, drawing on Indian and modal jazz influences.

Mike Bloomfield left after East-West. Elvin Bishop remained through the horn-augmented later albums. From The Resurrection of Pigboy Crabshaw (1967) onward the band expanded into a larger horn-section format: David Sanborn (alto and soprano sax), Gene Dinwiddie (tenor sax), Keith Johnson (trumpet), Phillip Wilson (drums). In My Own Dream (1968) also featured Al Kooper on organ on two tracks. Keep On Moving (1969) brought in Buzzy Feiten on guitar and Steve Madaio on trumpet. The band’s final Elektra album, Sometimes I Just Feel Like Smilin’ (1971), was the last under the Blues Band name.

Paul Butterfield’s Better Days (1972–1974)

After the Blues Band dissolved, Butterfield formed Paul Butterfield’s Better Days, a Woodstock-scene acoustic/roots outfit based at Bearsville Records. Members: Geoff Muldaur, Amos Garrett, Ronnie Barron, Bill Rich, Chris Parker (2). Co-produced with Geoff Muldaur. Engineer and sometime guitarist: Nick Jameson. Cover design both Better Days albums: Milton Glaser.

The two Better Days albums — Better Days (1973) and It All Comes Back (1973) — drew on country-blues, New Orleans, and Americana influences. Guest vocalists on It All Comes Back include Maria Muldaur and Bobby Charles.

Solo on Bearsville / Columbia (1975–1980)

Butterfield made two solo albums on Bearsville:

  • Put It In Your Ear (1975) — large-ensemble pop-soul record, produced and arranged by Henry Glover with a massive cast including Levon Helm, Garth Hudson, David Sanborn, Bernard Purdie, James Jamerson, Chuck Rainey, Levon Helm, Chris Parker (2). Cover by Milton Glaser.
  • North South (1980) — recorded at Hi Studios, Memphis, produced by Willie Mitchell; Memphis horns (Wayne Jackson, Andrew Love) and the Memphis Strings.

Put It In Your Ear was also reissued on Columbia. A 1980 Bearsville release Footprints On The Windshield Upside Down (live bootleg-style recording) also appeared in this period.

Guest Work and Final Years (1977–1987)

From the late 1970s Butterfield worked primarily as a guest and session player:

  • Levon Helm and the RCO All-Stars (1977, ABC Records) — harmonica and additional vocals alongside Levon Helm, members of The Band, and others.
  • The Last Waltz (1978, Warner Bros.) — performer at The Band’s farewell concert.
  • Frequent late-period touring partner Rick Danko; a series of live recordings document the Danko-Butterfield partnership circa 1978–79.
  • The Muddy Waters Woodstock Album (1975, Chess) — harmonica.
  • Muddy & The Wolf (1982, Chess) — harp alongside Eric Clapton, Steve Winwood, Charlie Watts.
  • Makosaa Rock (1985, Celluloid) — harmonica on the Deadline EP, produced by Bill Laswell, featuring Phillip Wilson, Manu Dibango, Bernie Worrell, Jaco Pastorius, Olu Dara. One of his last studio credits.
  • The Legendary Paul Butterfield Rides Again (1986, Amherst Records) — late-career studio album with Paul Shaffer, Anton Fig, Blondie Chaplin; produced by Thom Panunzio.

Butterfield died 4 May 1987 of peritonitis, attributed to long-term drug use and heavy drinking.
