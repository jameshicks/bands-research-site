Alex North

American film composer and conductor. Born December 4, 1910 (real name: Isadore Soifer). Died September 8, 1991. Discogs artist ID: 283106. Discogs group: Alex North & His Orchestra (ID: 1508068).

Roles: Composer, Conductor, Producer. Primary labels: Varèse Sarabande, Decca, Capitol Records, 20th Century Fox Records, Warner Bros. Records, MCA Records, United Artists Records.

Biography

Early Life and Formation (1910–1950)

Born Isadore Soifer in Chester, Pennsylvania to Russian immigrant parents. Studied at the Curtis Institute of Music in Philadelphia and the Juilliard School in New York, then traveled to Moscow to study at the Moscow Conservatory in the mid-1930s — a formative experience that deepened his engagement with European classical technique. He also studied with Ernst Toch and Aaron Copland. During the late 1930s and 1940s he worked as a dance accompanist and arranger in New York, collaborating with choreographers including Martha Graham and Anna Sokolow.

His earliest Discogs credit dates to 1947: Little Indian Drum (Young People’s Records), a children’s composition released in a run that would eventually accumulate 87 pressings — a measure of how widely that recording circulated.

Hollywood Breakthrough (1951–1963)

North’s feature debut, the score for A Streetcar Named Desire (1951, Capitol Records), established him as a major voice in Hollywood. Composed By credit on the original LP alongside conductor Ray Heindorf. The score was notable for its use of jazz idiom — blues inflections, solo saxophone — in a dramatic film context, which was then unusual for a major Hollywood production.

A string of high-profile assignments followed:

  • The Rose Tattoo (1955, Columbia) — composed and conducted
  • The Bad Seed (1956, RCA Victor)
  • The Rainmaker (1956–57, RCA Victor) — composed, conducted, and provided the score
  • South Seas Adventure (1958, Audio Fidelity) — early Cinerama work with the Cinerama Orchestra
  • The Long, Hot Summer (1958, Roulette)
  • The Sound and the Fury (1959, Decca)
  • Wonderful Country (1959, United Artists Records)

The Spartacus score (1960, Decca) — for Stanley Kubrick’s epic starring Kirk Douglas — became one of his most celebrated works. North composed and conducted; the LP credits also list Stanley Kubrick (Film Director), Kirk Douglas (2) (Executive Producer), and screenwriter Dalton Trumbo. The soundtrack has remained in print across 49 pressings on Discogs.

Cleopatra (1963, 20th Century Fox Records) was the largest production of his career. He composed and conducted; associate conductor was Lionel Newman; orchestrations were handled by Henry Brant and David Tamkin; sleeve notes were contributed by director Joseph L. Mankiewicz. The album generated a sprawling series of international pressings and licensed excerpts.

Mid-Career: Stage, Television, and Major Dramas (1964–1979)

The Agony and the Ecstasy (1965, Capitol Records) — the Michelangelo biopic — was another large-scale assignment. Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1966, Warner Bros. Records) showed North working in a more intimate, modern register; produced by Sonny Burke, engineered by Lowell Frank.

Television work became significant from the mid-1970s onward. Rich Man, Poor Man (1976, MCA Records) — the ABC miniseries — saw North compose and conduct the original music, co-conducting with Hal Mooney, with Sonny Burke again producing. The album includes North’s own program notes.

Carny (1980, Warner Bros. Records) was notable for including Robbie Robertson among the performers, reflecting a production that blurred the line between score and rock soundtrack. North also served as producer on that record.

The Unused 2001 Score (1968)

North was commissioned by Stanley Kubrick to compose a full orchestral score for 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). He recorded the sessions — with Henry Brant as orchestrator — in late 1967. Kubrick ultimately chose to use pre-existing classical recordings (Richard Strauss, Johann Strauss II, Ligeti, Khachaturian) rather than North’s commissioned score, releasing the film without notifying North until he saw the finished film at its premiere. The score was thought lost for decades.

The world premiere recording, Alex North’s 2001 (Varèse Sarabande, 1993, release ID: 1836321), was produced and conducted by Jerry Goldsmith with the National Philharmonic Orchestra. The project was co-produced and executive-produced by Robert Townson. Liner notes were contributed by Goldsmith, Elmer Bernstein, Henry Mancini, John Williams (4), and North’s daughter Anna North. The album appeared two years after North’s death and has accumulated six pressings on Discogs.

A second release, Music For 2001: A Space Odyssey (The Original Score) (Intrada, 2007, release ID: 3510239), drew on North’s original recordings rather than a new performance. This release was produced by Nick Redman with associate producers Abby North and Dylan North (2) (family members of the composer). Henry Brant is credited as conductor and orchestrator on this version.

Late Career and Legacy (1980–1991)

Dragonslayer (1981 film, 1983 LP on Label X (3)) — North’s score for the Paramount fantasy film represented one of his last major studio assignments. He composed and conducted.

The Dead / Journey Into Fear (1987, Varèse Sarabande) — two John Huston films scored late in North’s career; the LP was recorded with the Symphonie-Orchester Graunke and released on Varèse Sarabande.

The Prince and the Pauper (1989, Varèse Sarabande) — among his final scores; the National Philharmonic Orchestra appeared again.

The Last Butterfly (1990, Varèse Sarabande) — a German-produced release, recorded with the Symphonie-Orchester Graunke.

North died September 8, 1991 in Pacific Palisades, California.

Posthumous Compilations and Reissues

Unchained Melody (The Film Themes of Alex North) (1990, Bay Cities) and Unchained Melodies: The Film Themes of Alex North (2019, Kritzerland) served as career retrospectives. The Film Music of Alex North (1997, Nonesuch) was produced with the London Symphony Orchestra and remains one of the most comprehensive single-disc surveys.

All Fall Down / The Outrage (2003, Film Score Monthly) and Maléfices / The Bad Seed (2013, Finders Keepers Records) represent archival and specialist label reissues that have continued to appear in the decades since his death.

”Unchained Melody” and Popular Reach

The song “Unchained Melody” (lyrics by Hy Zaret) was written by North for the 1955 film Unchained. It became one of the most recorded songs of the 20th century. Discogs alone shows it credited to North as a songwriter on releases spanning 1979–2021, appearing on records by Righteous Brothers (via the Ghost soundtrack), U2 (Achtung Baby, 1991), Roy Orbison, Al Green, Joni Mitchell (Travelogue, 2002), and many others. The Righteous Brothers’ recording was licensed for the 1990 film Ghost, generating a new wave of chart activity.

Scene Connections

North occupied the center of the Hollywood scoring community from the early 1950s through the 1980s. His Discogs graph connects him to virtually every major figure of the era: Jerry Goldsmith, Henry Mancini, Elmer Bernstein, John Williams (4), Lionel Newman, Alfred Newman, Bernard Herrmann, Miklós Rózsa, Franz Waxman, Dimitri Tiomkin, and Victor Young all appear in his collaborator network.

His early career overlap with the Warner Bros. system (via Ray Heindorf) situates him in the studio-era orchestral tradition. His move into television (Rich Man, Poor Man, 1976) and then back to prestige theatrical releases (Dragonslayer, 1981; The Dead, 1987) maps the general trajectory of Hollywood scoring in that period.

The 2001 story is the single most discussed episode in his biography — less for his immediate circle than for the relationship with Stanley Kubrick, who also directed Spartacus (the two had a productive earlier collaboration before the 1968 break). The 1993 premiere recording, organized by Goldsmith and Townson at Varèse Sarabande, served partly as a statement of the film music community’s regard for North’s work.

  • His Moscow Conservatory years (ca. 1933–35) and early New York dance/theater work (Martha Graham, Anna Sokolow) are not represented in Discogs credits — that period predates the recorded soundtrack era.
  • The 2001 recording sessions: precise dates, studio location, and the full orchestra roster from the 1967 sessions are not in Discogs; the Intrada release credits Henry Brant as conductor/orchestrator but does not specify the ensemble.
  • Academy Award nominations (reportedly 15, with one win for Spartacus) are not confirmed via Discogs.
  • His studies with Ernst Toch and Aaron Copland are not documented in Discogs.
