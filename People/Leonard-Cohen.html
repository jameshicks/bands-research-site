Leonard Cohen

Singer-songwriter, poet, and novelist, born September 21, 1934, Westmount, Montreal, Quebec; died November 7, 2016, Los Angeles. Discogs artist ID: 227848. Real name: Leonard Norman Cohen.

One of the defining voices in 20th-century popular song, Cohen published poetry and fiction before recording his debut album at age 33. His catalog spans fifty years and fifteen studio albums, almost all of them on Columbia Records or its European subsidiary CBS. Known for an unhurried, spoken-near delivery, literary lyrics, and the recurring themes of desire, faith, and mortality, Cohen is most associated with “Suzanne” (1967), “Bird on the Wire” (1969), “Famous Blue Raincoat” (1971), “Hallelujah” (1984), and “You Want It Darker” (2016). Inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2008. Member of The Unified Heart Touring Band (Discogs artist ID: 3907900). Also contributes spoken-word vocals to a version of Prince’s “1999” on Paul Shaffer & The Party Boys Of Rock ‘N’ Roll’s The World’s Most Dangerous Party (1993).

Overview

Early life and literary career (1934–1966)

Cohen grew up in a middle-class Jewish family in Westmount. He published his first poetry collection, Let Us Compare Mythologies, in 1956 (McGill Poetry Series), followed by The Spice-Box of Earth (1961) and the novels The Favourite Game (1963) and Beautiful Losers (1966). His spoken-word recording Six Montreal Poets (Folkways Records, FL 9805, 1957) catches him reading his own verse alongside other Montreal poets — his only Folkways appearance. The Canadian Broadcasting Corporation documented him again on Canadian Poets 1 (1966).

Nashville period and Columbia debut (1967–1973)

Cohen moved to New York and signed with Columbia in 1967. Producer John Simon helmed the debut, Songs of Leonard Cohen (Columbia CS 9533, 1968), with backing vocals by Nancy Priddy. It established the spare guitar-and-voice template that would define much of his work. The next three studio albums — Songs from a Room (CBS CS 9767, 1969), Songs of Love and Hate (Columbia C 30103, 1971), and the live document Live Songs (Columbia KC 32032, 1973) — were produced by Bob Johnston, who had recently worked with Bob Dylan in Nashville. The house band for those sessions included guitarist Ron Cornelius, multi-instrumentalist Elkin “Bubba” Fowler, and, on Songs of Love and Hate, Charlie Daniels (guitar, bass, fiddle) and Paul Buckmaster (string and horn arrangements). Johnston also plays piano and organ on the live records.

John Lissauer era (1974–1985)

New Skin for the Old Ceremony (Columbia KC 33167, 1974) introduced co-producer and arranger John Lissauer, a New York multi-instrumentalist who shaped a warmer, more jazz-tinged sound. Guest vocalists included Janis Ian and Emily Bindiger. Lissauer returned for Various Positions (CBS CBS 26222, 1984 — the album Columbia declined to release in the US), which contained “Hallelujah,” “Dance Me to the End of Love,” and “If It Be Your Will.”

Between those two Lissauer albums, Cohen made the notorious Death of a Ladies’ Man (CBS 86042, 1977) with producer Phil Spector, recording at Gold Star Studios, Los Angeles. The “Wall of Sound” result divided critics; the dense arrangements and large session ensemble (Hal Blaine, Jim Keltner, Jesse Ed Davis, Clydie King, and dozens more) overwhelmed Cohen’s voice. Cohen later distanced himself from it. Recent Songs (CBS 86097, 1979) returned to a sparser approach, co-produced with Henry Lewy, and introduced oud player John Bilezikjian and violinist Raffi Hakopian. Jennifer Warnes provided harmony arrangements.

Return to popularity (1988–1994)

I’m Your Man (CBS 460642 1, 1988) — self-produced — updated the palette with synthesizers, drum machines, and Cohen’s own arrangements, the wit of “Tower of Song” and “Everybody Knows” attracting a new generation. Engineer and longtime associate Leanne Ungar co-ordinated technical production; Roscoe Beck handled production logistics and served as music director on subsequent tours. The Future (Columbia COL 472498 2, 1992) continued in the same electronic vein, adding a large gospel choir and strings arranged by David Campbell. Cohen Live (Columbia, 1994) documented the 1993 world tour.

Retreat, Ten New Songs, and Dear Heather (1994–2004)

After the 1993–94 tour, Cohen spent much of the mid-to-late 1990s at the Mount Baldy Zen Center, California, ordained as a Rinzai Buddhist monk. He emerged to record Ten New Songs (Columbia COL 501202 2, 2001), co-written and co-produced entirely with Sharon Robinson, who also handled all arrangements and programming. Engineer and mixer Leanne Ungar rounded out the core team. Dear Heather (Columbia COL 514768 2, 2004) was co-produced by Robinson, Anjani Thomas, and Ungar; Thomas, who became his partner, played piano and sang on both albums.

Concert return and final albums (2008–2019)

Destitute after his manager Kelley Lynch embezzled most of his retirement savings, Cohen launched a massive world tour in 2008–10 — his first concerts in fifteen years. Live in London (Columbia/Sony Music, 88697405022, 2009) and Songs from the Road (Columbia/Legacy, 2010) document the tour. Old Ideas (Columbia, 2012) was produced and mixed in collaboration with Patrick Leonard (who co-mixed), Ed Sanders (2), and Leanne Ungar; the Unified Heart Touring Band — including Roscoe Beck (bass), Bob Metzger (guitar), Javier Mas (archilaud), and vocalists Sharon Robinson, The Webb Sisters (Charley and Hattie Webb) — anchored the sessions. Popular Problems (Columbia, 88875014292, 2014) was produced by Patrick Leonard. You Want It Darker (Columbia, 88985365072, 2016), Cohen’s final studio album, was produced by his son Adam Cohen and recorded with the Montreal choir Shaar Hashomayim. Cohen died three weeks after its release.

The posthumous Thanks for the Dance (Columbia/Legacy, 2019), also produced by Adam Cohen, was assembled from voice sketches and demos recorded during the You Want It Darker sessions, with new orchestral and ensemble arrangements contributed by a range of collaborators.

Connections

  • Phil Spector — produced Death of a Ladies’ Man (1977); the record was an uncomfortable collaboration Cohen later criticized
  • Bob Johnston — produced Cohen’s Nashville-era records (1969–73), having also recently produced Bob Dylan at Columbia
  • Jennifer Warnes — close associate from the 1979 tour; released Famous Blue Raincoat (1986), a full album of Cohen songs
  • Sharon Robinson — co-writing and production partner from 1988 onward; sole producer of Ten New Songs
  • Adam Cohen — Cohen’s son; produced final two albums
  • Charlie Daniels — played on the Nashville sessions before his own country-rock career took off
  • Janis Ian — guest vocalist on New Skin for the Old Ceremony
  • Paul Buckmaster — string arranger on Songs of Love and Hate, also associated with Elton John’s early records
  • Paul Shaffer & The Party Boys Of Rock ‘N’ Roll — Cohen contributes spoken word to their 1993 cover of “1999”

Open Questions

  • The Discogs entry for Songs of Leonard Cohen credits John Simon as producer, but some sources credit John Hammond as the A&R man who signed Cohen; the exact creative division is unclear from DB data alone.
  • Various Positions was rejected by Columbia US (released on Passport Records in the US); the Discogs entries reflect the CBS European release (CBS 26222) as the canonical one. The US Passport pressing deserves a note.
  • I’m Your Man (1988) is listed in the DB as self-produced with no additional production credits beyond logistical coordinators — the exact recording setup (which synths, studios) is not captured in Discogs.
  • Cohen’s relationship with the Rinzai Zen Buddhist community at Mount Baldy and its musical influence on the late 1990s work is not documentable from Discogs alone.
  • Jennifer Warnes’s Famous Blue Raincoat (1986) should get its own Releases page.
  • Anjani Thomas released a solo album Blue Alert (2006) produced by Cohen — worth a stub/page.
