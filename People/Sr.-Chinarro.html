Overview

Sr. Chinarro is the long-running project of Antonio Luque, a Seville-born songwriter consistently cited as one of the finest lyricists in Spanish pop and a pioneer of Spain’s indie and alternative scene. Formed in 1990, the group made an early transatlantic leap when its debut album was recorded in New Jersey with Kramer — producer of records for Low, Galaxie 500, and Unrest — an unlikely alliance that set the project apart from its contemporaries. The catalogue spans four active decades and roughly 16 full-lengths, moving across indie rock, lo-fi, and acoustic modes, with a critical breakthrough arriving via El Fuego Amigo (2005), produced by J of Los Planetas, who returned to helm the follow-up El Progreso. After a formative run on Acuarela through 2003, Mushroom Pillow became the project’s main home from 2006 onward, anchoring 20 releases through 2024; recurring collaborators Alfonso Espadero, Pablo Cabra, David Belmonte, and Jordi Gil reflect the fluid ensemble Luque has drawn on throughout.

The Kramer connection — how it came about

The bridge between Antonio Luque and Kramer was Jesús Llorente, founder of both the Malsonando fanzine and Acuarela Discos. Multiple sources confirm that Llorente used his industry contacts (“sus contactos”) to get Kramer to listen to Sr. Chinarro’s material and agree to record and produce the debut album. The album was recorded in May 1994 at Noise N.J. (a New Jersey studio Kramer was associated with). Antonio Luque has described the trip as “una aventura, una aventura dura” — the band traveled to New Jersey with an unstable lineup, amid internal tensions among the usual members, so Luque effectively made the record without the group’s regular players.

Llorente’s route to Kramer is not documented in any source found: none of the available interviews or label histories specify whether the introduction came through a mutual contact, Acuarela’s US distribution network (the label had distribution in several countries including the US), or Llorente’s work as a music journalist. The connection was evidently personal to Llorente rather than institutional — Acuarela was barely a year old at the time of the recording.

The fit was not arbitrary: Kramer’s production credits for Galaxie 500, Low, and Unrest were direct touchstones for Sr. Chinarro’s sound, so Llorente was targeting a producer whose aesthetic matched what Luque was doing.
