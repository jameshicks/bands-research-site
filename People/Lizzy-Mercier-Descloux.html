Lizzy Mercier Descloux

Lizzy Mercier Descloux

Oct 10, 202618 min read

Lizzy Mercier Descloux

Real name: Martine-Elisabeth Mercier Descloux Born: 16 December 1956, Paris (raised in Lyon) Died: 20 April 2004, Saint-Florent, Corsica Alias: Mara Missangas (active 2003–2005 per DB) Groups: Rosa Yemen, Aural Exciters Partner: Michel Esteban (ZE Records co-founder)

Singer, musician, painter, photographer, collagist, and writer. The most critically significant French artist on ZE Records, and one of the figures connecting the Paris punk scene to downtown NYC. Her five studio albums each absorbed a different geography — New York, Nassau, Johannesburg, Rio de Janeiro, rural England/Wales — and the music reflected those places directly, years before “world music” became an industry category.

Early Life and Paris

Raised in Lyon; returned to Paris as a teenager. Attended the Ecole des Beaux-Arts in Paris (dropped out around 1975). Self-taught on guitar alongside her visual art practice.

With Michel Esteban, she co-founded Harry Cover — a Paris shop selling books, records, and clothing, described as “the temple of the punk movement in France,” with a basement rehearsal space for bands. They also co-created Rock News, one of the first European punk/new wave magazines covering the London/New York/Paris scene simultaneously. It was through Rock News that they first went to New York — traveling in 1975, where they met Patti Smith, Richard Hell, and Television.

How Esteban met Descloux: He saw her from the window of his apartment on rue des Halles in Paris, left her a note saying she looked nice, invited her to the shop. That was the beginning.

New York (1977 onward)

She and Michel Esteban relocated permanently to New York in 1977. She became a fixture on the CBGB circuit — described as “a regular heartbreaker on the CBGB scene.” She roomed with Patti Smith and had a romantic relationship with Richard Hell. Hell later based a female protagonist in one of his novels on her.

She appeared in Amos Poe’s no wave documentary film Blank Generation (1976 — shot during her earlier NY visit).

In 1977, she collaborated with Smith and Hell on Desiderata — a poetry and image book. Smith provided a preface and illustrations; Hell contributed. (A new edition was published by Inpatient Press / Mercurial Editions in 2025.)

She photographed Patti Smith’s Radio Ethiopia album insert (Arista, 1976) — a pre-ZE connection to the downtown scene.

Early NYC project: Rosa Yemen — a performance art duo with Didier Esteban (Michel’s brother, credited as D.J. Barnes). Their live mini-LP, recorded in NYC in July 1978, was one of ZE’s earliest releases.

Discography

Rosa Yemen (ZE Records, 1978)

Pre-Press Color project with Didier Esteban. Recorded live NYC, July 1978. One of the earliest ZE releases. Reissued on ZE Brazil in 2007.

Press Color (ZE Records, 1979)

Debut album. Recorded February 1979 at Blank Tape Studios (Blank Tapes Studio), New York City (the same room as James White & The Blacks). Engineers: Bob Blank and Joe Arlotta. Produced, arranged, and mixed by D.J. Banes, Erik Elliasson, and Descloux; Michel Esteban as exclusive producer. Recorded in approximately 15 days, most songs written during sessions. Running time: approximately 23 minutes.

The album is built on minimalist guitar — single-note lines, wrong-note harmonies, funky rhythms — with disco/dance elements and film score references. Covers include:

  • “Fire” (Arthur Brown) — lead single; released on ZE and licensed to Philips (France), Ariola/Columbia (Australia/NZ), Island (Japan)
  • “Tumor” — her version of Peggy Lee’s “Fever”
  • “Jim on the Move” and the Mission Impossible theme — Lalo Schifrin compositions

Original reception: poor commercially. Toured US and Europe.

Reissues:

  • ZE Records France (2003) — added bonus tracks including the Rosa Vertov EP, three unreleased tracks, and a duet with Patti Smith; also features Arto Lindsay on vocals on bonus tracks
  • P-Vine Records Japan (2009)
  • Light in the Attic (2015) — the reissue that drove critical reappraisal; Pitchfork 8.2/10 (“a vivid curio and cool personality splatter”); PopMatters 9/10 (“a quintessential record of the post-punk era”); Far Out 4.5/5
  • Press Color Remixes & Edits (ZE, 2015) — contemporaneous remix EP

Mambo Nassau (Philips/ZE, 1981)

Second album. Recorded at Compass Point Studios, Nassau, Bahamas — Chris Blackwell’s Island-bankrolled studio, home of the Compass Point All Stars. Michel Esteban as exclusive producer; Steven Stanley as engineer and producer (the same reggae/dub engineer who worked with Sly & Robbie).

Key contributors: Wally Badarou (synthesizer — confirmed in 2003 reissue credits; also co-wrote and co-produced), Sly Dunbar (drums), and a full ensemble including Brazilian session players: Djalma Correa (percussion), Jamil Joanes and Jorge Degas (bass), Leo Gandelman (saxophone), Sivuca (accordion), Paulinho Braga (drums).

Includes a cover of Kool and the Gang’s “Funky Stuff”. Label moved to Philips for the French releases; Columbia for Japan. AllMusic: 4.5 stars.

The 2003 ZE France reissue added bonus tracks including a vocal contribution by Arto Lindsay.

Reissues: ZE France (2003); P-Vine Japan (2009); Light in the Attic (2016).

Zulu Rock / Lizzy Mercier Descloux (CBS, 1984)

Third album. Recorded at Satbel Studio, Johannesburg, South Africa (1983), with additional sessions at Berry Street Studio, London (1984). Produced by Adam Kidron and Michel Esteban.

The South African sessions used township jazz and mbaqanga musicians: Sammy Kiaas, Steve Siewell, Desmond Malotana, Hayward Mahlangu, David Mabaso, Fats Mlangeni, Javas Magubane, Allian David, Thomas Phale. She had traveled through Africa following Arthur Rimbaud’s trail, visiting Ethiopia and South Africa before the sessions.

Released as a self-titled LP in France/Europe/Japan (CBS/Epic International) and as Zulu Rock in the UK (CBS) and US (Epic). Recording under apartheid in 1983 was a politically loaded choice; the album is an early and substantive engagement with African sounds that predated the “world music” marketing category by years.

Single “Mais Où Sont Passées Les Gazelles?” — a genuine hit in France; the album’s commercial peak. Singles “Wakwazulu Kwezizulu Rock” (Italy/France/Netherlands) and “Zulu Rock” (UK/US extended version) also released.

Reissues: ZE France (2006); P-Vine Japan (2009); Light in the Attic (2016).

One For The Soul (Polydor, 1986)

Fourth album. Recorded at Polygram Studios, Rio de Janeiro (July–August 1985); mixed Paris (September–October 1985). Label moved to Polydor.

The first album made without Michel Esteban as collaborator in the prior sense — the partnership, personal and professional, had ended between Zulu Rock and this record. Produced by Adam Kidron (who also sings backing vocals).

Features Chet Baker on trumpet and backing vocals — she had met Baker at a Rio de Janeiro jazz festival. Brazilian ensemble: Sivuca (accordion), Jamil Joanes and Jorge Degas (bass), Leo Gandelman (saxophone), Paulinho Braga (drums), Djalma Correa (percussion).

Singles: “Calypso Moguls”, “Fog Horn Blues”.

Reissues: ZE Brazil (2009); P-Vine Japan (2009); Light in the Attic (2016).

Suspense (Polydor, 1988)

Fifth and final studio album. Recorded in Oxfordshire and Wales. A return toward a more European, experimental direction.

Mark Cunningham (Mars — one of the four No New York bands) produced the album, played trumpet and bass, and contributed lyrics. Also on the session: John Brand (co-producer), Bruce Smith (drums — The Pop Group, Rip Rig + Panic, PiL), Kim Burton (accordion), Stuart Gordon (viola, violin, cello).

Single: “Gueule D’Amour”.

Reissues: ZE Brazil (2009); P-Vine Japan (2009); Light in the Attic (2016).

Other Appearances

Aural Exciters — “Spooks In Space” (ZE Records, 1981)

Descloux on vocals alongside Pat Place (guitar), James Chance (horns), Bob Blank (engineer, producer, guitar), and Andy Hernandez / Coati Mundi (vibraphone). The Aural Exciters were a ZE Records aggregation rather than a fixed band — this track places Descloux within the same Blank Tape Studios downtown orbit as her debut album.

Patti Smith — Radio Ethiopia (Arista, 1976)

Descloux photographed the album insert — a connection to Smith established in Paris and New York before ZE Records was founded. The two maintained the friendship through the ZE years; Smith contributed a preface to Desiderata (1977) and appears in a duet on the 2003 Press Color reissue.

Bill Laswell — Hashisheen: The End Of Law (Meta Records, 2000)

Descloux reads text on this spoken-word/music project by Bill Laswell, alongside Patti Smith, Iggy Pop, Genesis P-Orridge, and William S. Burroughs. Music by Jah Wobble and Laswell. A late-career document of her continued engagement with avant-garde circles despite having retreated from active music-making.

Each album was rooted in the specific place where she was living or working at the time. The geographical logic was consistent even when the sounds changed radically.

Light in the Attic Reissues (2015–2016)

Light in the Attic reissued the entire studio catalog: Press Color (2015), then Mambo Nassau, Zulu Rock, One For The Soul, and Suspense (all 2016). This was the critical reappraisal moment — the work was finally audible in its full sequence, reviewed by a generation that could place it in context. Esteban cooperated with the project after her death.

Death

Diagnosed with ovarian and colorectal cancer in 2003. Died 20 April 2004 in Saint-Florent, Haute-Corse, Corsica. She was 47. Her ashes were scattered at sea. In the mid-to-late 1990s she had already retreated from music to Corsica, where she focused on painting, writing, and working on an unpublished novel, Buenaventura.

The South Africa Sessions (1983) — Context and Reception

The boycott context

When Descloux and Esteban traveled to South Africa in 1983 to record what became Zulu Rock, the UN cultural boycott of South Africa was already active (formally from 1980). Esteban has acknowledged this directly: “It was forbidden for an artist to go to South Africa because there was an artistic boycott.” (NewBeats, March 2016, https://newbeats.com/2016/03/11/lizzy-mercier-descloux/) They proceeded anyway — Alain Levy, head of CBS France, backed the project.

The recording took place at Satbel Studio, Johannesburg, not in Soweto itself (contrary to some later descriptions), though Descloux returned to Soweto in 1984 to perform at The Pelican — then described as the largest underground club in Soweto — and to film the “Gazelles” music video. (Wikipedia, Lizzy Mercier Descloux, as of 2026-06-27)

The musicians’ initial reaction — the McLaren shadow

The township musicians were initially suspicious of Descloux. The reason: Malcolm McLaren’s Duck Rock (released May 1983) had drawn on South African, South American, and Caribbean music without crediting the source musicians, and McLaren had faced legal action as a result. Coming in the immediate wake of that controversy, two white French people arriving to record mbaqanga was viewed with wariness. According to sources citing the 2016 LITA reissue context, after approximately a week in the studio the musicians decided her intentions were honorable and the collaboration opened up. (Light in the Attic product page; also summarized in Big Takeover review below)

Esteban recalls the first week: “The first week, the musicians didn’t understand why two white French people came to do with them. But after a while, they understood. It was so much fun in the studio and I think that you can feel it on the album, too.” (NewBeats, March 2016)

The credits question

Descloux faced accusations of swiping existing mbaqanga and Shangaan disco tunes and refashioning them with French lyrics — a parallel to the McLaren charges. Her stated defense: “It’s not true — half of the music on this album is credited to its authors, and the other half is made up of popular music that I rearranged and re-adapted to such an extent that nobody has the right to put their name to it.” (Source cited in web aggregations of the LITA reissue context; original publication not confirmed — likely a 1984 French press interview.)

Adam Kidron’s account of the process, however, does not fully support that framing. In his liner notes for the 2016 LITA reissue he describes the method more candidly: “We listened to the top records in South Africa at the time, took the melodies, re-recorded them with a bunch of musicians and she just put French lyrics on top of them.” (LITA 2016 liner notes, cited at https://lightintheattic.net/releases/2060-zulu-rock)

The key single, “Mais Où Sont Passées Les Gazelles?”, is an adaptation of Obed Ngobeni’s 1983 hit “Ku Hluvukile ka ‘Zete’” — a Shangaan/Xitsonga track. (Not, as some 2016 reviews incorrectly stated, a song “associated with Mahlathini”; Mahlathini recorded his own “Kazet” version only in 1987 on Paris–Soweto.) The source of the original and the extent of Ngobeni’s credit on the album are not confirmed from available sources. (Research paper on Obed Ngobeni, HTS Teologiese Studies / Theological Studies; NewBeats)

Descloux’s stated position

Descloux used the album’s commercial success — “Gazelles” was a genuine hit in France in summer 1984 — to speak publicly against apartheid. Her position on the ethics of recording there:

“I’ve always been aware of racism and intolerance, but the project very quickly became political. But I think being subtle is better than barbed wire and a black fist on the cover. The fact that the record exists is significant enough.”

(Source: quoted in multiple 2016 retrospective sources; original interview venue not confirmed — likely French press, 1984.)

The album’s anti-apartheid content is also embedded lyrically. On “Sun’s Jive” she sings of the Afrikaners: “They should go back and disappear forever / Where? / The middle of nowhere / The bottom of the sea.”

She also spent eight hours during the 1984 video shoot interviewing Soweto residents about their experiences of apartheid, intending to release a book; the book was never published. (NewBeats; Wikipedia)

Contemporaneous press reaction — what is and isn’t documented

No specific NME, Melody Maker, or Libération reviews addressing the apartheid dimension of the recording have been located. The album received strong reviews in France (Esteban cites it as her commercial peak, some French critics named it album of the year) but no documented named reaction from anti-apartheid organizations or music press in 1984 specifically criticizing the boycott breach has been found.

The absence of organized criticism distinguishes it markedly from the Paul Simon Graceland controversy (1986), which generated named protests from the ANC, Artists United Against Apartheid, and sustained press debate. The Descloux case predates that moment when the boycott became a major Anglophone music-press flashpoint.

The 2016 Big Takeover review of the LITA reissue explicitly positions Zulu Rock as the overlooked “odd middle sibling” between McLaren’s Duck Rock and Simon’s Graceland — noting that all three involved Western artists drawing on South African township music during apartheid, and that Descloux’s case received the least scrutiny despite coming first. (Big Takeover)

The 2016 reissue — how it addresses the question

The Light in the Attic 2016 reissue (March 10, 2016) includes liner notes by Vivien Goldman (journalist; also wrote the Press Color reissue liner notes) and by producer Adam Kidron. The Kidron notes contain his candid account of the recording method (cited above). Goldman’s notes contextualize the trip through Africa following Rimbaud’s route — through Sudan, Ethiopia, Tanzania, Zimbabwe, and South Africa. The 2016 reissue prompted profiles in The Quietus (David McKenna, September 2015), The Pitchfork Review, and The Arts Desk. None of the accessible 2016 retrospective pieces contain evidence that the apartheid question became a source of organized contemporary criticism in 1984; the dominant 2016 framing treats the breach as a matter of record that is noted rather than litigated.

Notes (web): Adam Kidron

Adam Kidron had no documented staff role at ZE Records — he was an independent producer brought in for specific projects, not an A&R executive, co-owner, or label employee. His connection to ZE was through his relationship with Lizzy Mercier Descloux (Wikipedia describes her as his “then-girlfriend” during the Zulu Rock sessions) and through Michel Esteban, who remained the executive/exclusive producer credit on ZE releases.

Background before Descloux: British-born, Kidron entered the record industry in 1978, working in production at Rough Trade Records and Stiff Records, collaborating with Davey Payne and The Blockheads. He produced the debut albums of both Scritti Politti and Orange Juice. (Wikipedia — Adam Kidron, as of 2026-07-28)

The Descloux productions: He produced Zulu Rock (1984) co-credited with Esteban, and One For The Soul (1986) as sole producer. During the Zulu Rock sessions (1984), he was also producing Nina Hagen’s Nina Hagen in Ekstasy (1985) and suffered a severe motorbike accident during that period. (Wikipedia — Adam Kidron)

Post-ZE career: After the mid-1980s music production work, Kidron moved into television production — he became a partner at Crossbow Films Ltd., a UK-based independent TV production company, developing formats with Henry Winkler, Ben Elton, Quincy Jones, and Clarence Avant. In 1990 he founded Marvellous Pictures, Inc., and in 1991 co-produced Catwalk, a syndicated TV series notable for launching Neve Campbell’s career. He served as music supervisor and executive producer on the Jason’s Lyric soundtrack (1994), co-executive produced the Panther soundtrack (1995), and executive produced The Rhythm of the Games, the official 1996 Atlanta Olympics soundtrack. From 1999 onward he shifted into internet and tech ventures: Urban Box Office Networks (urban/reggaeton digital portal, raised $40M, bankrupt 2000); Urban Box Office record label (reggaeton/Latin compilations, bankrupt 2006); Beyond Oblivion (mobile music startup, spent $33M without launching, bankrupt 2011); 4Food (iPad restaurant ordering, 2010); and Yonder Music (mobile-only streaming service, 2014–2018, shut down March 2018). (Wikipedia — Adam Kidron, as of 2026-07-28)

Family: Son of Marxist economist Michael Kidron; brother of film director Beeban Kidron.

Conflict note: The existing vault entry (sourced from Discogs) lists Kidron as “co-produced Zulu Rock” and “produced One For The Soul” — this is consistent with Wikipedia. Wikipedia does not attribute any ZE Records staff title to him, and the ZE Records Wikipedia article does not mention him. His high shared-release count with Descloux (50 in the Discogs collaborator table) reflects multiple pressings and reissues of both albums rather than a broad independent label relationship.
