Cosey Fanni Tutti

Discogs ID: 93112 Real name: Christine Carol Newby. UK musician, performance artist, and author. Co-founder of Throbbing Gristle and Industrial Records.

Overview

Cosey Fanni Tutti was a central member of COUM Transmissions before co-founding Throbbing Gristle with Genesis P-Orridge, Peter Christopherson, and Chris Carter. On TG records she played lead guitar (described as “satellite lead guitar” and “gizmo guitar”), cornet, effects, and contributed synthesizer and vocals. She also contributed piano and vocals to Monte Cazazza’s Something For Nobody (IR0010, 1980).

Following TG’s dissolution in 1981, she formed Chris & Cosey with Chris Carter, releasing material on Rough Trade and Conspiracy International. Later collaboration projects include Carter Tutti and Carter Tutti Void.

Industrial Records Period (1976–1981): Art Sex Music (2017)

Cosey Fanni Tutti’s autobiography Art Sex Music (Faber & Faber, 2017), drawn from lifelong diaries, is the primary account of her perspective on the TG and IR years. The book was written partly in response to a 1999 TG biography she felt misrepresented her contribution — she noted in her diary at the time: “I’m determined to get a book out that puts the record straight.” [source: https://freq.org.uk/reviews/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music/]

Her Role and Musical Contributions

Multiple reviewers note that Cosey’s influence on TG was larger than her public credit suggested: she “was behind a lot of the best things that TG does, but she wears this influence lightly,” and the autobiography traces the evolution of their sound and pays “due credit to ‘who came up with what’ in terms of ideas and instrumentation.” [source: https://freq.org.uk/reviews/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music/]

Instrumentally, she played guitar using found objects and improvised techniques, routing her sound through effects units built by Chris Carter; she also performed cornet and provided vocals, most notably the breathy delivery on “Hot On The Heels Of Love” (1979), which anticipated the vocal approach she would develop with Chris & Cosey. [source: https://giuseppezevolli.com/2017/04/05/1786/ (Drowned in Sound review)]

The book frames TG’s working method as explicitly non-rock: “We weren’t a band,” Cosey clarifies. The four members — Cosey, Genesis P-Orridge, Peter Christopherson, and Chris Carter (2) — functioned as a synergistic entity, with improvisation and machine rhythms at the core. Carter built custom synthesizers and effects hardware (including the Gristleizer). The group prioritized experimentation and sought audience discomfort rather than approval: “We didn’t seek understanding. We didn’t want people saying, ‘You’re very good!‘” [source: https://www.huckmag.com/article/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music-outsider-interview]

TG’s formation is described as organic rather than strategic — Carter’s synthesizer equipment came into collaborative sessions and the group cohered within roughly a week, starting from improvised pieces before deciding to document their work. [source: https://desperate-living.com/2017/05/15/interview-cosey-fanni-tutti/]

Labor and the Economics of IR

The autobiography is candid about the gendered and financial realities behind IR’s operation. Cosey maintained various day jobs — including factory work, office work, stripping, nude modeling, and sex work — to help keep the band and label financially afloat. Domestic labor fell disproportionately on her as well: “Home didn’t feel like home but like more work.” The book makes clear that she regarded the sex work as hers to use on her own terms, “subverting it with her empowering and pure feminist approach” rather than as exploitation. [source: http://industrialantioppression.blogspot.com/2017/06/chatty-review-of-cosey-fanni-tuttis-new.html]

Administrative load (managing mail-order operations, etc.) is named as a factor in TG’s dissolution: the burden prevented creative work, making continuation unsustainable. [source: https://desperate-living.com/2017/05/15/interview-cosey-fanni-tuttis/]

Relationship with Genesis P-Orridge

The autobiography documents P-Orridge’s abusive behavior at length. Reviewers describe escalating mental and physical abuse over the IR period, including an incident described as P-Orridge nearly killing Cosey with a paving slab. He is characterized as “manipulative and self-serving, taking credit for others’ ideas” and “an iconoclastic but disastrous little bully, this emotional vampire.” Cosey describes recognizing his violence as his own issue rather than a reflection on her, and maintained emotional distance while managing the practical fallout on the group. She also describes P-Orridge claiming ownership of her modeling work. (The fractured relationship had legal afterlives: P-Orridge filed a 2013 lawsuit against Cosey and Chris Carter.) [sources: https://louderthanwar.com/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music-book-review/, http://industrialantioppression.blogspot.com/2017/06/chatty-review-of-cosey-fanni-tuttis-new.html]

Note: one anonymous blog reviewer (Industrial Anti-Oppression, industrialantioppression.blogspot.com) characterizes the industrial anti-oppression framing of the P-Orridge material in detail; this is a fan/commentary source without a byline, so treat specific phrasing from it with corresponding caution.

Relationship with Chris Carter

Carter is portrayed in strongly positive terms throughout. The Industrial Records period overlaps with the development of their personal relationship, which the book describes with “tenderness and warmth.” Carter is credited as supportive and non-controlling: he “never tries to control Tutti’s life,” and it was partly through him that she recognized the domestic conflict with P-Orridge was not normal. They eventually left London for Norfolk together. [sources: https://louderthanwar.com/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music-book-review/, https://www.huckmag.com/article/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music-outsider-interview]

Artistic Philosophy and TG’s Dissolution

The book articulates TG’s mission as addressing “the full spectrum of human behaviour” rather than mere shock value. The band used imagery deliberately — Nazi death camp associations, the slogan “Music From The Death Factory” — with conceptual intent. Cosey traces her own understanding of art as life-affirming, and reviewers note she maintained “a life of purity, even amongst a life of dissolution.” [source: https://freq.org.uk/reviews/cosey-fanni-tutti-art-sex-music/]

TG is described as “dysfunctional and always on the brink of collapse” throughout the IR period, mirroring equipment constantly pushed to breaking points. The band ended in 1981 because, in Cosey’s account, “it became something it was never meant to be” — success had turned them into unwanted “rock stars” and “cult heroes,” contradicting their anti-hierarchical philosophy. [source: https://desperate-living.com/2017/05/15/interview-cosey-fanni-tutti/]
