Art Blakey

Jazz drummer, bandleader, and perennial talent incubator. Born Abdullah Ibn Buhaina, 11 October 1919, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Died 16 October 1990, New York City, age 71. Discogs artist ID: 29977. Art Blakey & The Jazz Messengers Discogs ID: 262128.

Biography

Early Years (1919–1944)

Arthur Blakey grew up in Pittsburgh under a Seventh Day Adventist foster family, learning piano and Bible in parallel. In the early 1930s he was playing piano at the Democratic Club in Pittsburgh when — by his own account, at gunpoint from the club owner — he was ordered off the keys to make room for another pianist and put behind a drum kit instead. The forced switch had a productive side effect: it put him in proximity to drummer and bandleader Chick Webb, whom Blakey served as valet and under whom he developed his foundational technique.

Returning to Pittsburgh in 1937, Blakey formed his own band, backing pianist Mary Lou Williams. In 1939 he joined Fletcher Henderson’s orchestra for three years, followed by a residency at Boston’s Tic Toc club. His next significant posting was with Billy Eckstine’s big band — one of the defining bebop ensembles of the 1940s — where he performed alongside Fats Navarro, Dexter Gordon, and Miles Davis.

Transition and Islam (1944–1954)

When Eckstine disbanded his group in 1947, Blakey organized the Seventeen Messengers, a rehearsal band, and recorded with an octet called the Jazz Messengers — the first of many groups bearing that name. In 1948 he traveled to Africa, where he studied polyrhythmic drumming and converted to Islam, taking the name Abdullah Ibn Buhaina. He continued to record intermittently; early 1950s sessions found him alongside Thelonious Monk, Clifford Brown, and Horace Silver.

The group that became the Jazz Messengers formally crystallized around 1954–55 out of a cooperative between Blakey and Horace Silver. Their debut recording at the Café Bohemia in 1955 — At The Café Bohemia, Vols. 1 & 2 (Blue Note, 1956), with Silver on piano, Kenny Dorham on trumpet, Hank Mobley on tenor, and Doug Watkins on bass — set the template.

Blue Note Peak: Hard Bop Decade (1954–1965)

By 1956 Silver had departed to lead his own group, and Blakey became the Jazz Messengers’ sole leader. He maintained that role without interruption until his death. The group was never a fixed ensemble — Blakey rotated personnel deliberately, using the band as a teaching institution. Major lineups of this period:

1958 Moanin’ band: Lee Morgan (trumpet), Benny Golson (tenor), Bobby Timmons (piano), Jymie Merritt (bass). Golson composed the title track and “Blues March.” Recorded at Van Gelder Studio; produced by Alfred Lion.

1958 Atlantic sessions: A separate engagement with Thelonious Monk produced Art Blakey’s Jazz Messengers With Thelonious Monk (Atlantic, 1958), with Johnny Griffin on tenor and Bill Hardman on trumpet; supervised by Nesuhi Ertegun.

1961–65 classic quintet: The lineup of Freddie Hubbard (trumpet), Wayne Shorter (tenor), Curtis Fuller (trombone), Cedar Walton (piano), and Reggie Workman (bass) is widely considered the peak configuration. Key documents include Ugetsu (Riverside, 1963) and Free For All (Blue Note, 1965), both produced at their respective studios. This band captured Shorter mid-development — he joined fresh from Art Farmer/Gigi Gryce and departed for Miles Davis.

Side activities included drum extravaganzas — Orgy In Rhythm Vols. 1 & 2 (Blue Note, 1957), Holiday For Skins Vols. 1 & 2 (Blue Note, 1958) — explorations of polyrhythm rooted in Blakey’s Africa trip.

Columbia releases Drum Suite and Hard Bop (both 1957) broadened his commercial reach. Paris and European tours starting in 1959 (documented on Les Liaisons Dangereuses, Fontana, 1959; Paris Concert, Epic) established a major European following. Blakey’s music was woven into the French new wave — the Les Liaisons Dangereuses soundtrack is among the most-documented Jazz Messengers overseas recordings.

Transitional Period (1965–1977)

After the departure of Shorter and Hubbard, Blakey cycled through lineups on Limelight, Cadet, and a quieter stretch on Prestige/Milestone. Key releases: Soul Finger (Limelight, 1965), Anthenagin (Prestige, 1973) with Woody Shaw and Cedar Walton. Touring continued intensively, especially in Japan, where the band’s following remained enormous — multiple Japanese label pressings on Victor and CBS/Sony document this period extensively.

Renaissance: Concord and Timeless (1977–1985)

By the late 1970s a second peak arrived. Blakey recruited young musicians with formal conservatory training and shaped them as he had the beboppers before. In This Korner (Concord Jazz, 1978) documents one key configuration: Bobby Watson (alto), David Schnitter (tenor), Valery Ponomarev (trumpet), James Williams (piano), Dennis Irwin (bass). Reflections In Blue (Timeless, 1983) shows continuity with the same core: Watson, Ponomarev, Williams, Irwin.

The early 1980s Marsalis band — Wynton Marsalis (trumpet), Branford Marsalis (alto), Billy Pierce (tenor), Donald Brown (piano), Charles Fambrough (bass) — became another celebrated configuration. Keystone 3 (Concord Jazz, 1982) is the primary document. Album Of The Year (Timeless, 1981) preceded Wynton’s debut solo album and served as a launchpad.

Blue Night (Timeless, 1985) features Terence Blanchard (trumpet), Donald Harrison (alto), Jean Toussaint (tenor), Mulgrew Miller (piano), Lonnie Plaxico (bass).

Final Years (1985–1990)

Blakey continued to lead the Jazz Messengers until shortly before his death from lung cancer on 16 October 1990, five days after his 71st birthday. Late recordings document the late-80s Timeless and HighNote period.

A 1989 compilation Art Blakey (Italy) documents a retrospective session with historical collaborators including Freddie Hubbard, Woody Shaw, Wayne Shorter, Curtis Fuller, Cedar Walton, Reggie Workman, and Stanley Clarke — with Steve Turre contributing trombone on one track. Feel The Wind (Timeless, 1989) is a late duo or small-group document with Freddie Hubbard.

Drumming Style

Blakey’s playing was defined by insistent press rolls, driving ride cymbal work, and a textural use of the hi-hat that served as a forward propulsion mechanism rather than a timekeeper in the conventional sense. His trip to West Africa directly informed his polyrhythmic approach. He was also known for bringing the press roll back into bebop context after it had largely been associated with big-band swing.

Art Blakey & The Jazz Messengers as an Institution

The Jazz Messengers functioned as a finishing school for post-bebop jazz over four decades. Blakey deliberately sought young players, gave them room to develop compositions (not just solos), and pushed them toward leadership. The band’s roster reads as a who’s who of modern jazz.
