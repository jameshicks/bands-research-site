Peter Brötzmann

Peter Brötzmann

Aug 24, 202611 min read

Peter Brötzmann

Discogs ID: 120328 Born: 6 March 1941, Remscheid, Germany Died: 22 June 2023, Wuppertal, Germany Instruments: tenor saxophone, alto saxophone, bass saxophone, tárogató, clarinet (including bass clarinet and E-flat clarinet)

German free jazz saxophonist and clarinetist. One of the primary architects of European free improvisation from the late 1960s onward, and a figure who remained active across six decades until shortly before his death. Father of guitarist Caspar Brötzmann.

Biography

Origins and Visual Art Background

Brötzmann came up not through jazz conservatories but through the visual arts. He trained as a painter and worked as an assistant to Fluxus artist Nam June Paik — an experience that shaped his sense of artistic independence throughout his career. He continued making visual art (woodcuts, paintings, cover designs) in parallel with music for his entire life, frequently designing the covers and artwork for his own releases.

He began playing saxophone in the early 1960s, gravitating toward the collective improvisation forms that were breaking from conventional jazz structures. His early collaborators included pianist Fred Van Hove and drummer Han Bennink — a trio that would record and tour extensively through the late 1960s and 1970s.

Machine Gun and the Foundation of European Free Jazz (1968)

The pivotal document of Brötzmann’s early period — and arguably the single most influential European free jazz recording — is Machine Gun (1968), credited to the Peter Brötzmann Octet and self-released on his own BRÖ label. Recorded in Bremen, it featured a double-drummer, double-bass lineup of maximum density:

The album was self-financed and self-distributed, establishing from the outset Brötzmann’s insistence on operating outside commercial structures. Brötzmann described its sound as expressing the “anger and fears” of the time; its ferocity remained a reference point for decades of free noise, avant-metal, and noise rock. The BRÖ label was revived sporadically — notably for the 2013 release A Fish Stinks From The Head.

Engineer on Machine Gun: Günther Zipelius.

FMP Era: Berlin and the European Free Jazz Community (1970s–early 1990s)

Brötzmann’s primary label through the 1970s and most of the 1980s and beyond was Free Music Production (FMP), the Berlin-based independent co-run by producer Jost Gebers (Discogs 293826). FMP was the documentary backbone of European free improvisation, and Brötzmann was arguably its central artist — releasing well over a dozen albums there across two decades. Key FMP titles include:

Gebers appears as the most frequent collaborator in the Discogs database (48 shared credits) — a figure both as producer and as the infrastructure behind FMP’s documentation project.

During this period Brötzmann also participated in major collective formations including the Globe Unity Orchestra (Discogs 284214), Alexander von Schlippenbach’s large ensemble, and the ICP Orchestra (Discogs 1669446), Misha Mengelberg’s Amsterdam-based ensemble. He recorded with Misha Mengelberg, Han Bennink, Paul Rutherford (2), Derek Bailey, Evan Parker, Alexander von Schlippenbach, and many others in the overlapping circles of the European free improvisation network.

Regular trio/duo partners through the 1970s and 1980s include Fred Van Hove, Han Bennink (the trio Bennink, Discogs 1467671), and Harry Miller.

Last Exit (1986–1994)

In 1986 Brötzmann co-founded Last Exit with Bill Laswell (bass), Sonny Sharrock (guitar), and Ronald Shannon Jackson (drums), relocating his European free jazz into American no wave/funk-noise territory. The band was a genuine collision rather than a compromise — Laswell’s 6-string electric bass, Sharrock’s volume, and Jackson’s polyrhythmic drumming gave Brötzmann’s saxophone an entirely different foil than his European duo partners.

The band wound down in the early 1990s after Sharrock’s death in 1994. Posthumous archival releases (Allentown, Moers, both 2021) appeared on Bill Laswell’s self-released label.

The Chicago Turn: Okka Disk and the Tentet (1990s–2000s)

Brötzmann’s increasing engagement with Chicago’s free jazz scene in the 1990s produced one of his richest later periods. His principal label through this phase was Okka Disk (Discogs 37603, Chicago), run by Bruno Johnson and John Corbett.

The Peter Brötzmann Chicago Tentet (Discogs 393176) became the main vehicle — a large ensemble that drew on both European and American musicians and documented Brötzmann’s Chicago-network connections. Key personnel from the Stone / Water (2000, Okka Disk) credit sheet:

The Tentet’s Okka Disk releases: 1 / 2 / 3 (1998), Stone / Water (2000), Broken English (2002), Be Music, Night (2005).

The Brötzmann / Bennink duo (Discogs 1753702) continued alongside these larger formations, as did the Brötzmann/Drake Duo (Discogs 12776612) with Hamid Drake, which yielded The Dried Rat-Dog (1995, Okka Disk) — Brötzmann on tárogató, E-flat clarinet, alto and tenor saxophone; Drake on frame drum, tabla, drums. Cover art, booklet concept, and co-production by Brötzmann.

Parallel duo/trio work during this period reached to Japan (Toshinori Kondo, Shoji Hano) and to Chicago improvisers including Joe McPhee, William Parker, Rashied Ali, and Andrew Cyrille.

The Atavistic label (Discogs 16424) published The Inexplicable Flyswatter: Works On Paper 1959–1964 (2003) — a document of Brötzmann’s visual art practice — as well as full-length recordings by Full Blast (2) (Black Hole, 2009).

Die Like a Dog Quartet and FMP Continuity

The Die Like a dog quartet (Discogs 598165) formed in the early 1990s and was dedicated to the music of Albert Ayler. Its debut Die Like A Dog (Fragments Of Music, Life And Death Of Albert Ayler) appeared on FMP in 1994. The complete FMP recordings were later collected by Jazzwerkstatt (The Complete FMP Recordings, 2007). A further document appeared decades later: Fragments Of Music, Life And Death Of Albert Ayler (2024, CIEN FUEGOS).

Full Blast (2) (2006–2020)

Full Blast (2) (Discogs 1483843) was a power trio formed with Swiss musicians Marino Pliakas (electric bass) and Michael Wertmüller (drums). The lineup was an explicit bridge to rock-noise territory, with Wertmüller also handling mastering on the debut.

Hairy Bones

Hairy Bones (Discogs 2220949) paired Brötzmann with Toshinori Kondo (electric trumpet), Massimo Pupillo (electric bass, of Zu), and Paal Nilssen-Love (drums). The self-titled album (2009, Okka Disk) was produced by Bruno Johnson and Paal Nilssen-Love, mixed by Lou Mallozzi. Brötzmann played alto saxophone, tenor saxophone, bass clarinet, and tárogató, and again designed the artwork.

The I Surrender Dear solo (2019) is notable for Brötzmann as sole credited musician and designer — an austere late-career document. Recorded and engineered by Martin Siewert (Discogs 121023), who appears as the most-credited collaborator (24 shared releases) after Jost Gebers and Paal Nilssen-Love.

Other late collaborations: Konstrukt (Turkish free jazz ensemble, 9 shared releases), Keiji Haino (duo, Discogs 11749376), Black Bombaim (Portuguese band, Black Bombaim & Peter Brötzmann, 2016), and Lasse Marhaug (7 shared releases).

A 2016 collaboration album with Black Bombaim was released on Lovers & Lollypops and Shhpuma.

As Visual Artist

Brötzmann’s visual art practice was inseparable from his music. He designed cover art for dozens of his own releases across his career — from the FMP era through Okka Disk and Trost — as well as for Last Exit releases (Köln, Iron Path calligraphy). He worked in woodcuts. Atavistic published The Inexplicable Flyswatter: Works On Paper 1959–1964 (2003) as a dedicated document of his visual practice.
