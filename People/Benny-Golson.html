Benny Golson

Discogs ID: 185752 Real name: Bennie Golson Born: January 25, 1929, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania Died: September 21, 2024, Manhattan, New York

American bebop / hard bop jazz tenor saxophonist, composer, and arranger. One of the most consequential composer-performers in postwar jazz, responsible for at least eight widely-performed jazz standards, a founding co-leader of The Jazztet, a working sideman in every major hard bop band of the 1950s, and a prolific arranger for film and television during a twelve-year hiatus from performing.

Biography

Philadelphia Origins and Early Collaborators (1929–1952)

Golson grew up in Philadelphia in a cohort that amounted to a remarkable concentration of future jazz talent. While still in high school he played alongside contemporaries who would become major figures: Philly Joe Jones, John Coltrane, Jimmy Heath, Red Garland, Percy Heath, and Lee Morgan. The city’s jazz ecosystem in this period — Sunday jam sessions, ballrooms, after-hours clubs — gave Golson an early foundation in hard swing and emerging bebop.

After graduating from Howard University, Golson joined Bullmoose Jackson’s rhythm and blues band. The pianist in that band was Tadd Dameron, whom Golson came to regard as the most important single influence on his compositional voice. Dameron’s melodic approach to harmonic construction — lush, song-oriented, rooted in bebop but never purely an exercise in velocity — shaped what would become Golson’s signature as a writer.

Sideman Years and the Standards (1953–1958)

From 1953, Golson worked through a series of major bands in quick succession:

  • Tadd Dameron (as bandleader — Study In Dameronia, Prestige, 1953)
  • Lionel Hampton
  • Johnny Hodges
  • Earl Bostic
  • Dizzy Gillespie (1956–1958) — appearing on the Verve big-band records At Newport (1957), Birks’ Works (1958), and Dizzy In Greece (1957), among others. He also served as arranger on some Gillespie material.
  • Art Blakey & The Jazz Messengers (1958–1959) — briefly served as the Messengers’ music director; recorded on the landmark Blue Note Moanin’ (1958)

During this period Golson composed the body of work that would define his legacy. In 1956, after trumpeter Clifford Brown died in a car accident, Golson wrote “I Remember Clifford” as a tribute — his most enduring ballad composition. Around the same period he wrote “Stablemates,” “Killer Joe,” “Whisper Not,” “Along Came Betty,” and “Are You Real?” Each became a standard. The compositions share a quality of melodic logic that crosses the boundary between hard bop and popular song structure — identifiable head lines with enough harmonic interest to sustain improvisation.

The 1958 album Benny Golson’s New York Scene (Contemporary Records) captured his working style in a sextet format with Art Farmer on trumpet, Paul Chambers on bass, Charlie Persip on drums, Wynton Kelly on piano, and a front-line expansion on several tracks adding Gigi Gryce (alto), Sahib Shihab (baritone), and Julius Watkins (French horn). Supervised by Nat Hentoff. It was followed by a prolific run of leader sessions on New Jazz / Prestige: Groovin’ With Golson (1959), Gone With Golson (1960), and Gettin’ With It (1960), as well as The Other Side Of Benny Golson (Riverside, 1959) and Winchester Special (New Jazz, 1959, co-led with vibraphonist Lem Winchester).

The Jazztet (1959–1962)

In 1959, Golson co-founded The Jazztet with flugelhorn and trumpet player Art Farmer. The group immediately signed to Argo (6) and recorded Meet The Jazztet (1960) — their debut album, with recording supervision by Jack Tracy and production by Kay Norton. The lineup featured McCoy Tyner on piano, Curtis Fuller on trombone, Addison Farmer on bass, and Lex Humphries on drums. This album is notable as one of Tyner’s earliest recorded appearances; he would go on to join Coltrane’s classic quartet shortly after.

The Jazztet at Argo recorded a string of significant albums:

  • Meet The Jazztet (1960) — debut; Tracy supervisor, Tyner on piano
  • Big City Sounds (1961) — Cedar Walton on piano, Albert Heath on drums
  • The Jazztet And John Lewis (1961) — co-led with John Lewis; Tom McIntosh on trombone
  • Take A Number From 1 To 10 (1961)
  • At Birdhouse (1961 — Argo)
  • Turning Point (Mercury, 1963) — late-period Jazztet with Wynton Kelly, Paul Chambers, Jimmy Cobb; produced by Tracy

Jack Tracy supervised or produced every Argo Jazztet session. The comprehensive retrospective The Complete Argo / Mercury Art Farmer / Benny Golson / Jazztet Sessions (Mosaic Records, 2004) presents the full run with Tracy and Kay Norton credited as co-producers of the original sessions, and Michael Cuscuna as producer for the release.

The Jazztet disbanded in 1962 — a pressingly productive three years that generated several of Golson’s best-documented recordings and cemented the Farmer–Golson front line as one of the most coherent in mainstream jazz.

Studio, Film, and Television Work (1962–1975)

After the Jazztet’s first run ended, Golson largely stepped away from performing jazz and spent roughly twelve years doing studio and orchestral work for film and television. During this period he composed and arranged music for:

  • Ironside (NBC drama series)
  • Room 222 (ABC drama series)
  • M*A*S*H (CBS series — one of his highest-profile TV credits)
  • Mission: Impossible (CBS series)

This was a commercially substantial career pivot. Discogs documents it indirectly through a dense cluster of 1964–1972 arranger and conductor credits: sessions for Prestige, Verve, MGM Records, Columbia, and others in which Golson contributed orchestral writing and conducting for vocalists including Shirley Scott, Nina Simone (string arrangements), Cass Elliot, Lou Rawls, and others. He also contributed the instrumental orchestrations for the Lady Sings The Blues film soundtrack (Motown, 1972) and other commercial projects.

In 1977, Columbia released Killer Joe — a full electric/pop-jazz crossover album produced by Golson himself and co-produced with Bobby Martin. It featured a Los Angeles studio band including Ray Parker Jr., James Gadson, Stanley Clarke, Frank Rosolino, and a large brass ensemble. The album signaled Golson’s return to recording under his own name in a different mode — commercial rather than straight jazz.

Return to Jazz (1977–2024)

From the mid-1970s onward, Golson returned to performing and recording jazz as his primary activity. The catalogue through the late 1980s–2000s is substantial:

  • Blues On Down (Milestone, 1978) — straight-ahead quartet
  • California Message (Baystate, 1981) — Japanese market recording
  • This Is For You, John (Baystate, 1983) — tribute to John Coltrane, with Pharoah Sanders and Jack DeJohnette
  • Time Speaks – Dedicated To The Memory Of Clifford Brown (Baystate, 1983) — with Woody Shaw, Cecil McBee, Kenny Barron
  • Jazztet Reunion (1983): Golson revived the Jazztet with Art Farmer. The Jazztet - Real Time (Contemporary, 1988)
  • Stardust (Denon, 1987) — Japanese session with Mulgrew Miller
  • I Remember Miles (Alfa Jazz, 1993) — arranged tribute with Mulgrew Miller
  • Tenor Legacy (Keystone, 1996) — with Branford Marsalis and Harold Ashby
  • One Day, Forever (Arkadia Jazz, 2001) — ensemble record with strings
  • Terminal 1 (Concord Jazz, 2004) — late-career quartet
  • New Times, New ‘Tet (Concord, 2008) — new Jazztet version
  • Horizon Ahead (HighNote, 2016) — penultimate major studio release
  • European Tour 2019 (Blau Records, 2020) — live document, his final decade of touring

In 1995 Golson received the NEA Jazz Masters Award. He received the Mellon Living Legend Legacy Award in 2007, and was inducted into the International Academy of Jazz Hall of Fame in 2009. He continued to perform and record into his 90s; his last documented appearance on Discogs is Live In Chester (2024). He died on September 21, 2024 in Manhattan.
