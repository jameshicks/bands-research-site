Ravi Shankar

Indian sitarist, composer, and teacher. Born Robindro Shaunkor Chowdhury, 7 April 1920, Varanasi, United Provinces, British India; died 11 December 2012, San Diego, California. Discogs artist ID: 299241.

One of the most influential figures in bringing Indian classical music to Western audiences throughout the second half of the 20th century. His recordings, tours, and teaching work transformed Western perceptions of Indian music — and, by extension, shaped a generation of rock and classical musicians who absorbed his influence directly. Taught George Harrison sitar from 1966 onward, an association that ran for decades and produced multiple records and concert events. Organized the Concert for Bangladesh (1971) with Harrison — one of the earliest large-scale rock charity concerts. Released records on Dark Horse Records (Harrison’s label) from 1974. Collaborated with violinist Yehudi Menuhin on the landmark West Meets East series (1967–1976) and with composer Philip Glass on Passages (1990). His daughter Anoushka Shankar is a sitarist who has continued his tradition; his other daughter Norah Jones is the jazz/pop singer-songwriter.

Early Career and International Breakthrough (1940s–1960s)

Shankar trained under the sitar master Allauddin Khan — father of his eventual longtime collaborator Ali Akbar Khan — and eventually became music director of All India Radio in the late 1940s. His first Indian recordings date to 1948 on His Master’s Voice. International recognition came in the mid-1950s: Music Of India (Three Classical Rāgas) (His Master’s Voice, 1956, UK) was among his first releases aimed at Western audiences.

By the late 1950s and early 1960s he was recording for World Pacific Records in the US — a jazz-adjacent imprint associated with Pacific Jazz — including India’s Master Musician (1959), Improvisations & Theme From Pather Panchali (1962, with jazz players), and the film score to Satyajit Ray’s trilogy. The Monterey Pop Festival performance in 1967 — documented on Ravi Shankar At The Monterey International Pop Festival (Liberty, 1967; with Alla Rakha on tabla) — was a pivotal Western live crossover moment.

The Beatles / George Harrison Connection (1966–2012)

Shankar taught George Harrison sitar in 1966; the association shaped Harrison’s compositional voice during the Beatles’ psychedelic period and led to a friendship that lasted until Harrison’s death in 2001. Their documented collaborations on record:

  • Joi Bangla / Oh Bhaugowan (Apple Records, 1971) — single produced by Harrison; Alla Rakha and Ali Akbar Khan on the session
  • Raga (Apple Records, 1971) — soundtrack album; produced by Harrison
  • In Concert 1972 (Apple Records, 1973) — live double album with Ali Akbar Khan and Alla Rakha; co-edited/mixed by Harrison and Zakir Hussain
  • The Concert For Bangladesh (Apple/Paramount, 1993 reissue) — Shankar opened the event with a classical set; Harrison headlined the rock half
  • I Am Missing You (Dark Horse Records, 1974) — single; produced by Harrison
  • Ravi Shankar’s Music Festival From India (Dark Horse Records, 1976) — LP; produced by Harrison
  • Chants Of India (Angel/Dark Horse Records, 1997) — produced by Harrison; one of their last collaborations
  • In Celebration Highlights (Dark Horse Records, 1996) — produced by Harrison
  • Collaborations (Dark Horse Records/Rhino, 2010) — posthumous compilation of their recorded work together; overseen by Shankar and reissue-produced by Anoushka Shankar

Western Classical Crossover (1967–1982)

Shankar pursued parallel crossover work with European classical musicians. The West Meets East series with violinist Yehudi Menuhin (EMI/Angel, 1967; Album 2, 1968; Improvisations – West Meets East Album 3, 1976) was a Grammy-winning exchange between Indian raga and European violin sonata tradition — Menuhin’s sister Hephzibah Menuhin played piano on the Enesco pieces.

Shankar: Concerto For Sitar & Orchestra (Angel Records, 1971), co-credited with conductor André Previn and the London Symphony Orchestra, was his first formal sitar concerto commissioned for Western orchestra.

Gandhi / Music From The Original Motion Picture Soundtrack (RCA, 1982), co-composed with George Fenton, won the Academy Award for Best Original Score. The session included Alla Rakha on tabla, Ali Akbar Khan’s son Aashish Khan on sarod, and Lakshmi Shankar on vocals.

Private Music / Electronic Synthesis Era (1987–1990)

In the late 1980s, under the aegis of Private Music (Peter Baumann’s label), Shankar experimented with electronic synthesis. Tana Mana (Private Music, 1987; credited as “The Ravi Shankar Project”) used synthesizers, electronic drums, and a fusion ensemble — with George Harrison contributing auto-harp and synthesizer, Al Kooper on guitar, Patrick O’Hearn on bass, and Ray Cooper on marimba; produced by Peter Baumann and Frank Serafine.

Inside The Kremlin (Private Music, 1988) — a performance at the Moscow Kremlin — featured the Moscow Chamber Orchestra, Soviet choirs, and produced by Kurt Munkacsi (Glass’s long-time engineer/producer). It documented one of the earliest Western–Soviet classical crossover events in the Gorbachev era.

Passages (Private Music, 1990) — with Philip Glass as co-primary artist — was a landmark cross-cultural composition: two suites, each composer providing material for the other to arrange. Produced by Shankar, Glass, and Kurt Munkacsi.
