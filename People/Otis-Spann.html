Otis Spann

Chicago blues pianist and vocalist. Born 21 March 1930, Jackson, Mississippi; died 24 April 1970, Chicago, Illinois. Discogs artist ID: 322878.

Overview

Otis Spann was the most important blues pianist of the postwar Chicago school and one of the most recorded session musicians in the Chess Records catalog. He joined Muddy Waters’s band in the early 1950s and remained its pianist until his death, making him the rhythmic anchor of the most celebrated blues group of the era. His parallel solo career — running from 1954 through 1969 — documented his voice and compositional instincts separately from his sideman work, across labels including Checker, Candid, Decca, Stateside, Vanguard, Bluesway, and Blue Horizon.

Spann died of liver cancer in April 1970, aged 40, having recorded prolifically into the final year of his life. His connection to the British blues revival — through the 1963 American Folk Blues Festival tour and a 1969 session with Peter Green’s Fleetwood Mac — makes him a direct point of transfer between Chicago blues and the UK rock lineage that fed into post-punk and the wider vault.

Biography

Early Career and Chess Records (1950s)

Spann arrived in Chicago around 1951 and quickly entered the Chess orbit. His first release under his own name was the Checker single “It Must Have Been The Devil / Five Spot” (1954, Checker label — a Chess subsidiary), on which he also takes writing credits. By this point he was already embedded in Muddy Waters’s live band, a position documented on Muddy Waters At Newport 1960 (Chess), where he takes piano throughout and a vocal on side B.

His Chess session work across the 1950s and early 1960s is credited on dozens of releases for Muddy Waters, Howlin’ Wolf, Little Walter, Sonny Boy Williamson (2), Bo Diddley, Chuck Berry, Buddy Guy, and others. Discogs credits him on releases for all the major Chess/Checker/Cadet roster names, though many of his earliest credits appear as “Piano [Uncredited]” — the label did not always document session personnel in the 1950s.

First Solo Albums (1961–1965)

Otis Spann Is The Blues (1961, Candid) was his first full solo LP. Produced by Nat Hentoff and engineered by George Piros, the session is spare: Spann on piano and vocals, with Robert Lockwood Jr. on guitar. It remains his most intimate document.

The Blues Of Otis Spann (1964, Decca UK) was recorded in London during or around his UK tour appearances, produced by Mike Vernon and engineered by Gus Dudgeon. The band included Ransom Knowling on bass and Willie Smith on drums, with a guitarist credited as “Brother.” Mike Vernon’s involvement begins here — he would remain a key production figure for Spann’s British-linked recordings.

Stirs Me Up / See See Rider (both 1964, Decca UK / Storyville) document recordings from the same European period. The Storyville release Portrait In Blues Vol. 3 (1964, France) confirmed his European profile.

The Blues Never Die! (1965, Stateside UK) was produced by Samuel B. Charters and featured James Cotton on harmonica — Cotton was a fellow Muddy Waters band member, and their shared history is audible in the session.

American Folk Blues Festival 1963

Spann participated in the 1963 American Folk Blues Festival European tour, documented on the album American Folk Blues Festival ‘63 (released 1981 on L+R Records). His piano appears on multiple tracks alongside Willie Dixon on bass, Sonny Boy Williamson (2) on harp, Muddy Waters on guitar and vocals, Memphis Slim, Big Joe Williams, and Victoria Spivey. The festival was the primary mechanism by which Chicago blues musicians reached British audiences in the early 1960s and directly influenced the British blues movement.

Chicago Sessions (1966–1968)

Otis Spann’s Chicago Blues (1966, Testament Records) was produced by Pete Welding and features James Cotton on harmonica, Johnny Young on guitar, and S.P. Leary on drums.

The Blues Is Where It’s At (1967, Bluesway) is notable for featuring Muddy Waters on guitar — a reversal of their usual employer/employee arrangement. Also on the session: Luther Johnson and Sammy Lawhorn on guitars, Mac Arnold on bass, and Francis Clay on drums. Produced by Bob Thiele.

The Bottom Of The Blues (1968, Bluesway) again features Muddy Waters on guitar, alongside Luther Johnson, Sammy Lawhorn, and George “Mojo” Buford on harmonica. Lucille Spann (Otis’s wife) contributes backing vocals. Produced by Bob Thiele.

Can’t Do Me No Good (1968, Blue Horizon UK) — produced by Mike Vernon; personnel not fully documented in Discogs credits.

Blue Horizon / Fleetwood Mac Sessions (1969)

The most significant of Spann’s late recordings, and the most consequential for the vault’s lineage, are the Blue Horizon sessions organized in Chicago in 1969, bringing Spann together with Peter Green’s Fleetwood Mac.

The Biggest Thing Since Colossus (1969, Blue Horizon) is credited to “Otis Spann with Fleetwood Mac.” Personnel: Peter Green (2) and Danny Kirwan on guitars, John McVie on bass, S.P. Leary on drums. Produced by Mike Vernon.

Walkin’ (1969, Blue Horizon Netherlands) is similarly credited “Otis Spann with Fleetwood Mac,” produced by Mike Vernon.

Blues Jam At Chess (1969, Blue Horizon UK) / Blues Jam In Chicago — Volume One and Volume Two (both issued on Blue Horizon US, 1969–1970) document a larger session at the Chess studios in Chicago. The full ensemble: Fleetwood Mac, Otis Spann, Willie Dixon, Walter Horton, J.T. Brown, Buddy Guy, David “Honeyboy” Edwards, and S.P. Leary. Co-produced by Marshall Chess and Mike Vernon; recorded by Stu Black. The session was organized with Willie Dixon and Neil Slaven as co-coordinators. This is the closest the British blues revival came to the source — Fleetwood Mac at peak Peter Green-era power, recorded at Chess, with the architects of the style.

Spann also appeared on the Blue Horizon album Last Night’s Dream (1969) by Johnny Shines, playing piano on track B1, alongside Willie Dixon on bass and Walter Horton on harmonica.

Cracked Spanner Head and London (1969)

Cracked Spanner Head (1969, Deram UK) was recorded in London with a British ensemble: Ransom Knowling on bass, Willie Smith on drums, Bud Beadle on baritone sax, Steve Gregory on tenor sax, and a guitarist credited as Spit James, with McKinley Morganfield (Muddy Waters, uncredited by name in role) on guitar. Engineered by Gus Dudgeon and Roy Thomas Baker. Produced by Mike Vernon. Spann wrote all but one track.

Fathers and Sons (1969)

Fathers and Sons (1969, Chess) is Muddy Waters’s celebrated double album bringing together Chicago blues originators and younger white American blues-rock musicians. Spann plays piano throughout. The full personnel:

Vanguard and Other Late Solo Work (1969–1970)

Cryin’ Time (1969, Vanguard) was produced by Samuel B. Charters, with Luther Johnson on guitar, Barry Melton (of Country Joe and the Fish) on lead guitar, and Lucille Spann on backing vocals on two tracks.

The Biggest Thing Since Colossus, Walkin’, Cracked Spanner Head, and Cryin’ Time all appeared in 1969, making it Spann’s most prolific solo year as a recording artist.

Hungry Country Girl (1970, Blue Horizon) and Blues Jam In Chicago — Volume Two (1970, Blue Horizon) appeared posthumously in the year of his death.

Last Call — Live At The Boston Tea Party — April 2nd, 1970 (released 2000, Mr. Cat Music) documents his final known live recording, just weeks before his death.

Open Questions

  1. Does the Fleetwood Mac credit on The Biggest Thing Since Colossus include Mick Fleetwood on drums, or is S.P. Leary (credited by name) the only drummer? Discogs credits S.P. Leary by name alongside “Fleetwood Mac” as entity — is this a full band appearance or a reduced lineup?
  2. The 1954 Checker single “It Must Have Been The Devil / Five Spot” — is this the very first Spann studio recording, or are there undocumented Chess/Checker sessions predating it?
  3. Cracked Spanner Head credits “McKinley Morganfield” on guitar — is this Muddy Waters appearing uncredited under his birth name, or a different McKinley Morganfield? The session was recorded in London in 1969.
  4. The Discogs credit for American Folk Blues Festival ‘63 (the LP, released 1981) appears to be a compilation of festival recordings. Was there a contemporaneous LP or EP release in Europe in 1963–1964?
  5. Can’t Do Me No Good (1968, Blue Horizon) lists only Mike Vernon as producer — no session musicians are credited in Discogs. Who played on this record?
  6. Spann is listed as a member of “The Johnny Shines Blues Band” (Discogs group ID: 519450) — was this a formal recording unit or a live band? The Discogs group entry should clarify.
  7. The Cryin’ Time (Vanguard, 1969) session features Barry Melton of Country Joe and the Fish on lead guitar. Was this recorded in San Francisco or Chicago, and was it part of a Vanguard push to market Spann to a rock audience?
  8. What Chess releases specifically carry Spann’s earliest uncredited piano work in the 1952–1954 period? The Discogs record shows his first credited Chess appearance is the 1960 Newport album — earlier Chess session work almost certainly exists but may not be credited in the DB.
  9. Fathers and Sons does not credit Buddy Miles on drums, despite him being cited in some historical accounts of the session. Discogs credits Sam Lay only. Is Buddy Miles’s credit on some pressings, or is it a misattribution?
  10. The 1969 Blue Horizon sessions (Blues Jam at Chess, Walkin’, The Biggest Thing Since Colossus) — were all three recorded on the same dates in Chicago, or across separate sessions? Mike Vernon and Marshall Chess both produced the Chess session recordings, but The Biggest Thing Since Colossus and Walkin’ appear to be separate studio dates.
