Brian McMahan

Brian McMahan

Oct 19, 202611 min read

Brian McMahan

American guitarist and vocalist. Louisville, Kentucky. Discogs ID: 254076.

Best known as lead vocalist and guitarist of Slint and as the driving force behind The For Carnation. One of the central figures of the Louisville, Kentucky underground scene of the mid-1980s through 1990s, with credits spanning hardcore punk, post-rock, and experimental music.

Name variations on Discogs: B McMahon, B. McMahan, Brian MacMahan, Brian Scott / McMahan.

Biography

Louisville Beginnings — Squirrel Bait (1983–1988)

McMahan grew up in Louisville and as a teenager became part of the city’s small but tightly connected hardcore underground. He was a founding member and guitarist of Squirrel Bait, a band that also included Britt Walford (drums), David Grubbs (guitar), Peter Searcy (vocals), Clark Johnson (bass), Ben Daughtrey (drums on select tracks), and Richard Schuler. Squirrel Bait recorded two albums for Homestead Records: the self-titled debut EP (1985) and Skag Heaven (1986). Both were produced by the band with engineer Howie Gano.

Squirrel Bait were notable as one of the Louisville hardcore acts that pushed the form toward more complex arrangements and dynamics, a sensibility that would carry forward into Slint. David Grubbs departed to eventually form Bastro and later Gastr Del Sol; McMahan and Walford moved on to Slint.

King Kong (3) — Louisville Experimental Collective (c.1988–1991)

Parallel to Slint’s early activity, McMahan was a member of King Kong (3), a loose Louisville collective that also included Walford, David Pajo, Ethan Buckler, Grubbs, and numerous others (at various points: Amy Partin Ritchie, Brett Holsclaw, Darren Rappa, Peter Townsend, Ray Rizzo, Richard Schuler, Suki Anderson, Todd Hildreth, Willy MacLean). King Kong released Movie Star in 1989 (self-released), on which McMahan is credited as Performer / Guitar / Vocals.

Slint (1986–1994; reunions 2005, 2007, 2014)

McMahan co-founded Slint in Louisville in 1986 with Walford; they were soon joined by Pajo on guitar and Buckler on bass. All were teenagers at the band’s formation. Slint developed a sound built around extreme dynamic contrast, odd time signatures, and largely spoken-word vocal delivery — a marked departure from the more conventional hardcore of Squirrel Bait.

Tweez (1989) — Slint’s debut, released on Jennifer Hartman Records (a Louisville imprint), recorded by “Some Fuckin’ Derd Niffer” (a pseudonym of Steve Albini). Lineup: McMahan (guitar, vocals), Pajo (guitar), Buckler (bass), Walford (drums). Track titles are first names of band members’ parents. Shortly after recording, Buckler was replaced by Todd Brashear.

Spiderland (1991) — Recorded at River North Studios, Chicago, engineered by Brian Paulson. Lineup: McMahan (electric guitar, vocals — uncredited on original sleeve), Pajo (guitar), Brashear (bass), Walford (drums, vocals). Cover photograph by Will Oldham. Released on Touch And Go (T&G LP#64). Widely cited as a foundational post-rock document. The band effectively dissolved upon its completion.

Untitled 10” (1994) — Two tracks released posthumously on Touch And Go (TG132), believed to date from sessions around the Spiderland period.

Reunions — The band reformed for live dates in 2005 and 2007 (the 2007 performances included a new piece, “King’s Approach”). The 2014 reunion was the most extensive, tied to the release of a Spiderland box set on Touch And Go that included a Bonus Tracks LP of outtakes and demos, and a documentary film.

2019 — McMahan performed on the Record Store Day release Breadcrumb Trail / Good Morning, Captain (Touch And Go TG366LP), credited as Electric Guitar and Lead Vocals.

The For Carnation (1995–2000)

After Slint’s dissolution McMahan launched The For Carnation, his primary post-Slint project. The band’s lineup shifted across its releases but consistently involved McMahan on guitar, keyboards, and vocals, and drew on the same Louisville/Chicago experimental network.

Fight Songs (1995, Matador) — The debut EP. Full lineup: McMahan (guitar, vocals, programming, written-by), David Pajo (guitar, written-by), Douglas McCombs (bass, written-by, as “Doug McCombs”), John Herndon (drums, written-by), with string arrangements performed by Andrew Bonacci and the London Symphony Orchestra. Engineered by Grant Barger; mastered by Greg Calbi.

Marshmallows (1996, Matador) — EP. Expanded performer lineup: McMahan, McCombs, Herndon, Pajo, Brad Wood, Michael McMahan (Brian’s brother), Grant Barger, John Weiss, Tim Ruth. String arrangements by Gordon Jenkins And His Orchestra.

Promised Works (1997, Runt) — Compilation / retrospective gathering earlier recorded material. Two distinct performer groupings: tracks 1–3 feature McMahan, Pajo, McCombs, Herndon, and Andrew Bonacci; tracks 4–9 feature McMahan, Michael McMahan, John Weiss, and Tim Ruth. Engineers: Grant Barger and Brad Wood.

The For Carnation (2000, Domino / Touch And Go) — The self-titled full-length, the band’s definitive statement. Lineup: McMahan (guitar, keyboards, vocals), Michael McMahan (guitar, space echo effects), Bobb Bruno (guitar, keyboards, sampler), Todd Cook (bass), Steve Goodfriend (drums). Engineered by John McEntire, Rod Cervera, and Tom Grimley.

Maurice (51) and Later Activity

McMahan was also a member of Maurice (51) (Discogs ID: 4252570), a project shared with several Louisville orbit musicians including Walford and Pajo.

In 2005 he appeared as an interviewee on Looking For A Thrill: An Anthology Of Inspiration (Thrill Jockey), a compilation documenting the extended Thrill Jockey / Chicago scene. He contributed sequencing to Will Oldham’s “Highway Songs” (Drag City, 2016) and has appeared in engineering and mixing capacities on scattered releases (see credits table below).

Open Questions

Notes (web) — McMahan’s hospitalization after Spiderland

Question investigated: Is there a confirmed primary source (interview, press account, or documentary) for Brian McMahan checking into a mental hospital after the Spiderland sessions, beyond the rumour as reported by Wikipedia?

Finding: Partially confirmed via documentary, but McMahan has never spoken about it directly on record.

What the sources say

Wikipedia — Slint article states the claim as a rumour: “The day after Spiderland’s recording session ended, McMahan was rumored to have checked himself into a mental hospital where he was diagnosed with depression, ending the band.” No citation is given. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slint, as of 2026-08-14)

Wikipedia — Spiderland article goes further and actually cites a Britt Walford denial: “Rumors circulated that at least one member of Slint had checked into a psychiatric hospital. Walford later said that there was no truth to such claims, although the band was ‘definitely trying to be serious about things, pretty intense, which made recording the album kinda stressful.‘” (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiderland, as of 2026-08-14) — this is the strongest counter-evidence found.

Grantland / Alex Pappademas (May 1, 2014) — writing about the Breadcrumb Trail documentary — states as reportorial fact (not rumour): “In 1990, shortly before Slint went into the studio to record Spiderland, McMahan was struck by an oncoming car while helping a stranded motorist; shortly after the record was finished, he checked himself into a psychiatric hospital.” Pappademas then adds: “Bangs doesn’t push McMahan to talk about the accident or the reasons for his hospital stay, so if you want to — if you need to — you can assume there’s another story we’re not hearing. The film is inconclusive in a way that safeguards the myth; it offers no answers, only alibis.” (https://grantland.com/features/slint-indie-rock-spiderland-breadcrumb-trail-lance-bangs/) This treats the hospitalization as established in the documentary but notes that McMahan does not explain it on camera.

Drowned in Sound / Robert Cooke (March 6, 2014) — a phone interview with McMahan himself, conducted around the 2014 reunion — notes that McMahan “checked himself into a hospital shortly after recording Spiderland” and states plainly: “This is never expanded on, either in the film or during our phone call.” McMahan declined to elaborate when the topic arose. This is significant: McMahan neither confirmed nor denied it in direct interview, but did not correct the premise. (https://drownedinsound.com/in_depth/4147485-the-people-of-louisville-are-crazy---dis-meets-slint-part-one)

The Skinny interview (David Pajo and Brian McMahan) — does not address McMahan’s hospitalization. The only mental health reference is Pajo discussing other Louisville friends receiving government disability payments for mental illness. McMahan does not speak to his own situation. (https://www.theskinny.co.uk/music/interviews/quiet-revolution-slints-david-pajo-and-brian-mcmahan-revisit-spiderland)

Assessment

The claim does not have a clean primary-source confirmation. The clearest evidence chain is:

  1. The Breadcrumb Trail documentary (dir. Lance Bangs, 2014) appears to present the car accident and subsequent hospitalization as established facts — multiple reviewers writing about the film treat them as confirmed rather than rumoured — but director Bangs does not press McMahan on the hospital stay, and McMahan does not speak about it on camera.
  2. McMahan’s silence when asked directly (Drowned in Sound, 2014) is consistent with the event having occurred, but is not a confirmation.
  3. The Spiderland Wikipedia article contains a Walford denial of “rumors that at least one member had checked into a psychiatric hospital” — the source for that denial is cited (ref. 15 in the Wikipedia article) but the actual interview/source behind it was not located in this research pass.

Conflict with earlier vault note: The vault’s ## Open Questions comment cites Wikipedia (Slint article) as reporting the hospitalization as a rumour. The Spiderland article goes further and includes a Walford denial. These two Wikipedia articles are themselves in tension: one frames it as unverified rumour, the other cites Walford contradicting it. Neither cites a primary source confirming it as fact.

Bottom line: The Breadcrumb Trail documentary is the closest thing to a reliable secondary-source confirmation — it presents the event as fact and multiple journalists covering the film accept it without caveat — but McMahan has never confirmed it in his own words in any interview located. The Walford denial (cited in the Spiderland Wikipedia article) is the main counter-evidence and deserves further source-tracing.

Research Queue

  • What is the source for Britt Walford’s denial of psychiatric hospital rumors cited as ref. [15] in the Wikipedia Spiderland article? Identifying the original interview or press account would either confirm or undercut the Breadcrumb Trail framing.
  • Did any contemporary 1991 press coverage (at the time of Spiderland’s release) report McMahan’s hospitalization as fact, or was it strictly retrospective? A search of 1991 Spin, Option, CMJ, or NME archives could resolve this.
