Overview

Dave Eringa is a British producer and engineer who came up through Power Plant Studios before founding his own Electric Daveyland Studios. He has an exceptionally deep working relationship with the Manic Street Preachers — over 300 shared credits with the band and 267 with James Dean Bradfield alone — making him effectively their long-term production partner across multiple decades and labels spanning Columbia, Epic, Virgin, and Parlophone. Beyond the Manics, he has amassed a substantial alternative-rock and Britpop portfolio, with 87 credits alongside Idlewild and regular work beside engineers David Bascombe and Guy Massey. His 1,353 total Discogs credits run from 1990 to the present, covering Alternative Rock, Indie Rock, and Britpop.

Notes (web)

How Eringa became the Manic Street Preachers’ long-term producer

The relationship began around 1990–91 when Eringa was 18 or 19 and working as a tape operator (by his own description, “tea boy”) at Power Plant Studios in Willesden, London. His first job in the industry had been split between Maison Rouge and Power Plant; about nine months in, the Manic Street Preachers arrived to cut their first indie single, “Motown Junk”, with the studio’s in-house engineer and producer Robin Evans. Eringa was just the tape op. The band wanted a Hammond organ part; he knew where the D chord was, so they sent him downstairs to play it. His credit on the single reads “Assistant Engineer and Hammond, Dave Eringa.” — search result snippet quoting Eringa, sourced from KMR Audio interview part 1 (snippet only; page body did not render).

Evans continued to invite Eringa onto subsequent Manics sessions. Eringa describes the initial bond as the band seeing him as a fellow outsider: “Who are these mental buggers!” was his first reaction, and he believes the feeling was mutual — per Repeat Fanzine interview (undated).

By the time the band recorded Generation Terrorists (1992) Eringa had graduated from tape op to playing piano and electric organ on the record. He also toured as the band’s live keyboardist during 1992–93. This put him inside the band’s world as a collaborator rather than a hired studio hand before he had ever led a session for them. — Repeat Fanzine interview; confirmed by search result snippet citing LiquidSonics article (page body did not render).

When the band needed a producer for their second album, they turned to Eringa. He had by then gone freelance; they had him cut a test track, liked the results, did demos, and gave him the full record. Gold Against the Soul (recorded Jan–Mar 1993 at Outside Studios, Checkendon) was the outcome, produced and mixed by Eringa at age 21. It reached the UK top ten. — Headliner Hub profile; Repeat Fanzine interview.

The band’s reasoning, in Eringa’s telling: “they felt he was a peer, and could be a team member.” — Headliner Hub profile (snippet).

For why the relationship has lasted across so many subsequent albums, Eringa articulates a philosophy of subordinating his own aesthetic to the band’s: “I come from the second school — I don’t want to dictate how it should be, I want to capture what’s special about the band.” He calls himself “the fifth or sixth member of the band.” As of the time of the Headliner Hub piece he had been with them 28 years. — Headliner Hub profile; Repeat Fanzine interview.

A technical note on Gold Against the Soul from the Repeat Fanzine interview: Eringa worked entirely on tape, and the album contains no drum edits — all complete takes.
