J.J. Jeczalik

Discogs ID: 65172

British musician and Fairlight CMI specialist. Full name Jonathan Jeczalik. Co-founding member of The Art Of Noise at ZTT Records. 107 computer/electronics credits across ZTT releases per Discogs.

The technical backbone of The Art Of Noise’s sampler-based aesthetic. Credited on Welcome to the Pleasuredome (1984) for “Technician [Software, Hardware], Other [The Fifth Dimension].” Co-writer on all Art of Noise material alongside Anne Dudley, Gary Langan, Paul Morley, and Trevor Horn.

Departed The Art Of Noise circa 1990 alongside Anne Dudley.

Fairlight CMI: Models and Techniques (Web Sources)

Models Used

Jeczalik worked primarily with the Fairlight CMI Series II (released 1982) and later the Series III (released 1985/1986). The Paul Harkins academic chapter (see below) explicitly frames his practice around “the CMI Series II, IIx, and III between 1983 and 1988.” [source: Taylor & Francis chapter abstract, https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781351209960-3/page-art-loop-paul-harkins] [confirmed: Science and Media Museum blog, https://blog.scienceandmediamuseum.org.uk/fairlight-cmi-playlist/]

His first access to a Fairlight came through Geoff Downes, who owned one of the first ten units imported to the UK. The machine arrived with almost no documentation — “the manual was like four pages long.” [source: Super Deluxe Edition interview, https://superdeluxeedition.com/interview/ztt30-j-j-jeczalik-on-fairlights-the-art-of-noise-and-life-at-ztt/]

Series II specifications relevant to his techniques: 8-bit samples; sample memory limited (16 kB per voice / 8 voices); built-in Page R (Real Time Composer) sequencer introduced in this model. The Series IIx (1983) added MIDI. The Series III (1985–1986) moved to 16-bit, 44.1 kHz, 16 channels, and ~14 MB total sample memory — a 56× memory increase over the Series II. [source: Wikipedia, Fairlight CMI article, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairlight_CMI, as of 2025-01-01]

Note on sample rate conflict: The Wikipedia Fairlight CMI article states the Series II had a 32 kHz maximum sample rate; a separate search result (sourced from synthtrax.net snippets) states the Series I and II were at 24 kHz and the IIx raised it to 32 kHz. The Series III’s 44.1 kHz figure is consistent across sources. Not resolved from available sources.

Technique 1 — Doubling Sample Time by Halving Bandwidth

The Series II’s default usable sample duration was under half a second, a severe constraint for building musical phrases. Jeczalik’s primary workaround was to double the available recording time by halving the bandwidth (i.e., halving the effective sample rate). The side-effect — degraded, gritty, low-fidelity audio quality — became a defining feature rather than a flaw: the characteristic distorted drum textures of early Art of Noise productions arose directly from this technical limitation turned creative tool. [source: Super Deluxe Edition interview, https://superdeluxeedition.com/interview/ztt30-j-j-jeczalik-on-fairlights-the-art-of-noise-and-life-at-ztt/]

Technique 2 — Page R Sequencer for Loop-Based Collage

The CMI Series II’s Page R (Real Time Composer) sequencer was central to Art of Noise’s compositional method. Page R displayed note patterns horizontally and allowed users to build rhythmic sequences of sampled sounds — a visual, pattern-based approach that did not require conventional keyboard playing. Jeczalik and Gary Langan used Page R to construct loop-based arrangements that mirrored hip-hop’s breakbeat isolation aesthetic, stacking sampled loops in ways that were “strictly in time.” [source: Paul Harkins academic paper abstract, “The Art of the Loop: JJ Jeczalik and the Fairlight CMI Series II/III,” Edinburgh Napier University, presented at Over and Over: Exploring Repetition in Popular Music, University of Liège, 2015 — https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/output/684621/the-art-of-the-loop-jj-jeczalik-and-the-fairlight-cmi-series-iiiii] [confirmed: search snippet from synthtrax.net]

Technique 3 — Sound as Shape, Not Music Theory

Jeczalik described his approach to the Fairlight as thinking “of sound as shapes” rather than notes. He had no formal music theory training and selected pitches based purely on sonic character. He built an extensive personal sample library — recording “anything and everything” while living in Highgate — and selected material for its textural impact. The results were “aggressive,” “sharp and quite angular.” Anne Dudley’s melodic keyboard contributions provided contrast, and it was that juxtaposition — conventional melody against Fairlight texture — that Jeczalik identified as the core of the Art of Noise sound: “incredible because the contrast sonically was just amazing.” [source: Super Deluxe Edition interview, https://superdeluxeedition.com/interview/ztt30-j-j-jeczalik-on-fairlights-the-art-of-noise-and-life-at-ztt/]

Technique 4 — Disguising Samples to Avoid Copyright

Art of Noise deliberately used Page R and other CMI techniques to transform and obscure the source material in their samples, both to create new sonic patterns and to avoid copyright infringement. [source: Science and Media Museum blog, https://blog.scienceandmediamuseum.org.uk/fairlight-cmi-playlist/]

Technique 5 — RAM Constraints as Creative Driver

Later in his career, Jeczalik reflected on how early hardware limits shaped the work: “Nowadays sampling is incredibly clear, it’s perfect and you’ve got unlimited RAM. Back then, having 8MB of RAM was a big deal, so you’d have to find ways of making things fit.” [source: synthtrax.net snippet — https://synthtrax.net/index.php/Fairlight_CMI_Song_List; note: synthtrax is a fan/hobbyist resource — treat as secondary corroboration only]

Technique 6 — Recording Context as Part of the Sample (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik’s field-recording method deliberately incorporated acoustic environment into the sample. Working from a flat down a lane surrounded by trees, his outdoor recordings picked up “multiple reflections from all these trees that made the initial sound really huge because it had the equivalent of, I don’t know, 50 micro delays in it.” He subsequently sought out locations — breaker’s yards, tyre shops — specifically because the ambient reverb became part of the final sound texture. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Technique 7 — Rejection of the Fairlight as Instrument Replicator (RBMA 2016)

A McCartney session (date unspecified) was the turning point: Jeczalik put trombone samples into the Fairlight and McCartney played chords on them. The result “sounded a bit like a Hoover” because the looped trombone samples couldn’t replicate a horn section. Jeczalik’s conclusion: “Expecting the Fairlight to replicate musical instruments was a complete waste of time.” From that point he searched only for “really interesting sounds that had an identifiable character.” [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Technique 8 — Sample Rate Trade-Off: 14 kHz for Extended Duration (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik gives a specific figure for his bandwidth/duration trade-off: he “tended to sample at 14 kilohertz and get about a second-and-a-half out of it.” (The Fairlight’s preferred default was 40 kHz, giving under half a second.) The cost was “an incredible amount of aliasing and gaps in the sample because it was 8-bit.” Each sound therefore “had to have its own identity and its own shape.” This confirms and adds the specific 14 kHz figure to the technique already documented from the Super Deluxe Edition interview. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Technique 9 — Page R BPM Ambiguity and the “Clock Number” Problem (RBMA 2016)

Early Page R did not display BPM — it displayed a raw clock number (e.g. “3,286”). Translating that to an approximate BPM required manual calculation. Later a readout was added and eventually the system could sync to SMPTE timecode on 24-track tape. For Art of Noise, the practical result was “no more than four or five tracks at a time” from the Fairlight; everything else was overdubbed live on top. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Technique 10 — Nine-Bar Loops and the Stop Bar Device (RBMA 2016)

“Moments in Love” was deliberately constructed with nine-bar loops (not the standard four) to enable stop bars — structural gaps that clear all elements and allow a full rebuild. Jeczalik: “We had a lot of nine bar loops in there that people didn’t spot, but enabled us to create the little stop bars here and there.” The repetitiveness was intentional: “I said, ‘Let’s make it the longest, most boring track we can.‘” The “no drums” decision on that track was also deliberate — referencing 10cc’s “I’m Not in Love” as a template for sustained atmosphere without a backbeat. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Named Samples and Origin Stories (RBMA 2016)

Beat Box (Diversion One): Origin confirmed from RBMA interview directly (corroborating the Wikipedia/Research Queue note). Gary Langan recorded Alan White (Yes drummer) on a talkback mic in the ceiling — not close-miked — during 90125 sessions. White played bass drum, snare, and hi-hat individually. Jeczalik and Langan then built “Beat Box” in Page R from those sounds in an overnight session. Additional sounds include finger pops, and a reversed “money” → “yenom” vocal. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

The car-start sound (likely “Close (To The Edit)”): Jeczalik set out to record horses riding past his flat. The horse recording failed but he captured his neighbour’s car going up the hill, stalling, and driving off. That incidental recording became the car-start sound. The acoustic context (trees surrounding the lane) added natural multi-delay reverb. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Moments in Love orchestra stab: Anne Dudley provided the source material — “a cassette of the orchestra stab” — on a Friday. Jeczalik loaded it over the weekend, played it back on Monday (“ba, ba, ba, ba”), and Dudley (with perfect pitch) immediately built the melody and arrangement from there. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Fairlight Model Progression (RBMA 2016 — first-person confirmation)

Jeczalik directly confirms his Series progression: started with the Series II (Geoff Downes’s machine, c.1979), bought a IIx after leaving ZTT (“slightly better”), then eventually bought a Series III (“had very good sampling quality”). He notes that Opus 4 (China Records era) was not created on the Fairlight — his machine was in for repair — but that Anne Dudley and Gary Langan began it by “just started looping something” using the same attitude. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Kate Bush, Geoff Downes, and Early Session Work (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik confirms he did Fairlight programming for Kate Bush along with Geoff Downes: “I did some programming for her along with Geoff on some of her albums.” Bush then taught herself the machine independently. Jeczalik’s freelance period for Trevor Horn involved taking the Fairlight home with a Revox A77 tape machine and building a personal sample library, responding to two or three days’ notice for sessions. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Disk Library and Recovery Note (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik kept all his samples on floppy disks in a large leather bag, with no written catalogue — the entire library was held in memory. Approximately 30 years later (circa 2015) a man in Bristol contacted him having found a box of Jeczalik’s disks from a studio. All disks were in his handwriting and all worked perfectly after three decades in storage. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Gary Langan’s Engineering Role Clarified (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik repeatedly attributes sonic transformation of his raw Fairlight samples to Gary Langan’s engineering: “compression and reverberation,” manually set delays using a stopwatch and by ear (not plug-in delay — “there was always a point where the repeats would start to roll, because they’re all slightly out”). This is the source of the tracks’ “indefinable quality” that Logic-style perfect delay cannot replicate. Quote: “All the stuff that Gary used to do, he used to do with a stopwatch and entirely by ear.” [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

MCL (Music Control Language) — Early Programming Method (RBMA 2016)

Before Page R, Jeczalik used the Fairlight’s MCL (Music Control Language) — a command-line note-event system with no iteration. Every note required individual typed entries for pitch, velocity, sustain, and release. “If you wanted to do a bass drum on fours, you had to type in every single event.” Page R replaced this with copy-paste sequencing: “bar one, bar two, repeat” — a transformative improvement. Jeczalik describes MCL work as “many, many hours of programming” and says he “wasn’t terribly happy with the outcome.” [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Light Pen — Non-Use for Waveform Editing (RBMA 2016)

Jeczalik confirms he never successfully used the Fairlight’s light pen for waveform modification: “I just couldn’t get on with it. I found that it was too clunky. I talked to several other people who used it, and they couldn’t get any sense out of it either.” He treated the light pen purely as a navigation/pointer device, equivalent to a mouse. [source: RBMA Daily interview, https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2016/11/jj-jeczalik-interview/]

Post-Art of Noise Activity

Art Of Silence (1995–1996)

Jeczalik’s primary post-AoN project was Art Of Silence (Discogs artist ID: 17845), listed under his real name on Discogs. He co-produced and wrote two releases with Blue Weaver on his own Axiomattic Records label:

  • West 4 (1995, Axiomattic Records) — Written-By, Producer
  • The Giant (Remixes) (1996, Axiomattic Records) — Producer; Written-By, Arranged By

The 1996 album artofsilence.co.uk (Axiomattic Records) is the most substantial post-AoN release — credited to Art Of Silence, with Jeczalik handling keyboards, mixing, recording, production, and writing throughout. Co-produced with Bob Kraushaar; also features Blue Weaver and Eddie Kulak. The album spawned a single, Teach Me (1997, Axiomattic Records), also produced and written by Jeczalik.

The artofsilence.co.uk website (now redirecting to theartofnoiseonline.com) was his public-facing presence during this period.

The Art Of This (2025)

Jeczalik resurfaced in 2025 as a member of The Art Of This (Discogs artist ID: 15729189), a group also featuring Gary Langan, Paul Morley, and Ian Peel. Released on This Blessed Context (and in part on Electronic Sound magazine’s label):

  • An Introduction (2025) — Performer [This Is]
  • A Thisette (2025) — Performer [This Is]

This marks his first credited performance work since the AoN reunion era.
