Gram Parsons

Discogs ID: 270757 Real name: Ingram Cecil Connor III Born: November 5, 1946, Winter Haven, Florida Died: September 19, 1973, Joshua Tree, California (morphine and alcohol overdose, age 26) Origin: US

American singer-songwriter, guitarist, and pianist. One of the primary architects of country rock — what he called “Cosmic American Music” — blending country, blues, folk, and rock. Lead vocalist and principal songwriter for International Submarine Band, a member of The Byrds during their pivotal Sweetheart Of The Rodeo sessions, co-founder and driving creative force of The Flying Burrito Bros, and a solo artist whose two Reprise albums (recorded with Emmylou Harris) became foundational texts of the country-rock and alt-country canons.

Biography

Early Life and The Shilohs (pre-1967)

Born Ingram Cecil Connor III in Winter Haven, Florida to a wealthy Southern family. His father, “Coon Dog” Connor, died by suicide in 1958; his mother, Avis, remarried Bob Parsons and Ingram took the Parsons surname. He showed early aptitude for music, playing in a series of teenage bands. Discogs documents an early recording credit: Sum Up Broke (Columbia, 1966) as a member of The Shilohs, where he contributed lead vocals and rhythm guitar (uncredited). The Early Years 1963-65 (Sierra Records, 1979) collected pre-ISB material from his Florida years, including recordings with the group also catalogued under the name The Shilos (a variant spelling).

He briefly attended Harvard University (1965–66) before departing to pursue music full-time, a move that would prove decisive for the trajectory of country rock.

The International Submarine Band and Safe At Home (1967–1968)

Parsons formed International Submarine Band in New York around 1966, eventually relocating the group to Los Angeles. Members: Parsons (rhythm guitar, lead vocals), John Nuese (lead guitar), Chris Ethridge (bass), Jon Corneal (drums, backing vocals), Bob Buchanan (rhythm guitar, backing vocals), and Ian Dunlop. The band signed to LHI Records — Lee Hazlewood’s imprint on Sunset Boulevard — and recorded Safe At Home (cat# LHI-S-12001), released in 1968.

Producer: Suzi Jane Hokom Engineers: Eddie Brackett, Michael Lietz Steel guitar: Jay Dee Maness Piano: Earl Ball

The album mixes Parsons originals (“Blue Eyes,” “Luxury Liner,” “Do You Know How It Feels To Be Lonesome?”) with country covers: Merle Haggard (“I Must Be Somebody Else You’ve Known”), Johnny Cash (“Folsom Prison Blues” / “I Still Miss Someone”), Arthur “Big Boy” Crudup (“That’s All Right”), Jack Clement (“Miller’s Cave”), and Barry Goldberg (“Do You Know How It Feels”). Parsons also co-wrote “Luxury Liner” and other originals with Buchanan.

Notably, Suzi Jane Hokom produced — an unusual position for a woman in late-1960s American rock recording. LHI’s distribution shifted from Decca to ABC Records in 1968, meaning the album landed in a transitional window. It was not a commercial success on release but was retrospectively recognized as among the earliest country rock records.

The Byrds — Sweetheart Of The Rodeo (1968)

Parsons joined The Byrds in early 1968, recruited by Roger McGuinn and Chris Hillman as the band shifted toward country territory. His tenure was brief — roughly six months — but transformative. His guitar and songwriting contributions appear on Sweetheart Of The Rodeo (Columbia CS 9670, 1968), produced by Gary Usher.

Key Parsons originals on the record: “Hickory Wind” (co-written with Bob Buchanan) and “One Hundred Years From Now.” He also sang lead on several tracks including covers of the Louvin Brothers’ “The Christian Life” and Dylan’s “You Ain’t Going Nowhere.” (Contractual issues with LHI Records meant some of his lead vocal tracks were re-recorded by McGuinn for the final release — his voice remains audible on some pressings, and the full Parsons vocal versions circulated on bootlegs for years before being officially released on box sets.)

The album also featured Clarence White on guitar, Jon Corneal and Kevin Kelley on drums, Lloyd Green and Jay Dee Maness on steel guitar, John Hartford on banjo and guitar, and Roy Huskey on bass.

Parsons left the Byrds in mid-1968, declining to tour South Africa on political grounds (the Byrds were scheduled to play to segregated audiences). His departure opened the door to the Flying Burrito Brothers.

The Flying Burrito Brothers (1969–1970)

Parsons co-founded The Flying Burrito Bros with Chris Hillman, who had also departed the Byrds. The band’s debut, The Gilded Palace Of Sin (A&M SP 4175, 1969), is the most fully realized statement of their country-soul synthesis.

Highlights include “Sin City” (Parsons/Hillman), “Hot Burrito #1,” “Hot Burrito #2,” “Dark End Of The Street,” and “Do Right Woman.” The album blended honky-tonk country, R&B soul, and psychedelia in a way that had little commercial precedent. It sold poorly but became one of the genre’s most-cited reference points.

Burrito Deluxe (A&M SP 4258, 1970) followed with Bernie Leadon replacing Ethridge. Producers: Henry Lewy and Jim Dickson. The recording is looser and less focused than Gilded Palace, though notable tracks include “Lazy Days” (Parsons), “Wild Horses” (Jagger-Richards — the Burritos recorded this before the Stones released it on Sticky Fingers), and “Man In The Fog” (Parsons/Leadon). Leon Russell played piano on “Man In The Fog” and “Wild Horses.” Byron Berline contributed fiddle. Michael Clarke played drums.

Parsons departed the Flying Burrito Brothers in 1970, reportedly owing to personal difficulties and increasing drug use. The band continued under Hillman and later Rick Roberts.

Solo Career — GP and Grievous Angel (1973–1974)

Parsons signed to Reprise Records for two solo albums, both co-produced with the core band led by Glen Hardin (piano/organ/leader) and featuring James Burton (guitar/dobro), Al Perkins (steel guitar), Ron Tutt (drums), and Rick Grech (co-producer on GP).

The defining feature of both records is the vocal partnership with Emmylou Harris, introduced to Parsons in 1972 through mutual connections in the Washington D.C. folk scene. Their blend — Harris’s crystalline soprano against Parsons’s weathered tenor — became one of country music’s most celebrated duet pairings and reshaped her subsequent career.

Gram Parsons & The Fallen Angels (1973)

In 1973, Parsons toured as Gram Parsons & The Fallen Angels — a road band that included Emmylou Harris (acoustic guitar, harmony vocals), Neil Flanz (pedal steel), Kyle Tullis (bass), N.D. Smart II (drums), Jock Bartley (electric bass), and Gerry Mulé. Live 1973 (Repertoire Records, 1982) captures the March 1973 tour, with Parsons and Harris trading leads. A radio broadcast from Boston (March 18, 1973) also circulated as an archival release.

Death

Parsons died on September 19, 1973 at the Joshua Tree Inn in California, of a morphine and alcohol overdose, six weeks before Grievous Angel sessions were completed. He was 26. His road manager, Phil Kaufman, controversially drove the body to Joshua Tree National Monument and attempted to cremate it, reportedly fulfilling a pact Parsons had expressed; the body was recovered. He was eventually buried in New Orleans.
