From Discogs

Bay Area, California scenester. After releasing a series of cassette compilations of local East Bay punk and hardcore bands and working on Maximum Rock ‘n’ Roll’s “Turn It Around” compilation, David Hayes co-founded Lookout! Records (along with Lawrence Livermore) in 1988 to document the burgeoning underground punk scene that was centered around Berkeley’s “Gilman Street Project.” Hayes was responsible for releasing some of the most legendary and influential recordings of the late 1980s East Bay punk scene (including releases by Operation Ivy, Crimpshrine, Sewer Trout, Stikky, Isocracy, and many others).

Hayes left Lookout! Records in 1989 after two years. In David Hayes’ words, “When I quit, I told [Lawrence Livermore] I wanted nothing to do with the label, for better or worse, thereby screwing myself out of at least a million dollars with one simple verbal statement…”

Hayes would go on to start Very Small Records and Too Many Records.

Overview

David Hayes is a Bay Area punk scenester who co-founded Lookout! Records with Lawrence Livermore in 1988, having already been documenting the East Bay underground through cassette compilations and work on the Maximumrocknroll “Turn It Around” compilation. The label became the primary document of the Gilman Street Project scene, releasing records by Operation Ivy, Crimpshrine, Stikky, Isocracy, and others — but Hayes walked away after just two years, explicitly waiving any ownership stake. He subsequently founded Very Small Records and Too Many Records, with credits that run from 1987 through the late 2000s and span punk, hardcore, ska, and experimental music. His work is primarily curatorial — compilation producing, editing, and artwork — alongside recurring studio collaborators John Golden, Alex Sergay, and Kevin Army.

Notes (web) — Hayes’ departure from Lookout! Records, 1989

Summary of findings: Multiple secondary sources drawing on Lawrence Livermore’s memoir How to Ru(i)n a Record Label (Don Giovanni Records, 2015) and Kevin Prested’s oral history Punk USA: The Rise and Fall of Lookout! Records (Microcosm Publishing, 2014) give a consistent account.

The philosophical split. Hayes’ departure was rooted in a growing divergence in values between him and Livermore. Hayes was aligned with the Maximumrocknroll ethos that viewed bands achieving mainstream success, national radio play, and MTV exposure as a betrayal of the underground. Livermore was open to bands reaching larger audiences. As the label began generating real commercial momentum — particularly around 1989, with Operation Ivy’s Energy and the broader Gilman Street scene attracting wider attention — Hayes grew increasingly uncomfortable. (punktuationmag.com)

Hayes’ own words (two quotes cited in secondary sources). He told Livermore: “There’s too much golden light around Lookout right now,” and separately described the label work as having become “too much like a job.” (punktuationmag.com; Wikipedia, Lookout Records article, as of 2026-09-29)

Livermore’s retention offer. According to the Wikipedia article on Lookout Records, Livermore attempted to keep Hayes by offering him “operational control and half the profits.” Hayes rejected this. Livermore later said he did not understand Hayes’ “golden light” remark at the time but in retrospect recognised Hayes “had been able to see what was coming and had decided not to be a part of it.” (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookout_Records, as of 2026-09-29)

Formal departure date. Wikipedia gives January 1, 1990 as the date Hayes officially departed, declaring he wanted “nothing more to do with Lookout, and nothing more from Lookout.” (Discogs and most secondary sources say “1989,” likely referring to the decision rather than the formal date — minor discrepancy, not a material conflict.) (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookout_Records, as of 2026-09-29)

Waiving ownership. The waiver of ownership is presented across sources as consistent with Hayes’ anti-commercial stance — consistent with the same values that drove the departure, not a separate act of generosity or grievance. No source found with more detail on the mechanism (written, verbal, formal or informal) beyond the existing Discogs quote (“I told him I wanted nothing to do with the label, for better or worse, thereby screwing myself out of at least a million dollars with one simple verbal statement”).

Source quality note. The Punktuation Magazine article and the Wikipedia article do not cite their sources inline for these details; both appear to be drawing on Livermore’s memoir and/or the Prested oral history. The quotes attributed to Hayes (“golden light”; “too much like a job”) may originate from either of those books rather than a direct interview. The underlying primary sources (Livermore’s memoir; Prested’s book) were not directly accessible for this search.

What was checked (dead ends). The Talkhouse excerpt from Livermore’s memoir does not cover Hayes — it concerns the Potatomen. The Willamette Week 2016 interview with Livermore does not mention Hayes. The Sisters in Punk Substack article mentions Hayes only in passing, characterising his exit as “artistic compass pointing elsewhere.” No direct interview with Hayes himself about the departure was found via web search.
