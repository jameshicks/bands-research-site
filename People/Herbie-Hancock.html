Herbie Hancock

American jazz pianist, keyboardist, composer, and bandleader. Born Herbert Jeffrey Hancock, April 12, 1940, Chicago, Illinois. Discogs artist ID: 3865. Also credited as Mwandishi (Swahili for “composer/writer,” Discogs artist ID: 320105), his adopted name during the early-1970s electric band period.

Member of the Miles Davis second great quintet (1963–68) alongside Tony Williams, Ron Carter, and Wayne Shorter. Prolific Blue Note leader in the 1960s. Moved into electric fusion territory in the early 1970s with the Mwandishi band; major commercial crossover with Head Hunters (1973, Columbia). V.S.O.P. Quintet acoustic reunions from the mid-1970s. Hip-hop-influenced breakthrough with “Rockit” (1983). Late-career orchestral and tribute projects for Verve Records. Extensive film scoring throughout.

Biography

Early Career and Blue Note Era (1962–1969)

Hancock came to professional attention through Miles Davis, who introduced him to Alfred Lion, co-founder of Blue Note. He recorded his debut album Takin’ Off in 1962 — a sextet date with Freddie Hubbard on trumpet and Dexter Gordon on tenor sax — which contained “Watermelon Man,” a composition that has followed him throughout his entire career.

Throughout the 1960s Hancock recorded prolifically for Blue Note as both leader and sideman. As a leader he developed a personal style rooted in post-bop but increasingly open to chamber music voicings and unorthodox instrumentation. Empyrean Isles (1964) featured a piano-quartet with Hubbard, Ron Carter on bass, and Tony Williams on drums; all four tracks were Hancock originals and the album is considered one of the definitive Blue Note releases of the decade. Maiden Voyage (1965) extended the approach with George Coleman on tenor and a pronounced suite-like architecture around oceanic themes. Speak Like a Child (1968) introduced a subdued chamber setting with flugelhorn (Thad Jones), alto flute (Jerry Dodgion), and bass trombone (Peter Phillips) alongside a conventional rhythm section; Duke Pearson produced.

Simultaneously, from 1963 to 1968 Hancock served as pianist in Miles Davis’s second great quintet — the group with Tony Williams, Ron Carter, Wayne Shorter, and Davis — which was among the most transformative ensembles in jazz history. Davis’s influence pushed Hancock toward freer harmonic conception and more interactive ensemble playing.

His final Blue Note session as leader, The Prisoner (1970, recorded 1969), was a larger-ensemble date produced by Duke Pearson, with Joe Henderson on tenor and Buster Williams on bass.

Transition: Fat Albert Rotunda and Warner Bros. (1969–1970)

After leaving Davis’s group, Hancock signed to Warner Bros. His first release there, Fat Albert Rotunda (1969), was a soundtrack for Bill Cosby’s animated TV special and marked a shift toward funky, soul-inflected writing. It employed a large ensemble including Joe Henderson, Joe Farrell, Buster Williams, and Bernard Purdie on drums.

Mwandishi Band (1971–1973)

Hancock assembled an electric sextet that pursued an exploratory fusion aesthetic rooted in free jazz, electronics, and spiritual music. The band members adopted Swahili names: Hancock became Mwandishi, Bennie Maupin became Mwile, Buster Williams became Mchezaji, Billy Hart became Jabali, Eddie Henderson became Mganga, and Julian Priester became Pepo. Synthesizer work was contributed by Patrick Gleeson on Crossings and Sextant.

All three albums were produced by David Rubinson (or David Rubinson & Friends, Inc.) and engineered by Fred Catero.

Mwandishi (1971, Warner Bros.) — debut of the Mwandishi name; Leon Ndugu Chancler also appeared on drums alongside Billy Hart.

Crossings (1972, Warner Bros.) — added Patrick Gleeson on Moog synthesizer; includes voices (Sandra Stevens, Scott Beach, others) on the side-long title composition; Bennie Maupin co-wrote the B side.

Sextant (1973, Columbia) — the most densely layered and electronic of the three; the band members appear under their Swahili names throughout; Julian Priester’s Swahili name Pepo credited with “Random Resonator” effects; Robert Springett’s cover painting became iconic.

The band never achieved commercial success and Warner Bros. dropped Hancock after Crossings. The complete Warner Bros. recordings were reissued as a box set in 1994.

Head Hunters Era (1973–1975)

Seeking greater commercial reach, Hancock dissolved the Mwandishi band and assembled a new funk-oriented group anchored by Harvey Mason (drums), Paul Jackson (2) (bass), Bill Summers (percussion), and Bennie Maupin (reeds). Hancock and Maupin were the only continuity from the previous band.

Head Hunters (1973, Columbia) — Hancock’s commercial breakthrough; co-produced with David Rubinson; recorded at Wally Heider’s studio. The album opened with “Chameleon,” a 15-minute groove construction. It became one of the best-selling jazz albums of its era. Victor Moscoso designed the cover.

Thrust (1974, Columbia) — follow-up with Mike Clark replacing Harvey Mason on drums; Paul Jackson and Bill Summers retained; Bennie Maupin on reeds; still produced by Hancock and Rubinson; Rob Springett cover art.

Hancock also released the Death Wish film score (1974) and Man-Child (1975, with a larger cast of funk musicians) during this period.

V.S.O.P. Quintet (1977–1980s)

The V.S.O.P. Quintet (Discogs artist ID: 813480) was an acoustic group effectively reuniting the Miles Davis second quintet with Freddie Hubbard substituting for Davis. Core lineup: Hancock, Wayne Shorter, Hubbard, Ron Carter, Tony Williams. The group appeared at the 1976 Newport Jazz Festival in New York and subsequently recorded for Columbia.

V.S.O.P. (1977, Columbia) — live double album. The set also included separate performances with a “Mwandishi reunion” portion (tracks C1–C3 on the original LP), with Billy Hart, Buster Williams, Bennie Maupin, and Julian Priester present alongside the V.S.O.P. quintet tracks, and an electric set on side D featuring Wah Wah Watson and Ray Parker Jr. on guitar.

Electric and Pop Years (1978–1988)

Hancock moved through several phases during this period: Vocoder-heavy productions (Sunlight, 1978; Feets Don’t Fail Me Now, 1979); a duet acoustic project with Chick Corea (An Evening With Herbie Hancock & Chick Corea In Concert, 1978); and a collaboration with Foday Musa Suso on Village Life (1985).

The pop breakthrough came with Future Shock (1983, Columbia), produced in collaboration with Bill Laswell and Material. “Rockit,” featuring scratching by DJ Grandmixer D.ST (D.ST), won multiple Grammy Awards and brought hip-hop into mainstream pop consciousness. Sound-System (1984) continued in the same direction.

Hancock scored Round Midnight (1986), Bertrand Tavernier’s film about jazz in Paris, earning an Academy Award for Best Original Score.

Later Work: Verve Period and Orchestral Projects (1994–2010s)

After a mid-1990s period on Mercury/Verve experimenting with hip-hop collaborations (Dis Is Da Drum, 1994; The New Standard, 1995), Hancock settled into a more prestigious, orchestral mode.

Gershwin’s World (1998, Verve) — produced by Robert Sadin; an expansive tribute to George Gershwin featuring Stevie Wonder, Joni Mitchell, Wayne Shorter, Kenny Garrett, Eddie Henderson, Terri Lyne Carrington, James Carter (3), Kathleen Battle, Chick Corea, and the Orpheus Chamber Orchestra; also incorporated Ravel and Duke Ellington material.

River: The Joni Letters (2007, Verve) — produced by Hancock and Larry Klein; a tribute to Joni Mitchell’s songwriting featuring Norah Jones, Tina Turner, Corinne Bailey Rae, Leonard Cohen, Luciana Souza, and Wayne Shorter; rhythm section of Dave Holland (bass) and Vinnie Colaiuta (drums); Lionel Loueke on guitar. Won the Grammy Award for Album of the Year (2008) — the first jazz album to win the award since Getz/Gilberto in 1965.

The Imagine Project (2010, Hancock Records) — global collaboration featuring artists from multiple continents.
