Pascal Comelade

Discogs ID: 135795
Full name: Pascal Paul Vincent Comelade
Born: June 30, 1955, Montpellier, France

French-Catalan musician. Known for strange cover songs of rock and easy listening standards performed on toy piano, singing saw, and other unusual instruments. Developed a deeply idiosyncratic style across a career from 1979 to the present. Collaborated with Robert Wyatt, Faust, and PJ Harvey. Alias: Fluence.

Central artist on Cougouyou Music (9 releases, 2016–2023), a sublabel of Staubgold in Perpignan. Earlier career primarily on Les Disques Du Soleil Et De L’Acier and Because Music. Also releases on Discmedi (Catalan label).

Collaborated with Lee Ranaldo and Ramón Prats on Velvet Serenade (cougouyou 22 / staubgold 169, 2023) — a Velvet Underground covers LP.

Leads Bel Canto Orchestra, a seven-piece ensemble using toy instruments alongside classical ones. Also in Fall Of Saigon and Pascal Comelade Et Son Nouvel Orchestre.

Catalan cultural identity — meaning and significance

Origins and formation

Comelade was born in Montpellier but grew up in the Catalan cultural orbit of Southern France. His father was deeply invested in Catalan culture, bringing Nova Cançó performers including Lluís Llach and Maria del Mar Bonet to Montpellier during the 1960s–70s. Comelade spent time in Barcelona from the early 1970s — living with Lluís Llach in 1972–1974 — and has described Barcelona as his creative capital: “Decideixo que aquesta ciutat és la meva capital. Barcelona, capital” (Vilaweb, 2019, https://www.vilaweb.cat/noticies/entrevista-pascal-comelade-concerts/). Around 2002, when his French career stalled, he relocated to Barcelona again; he has stated “I positioned myself as a barcelonese musician” (La Revue des Ressources).

He lives near Perpignan (in Passa and Céret), in Catalunya Nord — a region that was historically Catalan and remains Catalan-speaking in part but has been administratively French since the 1659 Treaty of the Pyrenees. This divided identity — Catalan culturally, French politically — is central to Comelade’s self-understanding: one profile describes him as “Catalan, belonging to this territory divided in two by a border, always in motion within this perimeter” (search snippet, mediamus.fr).

The Catalan language

Comelade uses Catalan language throughout his work — in album and track titles, in song lyrics, and in public positioning. Album titles in Catalan include Mètode de Rocanrol (2007; “mètode” uses Catalan orthography, not French), Mosques de Colors (2013), El Steinway a la Guillotina (2008), La Filosofia del Plat Combinat (2004), El Cabaret Galactic (1995), Psicòtic Music’ Hall (2002/2010), and many individual track titles. He speaks Catalan with his daughter, who attended La Bressola (a Catalan-language school network in Catalunya Nord) (Vilaweb ibid.).

His collaboration with Catalan-language poet Enric Casasses (28 shared releases) is one of the deepest working relationships of his career. The album La Manera Més Salvatge (2006) with Casasses is in Catalan, and Comelade has championed Casasses as an artist who connects punk energy with medieval troubadour tradition.

Political and activist engagement

In 1998 Comelade co-authored the “Manifest revulsista nord-català” (North Catalan Seditious Manifesto) with writer Joan-Lluís Lluís and Àlex Renyé, a text produced by linguistic and cultural activists in Catalunya Nord (Wikipedia on Joan-Lluís Lluís). The manifesto situates him explicitly in the North Catalan cultural-linguistic revival movement. In later interviews he has described the French media’s near-total silence on Catalan independence politics as “silenci absolut” amounting to “complicitat” (complicity) (Vilaweb ibid.).

The Col·lecció Nord series and traditional Catalan forms

A dedicated strand of Comelade’s discography — released primarily on Discmedi and its sublabels — documents the musical heritage of Catalunya Nord and the broader Catalan lands:

  • Pop Songs del Rosselló (2 volumes, 2000–2001; compiled 2014): covers of traditional and popular songs from the Roussillon/Rosselló area, sung by Gérard Jacquet. Directly engages the local oral/popular tradition of the region.
  • Musica Pop (Danses de Catalunya Nord) (2003): instrumental covers of traditional Catalan folk dance tunes from Northern Catalonia.
  • Música Electrònica a Catalunya Nord (Vol. 3, 2014, with Raph Dumas and Solo Music): extends the series into electronic territory while maintaining the territorial focus.
  • The series as a whole constitutes what was presented as the “Col·lecció Nord” (North collection), four new volumes of which were presented c. 2014 (El Punt Avui, https://www.elpuntavui.cat/cultura/article/19-cultura/801547-es-presenten-4-nous-cd-de-la-colmleccio-nord-de-pascal-comelade.html — page inaccessible due to CAPTCHA; cited from search snippet).

The cobla and sardana

The cobla (11-piece traditional Catalan wind-and-percussion ensemble, the usual accompaniment of the sardana circle dance) appears across his work as a recurring Catalan signifier:

  • Sardanes à Vernet-les-Bains (2005): live recording with Bel Canto Orquestra incorporating traditional Catalan dance music.
  • Espontex Sinfonia (2006): includes “Sardana dels desemparats,” a sardana composition.
  • La societat del piano-obstacle (2008): composed for cobla, piano, and percussion — described as “minimalist, sound-based, naïf, pop” in style (vivonzeureux.fr).
  • Pascal Comelade & Cobla Sant Jordi (DiscMedi Blau, 2011): full album of Comelade compositions reinterpreted by the Cobla Sant Jordi-Ciutat de Barcelona ensemble. Premiered in Perpinyà — framing it explicitly as a gift to Catalunya Nord audiences (Enderrock). A subsequent concert was scheduled for Tortosa (South Catalonia).
  • El contrapàs vertical (2025): a revisited reissue in collaboration with Cobla Sant Jordi (vivonzeureux.fr).

He has spoken of the cobla as a formative childhood sound: a concert attendee noted he “is sensitive to the Catalan orchestra because he heard it when he was a child” (franceinfo).

Catalan Modernisme and historical figures

Comelade explicitly claims Catalan modernist and avant-garde figures as his intellectual heritage. He has described Arthur Cravan, Francis Picabia, and Marcel Duchamp as part of his “interior geography” because “They are all related to the country. They lived there. They were active there” (La Revue des Ressources). He celebrates Catalan composers Frederic Mompou, Isaac Albéniz, and Enrique Granados as pianistic ancestors, and singles out pianist Ricardo Viñès — who introduced Satie and Ravel to Paris while remaining devoted to his homeland — as an embodiment of his own principle: “a desire for universality while remaining attached to their own country” (ibid.).

Published writing on Catalan music

Approximately twenty years before a 2009 interview (i.e. c. 1989), Comelade published the “Enciclopedia logicofobista de la musica catalana” (Encyclopedia of Logic-Phobic Catalan Music), demonstrating early and systematic scholarly engagement with the Catalan musical tradition (poptronics.fr).

Reception asymmetry

In France, Comelade receives little mainstream media attention and is filed under experimental or film-music categories. In Catalonia, he is regarded as an essential musician. He himself frames this as structural: “In France, you are in the system or on the margins” — and has situated himself firmly as a Catalan (not French) artist operating from that margin (savantsounds.blogspot.com, poptronics.fr).
