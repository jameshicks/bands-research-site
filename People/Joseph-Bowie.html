Joseph Bowie

Joseph Bowie

Aug 11, 202620 min read

Joseph Bowie

Born: October 17, 1953, St. Louis, Missouri
Instrument: Trombone, vocals, congas
Discogs artist ID: 265057
Also billed as: Joe Bowie, “Mr. Defunkt” Joseph Bowie
Lives: Gorinchem, Netherlands (as of latest Discogs profile)

American jazz trombonist, singer, composer, and bandleader. Founder and leader of Defunkt. Younger brother of trumpeter Lester Bowie (Art Ensemble of Chicago) and saxophonist Byron Bowie. One of the bridges between the St. Louis avant-garde jazz tradition and the New York No Wave/funk-punk scene of the late 1970s–early 1980s.

Biography

St. Louis Origins and the Black Artists Group (early 1970s)

Joseph Bowie was born October 17, 1953, in St. Louis, Missouri — youngest in a musical family that included his older brother Lester Bowie, by then already one of the leading voices in the AACM orbit and a co-founder of The Art Ensemble Of Chicago.

In St. Louis, Joseph was a founding member of the Black Artists Group (BAG), a multi-disciplinary collective modelled on Chicago’s AACM. BAG brought together musicians, poets, actors, and visual artists committed to Black avant-garde expression. Other BAG members included Oliver Lake (saxophone), Charles Bobo Shaw (drums), Baikida Carroll (trumpet), Floyd LeFlore (trumpet), and Shirley LeFlore (poet). Bowie wrote compositions for the group and played trombone and percussion.

Key early BAG document: In Paris, Aries 1973 (self-released, BAG 324 000, 1973) — recorded live in Paris with the core quintet of Bowie, Lake, Shaw, Carroll, and LeFlore. Bowie co-wrote “Echos” and contributed trombone and percussion throughout. Another early credit: Whisper Of Dharma (Universal Justice Records, 1972) with the same circle.

Also from this period: Çonceré Ntasiah (Universal Justice Records, 1976), and his appearance on Red, Black & Green (1972, Not On Label).

Move to New York (1973 onward)

Bowie moved to New York City in 1973. He remained active in the extended BAG/loft jazz network while beginning to connect with the downtown avant-garde scene.

His 1976 duo album with Oliver Lake — Joseph Bowie • Oliver Lake (Sackville Recordings, 1976; Discogs ID: 841974) — is one of his most concentrated early statements: a trombone/reed duet recorded in Canada, with Lake on flute, alto sax, and Bb saxophone.

He appeared on a series of free jazz records across this period: Fast Last! (Muse, 1974) and Rope-A-Dope (Muse, 1976) with Phillip Wilson; Fresh (Arista/Freedom, 1975) and NTU: Point From Which Creation Begins (Arista/Freedom, 1976) with Oliver Lake; The Flam (Black Saint, 1976) with Sam Rivers; Funky Donkey (Circle, 1977) with the Human Arts Ensemble; and Junk Trap (Black Saint, 1978) — co-billed to Charles Bobo Shaw / Human Arts Ensemble / Joseph Bowie — an especially important record that brought the St. Louis avant-garde to the Italian Black Saint label. He also appeared on Anthony Braxton’s Orchestra (Paris) 1978 (Braxton Bootleg, 2011 release) and with the Jazz Composers’ Orchestra.

Defunkt and the No Wave Scene (1978–1982)

By the late 1970s, Bowie was operating in the same downtown Manhattan scene as the No Wave groups — the loft jazz world and the punk/funk world were overlapping, and Black musicians from the jazz tradition were key to both. Bowie founded Defunkt around 1979–1980, building a band that fused free jazz trombone with funk rhythms and punk energy.

The Defunkt self-titled debut EP (Island Records, 1980; Discogs ID: 662290) established the blueprint. Bowie plays trombone and vocals; the band includes Kelvyn Bell and Martin Aubert (guitars), Melvin Gibbs (bass), Ronnie Burrage (drums), and his brother Byron Bowie (flute, saxophone) who also co-produced and arranged the horns. Charles Bobo Shaw (St. Louis connection) appears on two tracks. The record was produced by Janos Gat and Byron Bowie.

Alongside his Defunkt work, Bowie appeared as a sideman on two key James Chance/James White projects (see below).

Razor’s Edge / Stranglin’ Me With Your Love (Hannibal Records, 1981; Discogs ID: 1562426) — Defunkt 7” single. Bowie takes lead vocals and trombone. Lineup also includes Luther Thomas (alto sax), Kim Clarke (bass), Kelvyn Bell (guitar, vocals), Richie Harrison (drums), Byron Bowie (tenor sax), Clarice Taylor (vocals), and notably Lester Bowie on trumpet — a rare documented moment of the two brothers on the same record.

Thermonuclear Sweat (Hannibal Records / Inelco, 1982; Discogs ID: 1608207) — Defunkt’s first full LP. Bowie co-produced with Joe Boyd, plays trombone and lead vocals. Vernon Reid plays guitar on tracks A2–B3, an early credit for the future Living Colour founder. Kim Clarke on bass, Kenny Martin on drums, Kelvyn Bell on guitar, Dave Hubbard on saxophone, John Mulkerin on trumpet.

With James Chance and James White (1981–1982)

Bowie appears on two releases by the James Chance/James White axis — his most direct points of overlap with the No Wave world as a sideman:

1. Live In New York (ROIR A100, 1981; Discogs ID: 575493) — James Chance & The Contortions. The first release ever on the ROIR cassette label. Bowie is credited as “Joe Bowie” — Trombone (release-level credit, no specific tracks listed). The lineup: Chance (vocals, alto sax, mixed by), Colin Wade (bass), Richie Harrison (drums), Bern Nix (guitar), Tomás Doncker (guitar), Bowie (trombone).

2. Sax Maniac (Animal Records, 1982; Discogs ID: 117536) — James White & The Blacks. Bowie credited as Trombone on tracks A1, B1, B2 (not the full album). James White produced and arranged; Bob Blank engineered. Other horn players: Robert Aaron (tenor sax), Luther Thomas (saxophone), John Mulkerin (trumpet). Colin Wade (bass), Ralph Rolle (drums, percussion). Maripol did art direction; the cover concept was by Anya Phillips (posthumously — she had died in June 1981). The album was dedicated to her memory.

A 1991 ROIR reissue of Live In New York (Discogs ID: 465539) credits Bowie as “Trombone, Backing Vocals” — slightly expanded from the original sleeve.

Not on No New York (1978): Bowie does not appear on the Brian Eno-produced No Wave compilation. His entry into the Chance orbit came after BAG/loft jazz and when Defunkt was already forming.

1980s–1990s: Defunkt Expands

Bowie continued to lead Defunkt through various lineups, releasing on Hannibal, Antilles, DIW, and Enemy Records:

  • Illusion (Hannibal, 1982; Discogs ID: 1582962) — produced by Bowie
  • Avoid The Funk… A Defunkt Anthology (Hannibal, 1988) — compilation/retrospective
  • In America (Antilles New Directions, 1988)
  • Heroes (DIW, 1990)
  • Live At The Knitting Factory NYC (Enemy Records, 1991)
  • Crisis (Enemy Records, 1992)
  • Cum Funky (Enemy Records, 1993)
  • A Blues Tribute — Jimi Hendrix & Muddy Waters (Enemy Records, 1994)
  • One World (Blue Funk, 1995)

He also appeared on compilations on Blue Note (Sacred Common Ground, 1995, with Don Pullen), Chameleon Records (Dance With The Ancestors, 1993), and other labels.

Later Career and Netherlands Base

By the mid-1990s Bowie had relocated to the Netherlands, where he remained active. He recorded Rhythm Voice (Plainisphare, 1998) and Soul Integration (Challenge Records, 2007) as leader from there, and maintained Defunkt as a working band.

Later credits include collaborations with Adam Rudolph, Kahil El’Zabar’s Infinity Orchestra, the Ethnic Heritage Ensemble, the Oliver Lake Big Band, Murphy’s Law (2), and many European-based ensembles.

In 2024: trombone credit on For Peace And Liberty (INA / Wewantsounds, France; Discogs ID: 31696127).

Relationship with James Chance

How the Collaboration Began

The precise mechanism of introduction is not documented in a single authoritative source, but the available evidence points to a shared downtown scene rather than a formal recruitment. By 1978 Bowie was back in New York operating in the loft jazz and avant-garde circuit, and the No Wave world overlapped with that milieu by proximity. According to multiple sources including the James Chance official biography (jameschanceofficial.blogspot.com), Bowie set up the first lineup of James White and the Blacks — meaning he was not simply hired as a sideman but played an active organisational role in constituting the band. Wikipedia’s Joseph Bowie entry states he “began singing with punk and funk musician James Chance and the Contortions” after returning to New York in 1978, and that “Defunkt was born during that time.” (Wikipedia, Joseph Bowie, as of 2026-05-23)

The PopMatters interview (2020) confirms Bowie’s organisational role: Chance asked him to find strong Black musicians for the band, and Bowie brought in horns, drummers, guitarists, and bassists — including Luther Thomas, John Mulkerin, and Richie Harrison, players who appear on both Live In New York and Sax Maniac and who also populated early Defunkt lineups. (popmatters.com, “All That Jazz and Then Some: An Interview with Defunkt’s Joseph Bowie,” 2020)

The Jazzwise interview corroborates the sequence: “I got a job playing with James Chance, playing a mixture of punk and funk. After a while I said I might as well form my own band. So I formed Defunkt and we were opening shows for James and that is how Defunkt began.” (jazzwise.com, “An interview with Joseph Bowie: Still funking after Defunkt”)

The Bowie-Chance Working Relationship

Bowie’s statements about Chance are consistently warm but frank. From the PopMatters interview (2020), reposted on his own site:

“James was one of the kingpins in the genre…James was great. He possessed a special personality and his music was very unique and passionate to frenzy!”

“James was/is a high-energy and sometimes volatile bandleader that takes no shit from the audience either!”

“I had a closer relationship with James because of shared musical likes and desires. We also shared a relationship with drugs (heroin).”

“This was great and a real lift in my career. A new dimension was opening up to me.”

On the transition to Defunkt:

“After a while, I realized that I could form my own band to open for James.”

(josephbowie.com, reposting PopMatters 2020 interview)

These statements frame the Chance work as genuinely significant — musically “a real lift” — and personally close (shared drug use is disclosed without apparent embarrassment). There is no recorded ambivalence or tension in Bowie’s public statements. The move to Defunkt is framed as an organic extension, not an exit from an uncomfortable situation.

Defunkt’s Origin and the Blacks Lineup — Conflict Note

Several secondary sources (including the James Chance official biography, Grokipedia, and a Don Armstrong 2024 blog post) assert that the Blacks lineup “pulled double duty” as the first Defunkt lineup, or that “Defunkt emerged out of the Blacks.” However, the Discogs personnel records for the Defunkt EP (Island Records, 1980 — ID 662290) show a substantially different lineup from Live In New York (ROIR, 1981): the Defunkt EP features Kelvyn Bell, Martin Aubert, Melvin Gibbs, Ronnie Burrage, and Martin Fischer, while the Live In New York lineup features Richie Harrison, Colin Wade, Bern Nix, and Tomás Doncker — few of whom are on the Defunkt EP.

Conflict: Web sources say Defunkt emerged from or shared a lineup with the Blacks. Discogs credits on the 1980 Island EP show a different core lineup. The most defensible reading: Bowie organised the Blacks personnel pool (Harrison, Wade, Thomas, Mulkerin) from his own networks, and those players circulated between the two projects — but Defunkt as released on record had a distinct core lineup by 1980. The Jazzwise quote (“opening shows for James — that is how Defunkt began”) positions Defunkt as a parallel project opening for the Blacks, not as the same band under two names.

Critical Reception of Bowie’s Work with Chance

Lester Bangs, writing in Musician (April–May 1980), attended a James Chance performance and described Bowie’s trombone playing as “an absolute motherfucker” — a distinctive notice in Bangs’ idiom that signified serious instrumental authority. (don-armstrong.com, “Listening to the Unlistenable: Mediating James Chance Pt. 4,” 2024, citing Bangs, Musician, 1980)

A review of Sax Maniac by the Daily News’s Hugh Wyatt described Chance’s saxophone on the album as “refreshing madness” that “defy[ies] all music tradition,” with Robert Palmer’s New York Times review noting the album “evidences considerable growth” with improved horn arrangements — a growth partly attributed to the Sax Maniac horn section Bowie helped constitute. (don-armstrong.com, 2024)

The Vinyl District’s review of Sax Maniac noted Bowie and John Mulkerin as “both of NYC Downtowners Defunkt,” positioning their involvement as lending instrumental credibility to the session. (thevinyldistrict.com, “Graded on a Curve: James White and the Blacks, Sax Maniac”)

Racial Politics — BAG Background and the No Wave Scene

Bowie’s BAG background is directly relevant to how his participation in the Chance project can be read. BAG was a Black-led collective that formed partly from frustration with racial discrimination in the arts. From the Jazzwise interview: “I got involved in the Black Artist Group in St. Louis, with Oliver Lake and Bobo Shaw, at a very early age. I was fifteen.” (jazzwise.com) This formation — Black avant-garde collective, commitment to Black cultural ownership — preceded by nearly a decade his work in a band led by a white musician named “James White and the Blacks.”

The PopMatters piece frames Bowie’s involvement in explicitly political terms: he “asserted the right of Black musicians to participate in a style of music rooted in Black jazz.” (popmatters.com, 2020) In this reading, Bowie was not serving Chance’s project — he was exercising ownership over a tradition.

Bowie’s own summary of the scene’s ethos: “All of the no wave bands were a rebellious family for change.” (josephbowie.com, 2020) No ambivalence toward the scene itself is documented in his public statements; his identification with the “rebellious family” framing suggests he saw the No Wave project as shared, not as Chance’s private appropriation.

Whether his BAG-era commitment to Black cultural sovereignty created internal tension with working under the name “James White and the Blacks” is not addressed in any available interview.

After 1982

No documented ongoing collaboration after Sax Maniac (1982). Bowie relocated to St. Croix to recover from heroin addiction and returned to New York in 1985. Bowie subsequently referred to Chance as “his former employer” — a characterisation that suggests the relationship was primarily professional in retrospect. (Wikipedia, Joseph Bowie) The Jazzwise interview frames it as a job that led to Defunkt, not as a sustained partnership. No joint recordings, touring credits, or interviews together after Sax Maniac are documented in any available source.

Defunkt vs. James White — Critical Framing

Both Defunkt and James White and the Blacks operated in the same downtown funk-punk-jazz space at the same time (1979–1982), with overlapping personnel. Critics have generally treated them as complementary rather than competing — two expressions of the same cultural collision, one Black-led (Defunkt), one white-fronted (the Blacks). The analytical distinction that emerged in later criticism is that Defunkt represented Black musicians claiming the space, while James White represented a white musician appropriating the same musical tradition but with a self-awareness about the appropriative act that set him apart from unreflective imitators.

Lucy Sante (The Baffler, 2024) characterised the Blacks musicians as “crack professionals” but “hirelings, not collaborators,” arguing the late recordings lacked the “warmth and singular wildness” of the original Contortions — a reading that implicitly diminishes Bowie and the others’ artistic agency in that context. (thebaffler.com, 2024) The don-armstrong.com analysis foregrounds Bowie’s participation as symbolically significant — a Black musician asserting ownership over the jazz roots the white bandleader was drawing on.

Open Questions

  • AACM/BAG relationship: Was Bowie formally affiliated with the AACM (Chicago), or only with BAG (St. Louis)? Discogs lists no AACM releases as primary credits, but the BAG/AACM parallel was close. Needs web/print confirmation.
  • Route to James Chance: [Partially resolved — web research May 2026] The James Chance official biography states Bowie organised the first Blacks lineup. The PopMatters/Jazzwise interviews confirm Bowie “got a job” with Chance and brought in musicians from his own networks. The specific introduction mechanism (who introduced Bowie to Chance, what year the first performance was) remains unconfirmed.
  • Defunkt founding date: Discogs dates the first release to 1980, but Defunkt is often cited as forming 1978–1979. Confirmed only that the Island Records EP is from 1980.
  • “Nam-Myoho-Renge-Kyo” alias: Discogs lists this as an alias for Bowie (ID unresolved). Context unclear — possibly a Buddhist practice name. No releases under this name in the DB.
  • Byron Bowie (ID 363770): A third Bowie brother (saxophone/flute); produced the Defunkt EP and played on the Razor’s Edge single. Deserves own documentation.
  • Bahnamous Bowie (ID 299640): Appears on Joseph Bowie’s Defunkt Soul compilation. Relation to Joseph unclear from Discogs — possibly another family member.
  • Discogs coverage gaps: Many of Bowie’s post-2000 European releases (Netherlands-based work, big band records, French projects) are sparsely credited. The full scope of his later career would benefit from web research.
