John Lurie

Born: December 14, 1952, Minneapolis, Minnesota
Role: Saxophonist, composer, actor, painter, filmmaker
Discogs artist ID: 143566

American musician, actor, visual artist, and filmmaker. Co-founder and leader of The Lounge Lizards. A central figure in the early 1980s NYC downtown scene — active simultaneously in avant-garde jazz, No Wave, and independent film. Led John Lurie National Orchestra in the 1990s. Extensive career as a film actor, primarily with Jim Jarmusch. After Lyme disease curtailed music and acting from around 2000, pivoted to painting as primary practice, including the HBO series Painting with John (2021–2023). Released music under the fictional alias Marvin Pontiac. Brother of pianist Evan Lurie.

Biography

Minneapolis and early moves (1952–1977)

Born in Minneapolis to a mother who was Welsh and an artist, and a father who was half Russian Jewish and half Sicilian. Grew up in New Orleans, Louisiana, and Worcester, Massachusetts, alongside siblings Evan and Liz. In high school played basketball and harmonica; sat in with blues musicians Mississippi Fred McDowell and Canned Heat in 1968. Briefly played harmonica in a Boston band, then switched to guitar and saxophone. Hitchhiked to Berkeley, California, before moving to New York City in 1974. Performed his first saxophone solo at London’s Acme Gallery. Wikipedia, as of 2026-06-26

NYC downtown — The Lounge Lizards and No Wave (1978–1985)

In 1978, Lurie co-founded The Lounge Lizards with his brother Evan Lurie, initially playing saxophone alongside Arto Lindsay (guitar), Steve Piccolo (bass), Anton Fier (drums), and Evan on keyboards. The group emerged from the No Wave scene and was described by Lurie himself as “fake jazz” — ironic, deadpan, borrowing jazz vocabulary while subverting it. The 1981 debut LP was produced by Teo Macero and released on Editions EG.

During this period Lurie became embedded in the wider downtown milieu — friends with Jean-Michel Basquiat, John Zorn, James Chance, and the experimental film world around Jim Jarmusch. Wikipedia, as of 2026-06-26

Around 1980, Lurie made a cameo in Jarmusch’s student film Permanent Vacation. The genesis of Stranger Than Paradise (1984) grew out of this friendship: when Jarmusch received leftover film stock from Wim Wenders’s The State of Things (1982), Lurie suggested the plot structure — his character as a Hungarian-American, his cousin visiting from Hungary, and their friend. Lurie has stated: “The very first part of Stranger Than Paradise was completely my idea.” Their process was genuinely collaborative: “everybody was working together in a creative way.” Damien Love interview

Acting peak and film scores (1984–1998)

Through the mid-1980s Lurie was one of the most distinctive presences in American independent film, primarily through Jarmusch. He also acted in major studio productions (Wim Wenders, Martin Scorsese, David Lynch) and contributed as a film composer. His scores drew on the same chamber-jazz vocabulary as the Lounge Lizards, often performed by close collaborators.

Fishing with John (1991)

Lurie created, wrote, directed, and starred in a six-episode television series in which he takes celebrity friends on fishing trips. Since Lurie has no expert fishing knowledge, the interest lies in the interactions and in narrator Robb Webb’s absurdist, off-topic voice-over. Originally aired on Bravo, November–December 1991; later re-ran on IFC. Released on DVD by Criterion Collection in 1999; now on the Criterion Channel. The series earned a cult following and is a spiritual predecessor to Painting with John.

Lyme disease — career interruption (2000–present)

Lurie began experiencing neurological symptoms around 1994; by 2000 he was severely debilitated. He attributes his condition to chronic Lyme disease, diagnosed after years of physician disagreement (“eight different purveyors of contemporary medicine”). He has described being told at one point he had a year to live. The illness effectively ended his performing and acting career. From approximately 2004 onward he devoted 10–12 hours a day to painting. Wikipedia; Artnet News

Visual art and Painting with John (2003–present)

Lurie had been painting since the mid-1970s — early works in watercolor and pencil. After Lyme disease curtailed music, painting became his primary output. In the 2000s he added oil to his practice. Exhibition history from 2003 onward includes: Anton Kern Gallery (New York, 2004), Galerie Daniel Blau (Munich), P.S.1 Contemporary Art Center (New York), Montreal Museum of Fine Arts, and Watari Museum of Contemporary Art (Tokyo). MoMA has acquired works for its permanent collection. Wikipedia

A watercolor, Bear Surprise, became a viral meme in Russia in 2006, known locally as “Preved.” Wikipedia

Published art books:

  • Learn To Draw (Walther König, 2006)

Music

The Lounge Lizards (1978–1998)

See The Lounge Lizards for full discography and personnel. The Lurie brothers were the only constant members across approximately twenty years and many lineup changes. Lurie composed the majority of the repertoire. Anton Fier, Marc Ribot, Arto Lindsay, Dougie Bowne, and others cycled through.

Key LP titles: Lounge Lizards (1981, Editions EG) · No Pain for Cakes (1987, Antilles) · Voice of Chunk (1988, Milan) · Queen of All Ears (1998, Strange & Beautiful Music).

John Lurie National Orchestra (1990s)

A smaller trio-based ensemble: Lurie on alto and soprano saxophones, G. Calvin Weston on drums, and Billy Martin on congas, timbales, and small percussion. Heavily improvised. Two known releases:

Marvin Pontiac (1999–2017)

Lurie created a fictional persona — Marvin Pontiac, an African-Jewish musician — as a vehicle to release vocal music without it seeming “ostentatious or pretentious” under his own name. The character came with a fabricated biography, a cover photograph purportedly of Pontiac, and a tragic life story. Elmore Leonard’s 2002 novel Tishomingo Blues featured detailed descriptions of the fictional character.

Writing

Memoir: The History of Bones (Penguin Random House / Random House, August 17, 2021, 435 pp.) — covers Lurie’s life through New Year’s Eve 1989, focusing on the NYC downtown scene, friendships with Jean-Michel Basquiat, and “conflicts with Jim Jarmusch.” Reviews describe it as a portrait of the East Village and 1980s downtown milieu. Sources: Penguin Random House; NYS Music review
