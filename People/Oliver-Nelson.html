Oliver Nelson

Biography

Early Life and First Sessions (1932–1958)

Oliver Edward Nelson grew up in St. Louis, Missouri, in a musical family. He was playing professionally while still in high school, working with the Jeter-Pillars Orchestra, George Hudson, and Eddie Randle. After military service he studied composition and theory at Washington University (1954–57) and Lincoln University (1957–58), building a rigorous theoretical foundation that would distinguish him from most jazz players of his generation.

His first Discogs-documented credit dates to 1955: an uncredited alto saxophone part on a Vee Jay Records session. By 1958 he was conducting for Nat King Cole on Capitol Records in Italy — an arranger’s credit, not a sideman job — which signals how quickly his skills were recognized beyond pure performance.

Prestige / New Jazz Period (1959–1961)

Nelson moved to New York and began recording as a leader for Prestige’s New Jazz imprint from 1959. His debut Meet Oliver Nelson (New Jazz, 1959) established him as a post-bop tenor voice. The years 1960–61 were prolific: ‘Trane Whistle, Taking Care Of Business, and Lem’s Beat all appeared on New Jazz; Straight Ahead followed in 1961.

During this stretch he also appeared as a sideman on Prestige, Riverside, and Tru-Sound dates alongside Lem Winchester, Eddie “Lockjaw” Davis, and others. He was simultaneously developing as an arranger, scoring reed sections for sessions on Mercury and Prestige.

Impulse! Peak: The Blues and the Abstract Truth (1961)

In February 1961 Nelson recorded what would become his defining work for Impulse!, produced by Creed Taylor and engineered by Rudy Van Gelder. The album “The Blues and the Abstract Truth” (Impulse!, A-5) placed Nelson in a remarkable group (see dedicated section below) and produced “Stolen Moments,” a composition that would prove extraordinarily durable. The album sold steadily and cemented Nelson’s status.

A companion Impulse! LP, More Blues and the Abstract Truth (1965), returned to similar territory with a larger orchestra. He recorded several other Impulse! albums through the late 1960s: Sound Pieces (1967), Happenings (1966), Oliver Nelson Plays Michelle (1966), and the Alfie film score (1966).

Big-Band Arranger (1962–1968)

From 1962 onwards Nelson’s Discogs credits shift decisively toward “Arranged By” and “Conductor.” He became one of the most in-demand arrangers in mainstream jazz and pop, working for Verve with Jimmy Smith (Bashin’, 1962; Monster, 1965; Hoochie Cooche Man, 1966), Wes Montgomery (Jimmy & Wes: The Dynamic Duo, 1967; Goin’ Out Of My Head, 1966), and others. His chart for the Walk on the Wild Side hit (Jimmy Smith, Verve, 1962) crossed over to pop radio.

He also scored or contributed arrangements to film and TV work from this period: Satan in High Heels (1962), The Seventh Dawn (1964), the Alfie soundtrack (Impulse!, 1966), and Zigzag (MGM, 1970).

Flying Dutchman / Hollywood Phase (1969–1975)

Nelson relocated to Los Angeles ca. 1967–68, becoming a full-time TV and film composer. His primary album label during this phase was Flying Dutchman: Black, Brown and Beautiful (1970), Afrique (1971), In Berlin (1971), Swiss Suite (1972), and Skull Session (1975). These albums moved between post-bop, free jazz, and orchestral jazz with a social-justice undertone (Black, Brown and Beautiful addressed Black American experience directly).

TV credits included Ironside and The Six Million Dollar Man — the latter referenced on a 1998 European compilation ($ix Million Dollar Man, Epidrome). He also arranged Louis Armstrong’s final studio recordings: Louis Armstrong and His Friends (Philips, 1970). The Lady Sings the Blues Motown soundtrack (1972) carries his arrangement credit.

Nelson died of a heart attack in Los Angeles on October 28, 1975, at 43. His final credited leader date as primary performer was Stolen Moments (East Wind, Japan, 1975).

Context

This session brought together six musicians who each had or would have major careers in their own right. Eric Dolphy’s flute and alto contributions are the most distinctive voice alongside Nelson’s tenor. Bill Evans was fresh off his work with Miles Davis’s Kind of Blue (1959). Freddie Hubbard was 23. The album’s central composition, “Stolen Moments,” became a jazz standard and later a sample source — most prominently Telefon Tel Aviv’s rework for Remixes Compiled (2007).

Scene Connections

  • Impulse! — primary label 1961–1968; part of the label’s core jazz roster alongside Coltrane, Archie Shepp, Charles Mingus
  • New Jazz — Prestige sub-label where Nelson debuted as leader (1959–1961)
  • Prestige — also released several Nelson dates; New Jazz was a Prestige imprint (ID: 76075)
  • Flying Dutchman — Bob Thiele’s label, Nelson’s main outlet 1969–1975
  • Creed Taylor — produced Blues and the Abstract Truth; later founded CTI Records
  • Rudy Van Gelder — engineered virtually all the early Prestige/Impulse! sessions
  • Eric Dolphy — co-star of Blues and the Abstract Truth; both were central to the early Impulse! aesthetic
  • Bill Evans — pianist on Blues and the Abstract Truth; otherwise primarily a Blue Note/Riverside artist
  • Telefon Tel Aviv — reworked “Stolen Moments” for Remixes Compiled (2007); arrangement by Joshua Eustis with the Loyola University Chamber Orchestra
  • Wes Montgomery — Nelson arranged several of Montgomery’s Verve albums; probably the most commercially successful arrangement work
  • Jimmy Smith — Nelson arranged Bashin’ (1962), Monster (1965), Hoochie Cooche Man (1966) and more at Verve
  • Louis Armstrong — Nelson arranged Armstrong’s final studio LP Louis Armstrong and His Friends (Philips, 1970)
