Gerard Cosloy

Gerard Cosloy

Oct 16, 20269 min read

Gerard Cosloy

Co-owner, Matador Records. Discogs ID: 584171. Also listed as a member of Dustdevils on Discogs — played bass on their final release Extant (Matador, 1996).

Former manager of Homestead Records — one of the defining American underground labels of the 1980s (releasing early records by Sonic Youth, Big Black, Dinosaur Jr., Nick Cave). Joined Chris Lombardi at Matador as co-owner in 1990. The Homestead-to-Matador lineage is the backbone of American indie rock’s institutional memory in this period. The early Matador aesthetic — lo-fi, literary, anti-commercial — bears Cosloy’s Homestead fingerprints.

Current Status (as of 2025–2026)

Cosloy remains a co-owner of Matador Records. The official Matador info page states: “The label continues to be run by Patrick Amory, Chris Lombardi and Gerard Cosloy.” The ownership structure is described as Lombardi and Cosloy as co-owners (founders), Amory as co-owner and president, with Beggars Group holding 50% of the label. (matadorrecords.com/pages/info, accessed 2025)

A Beggars Group announcement covering recent staff promotions (Alex Keague-Davies to global GM, Emma Buchanan to Director of A&R, etc.) described Lombardi, Cosloy, and Amory as having “uninterrupted ownership” of Matador “spanning over a quarter-century.” No change to Cosloy’s co-owner status was announced. (beggars.com)

Cosloy was included in Billboard’s 2026 Indie Power Players list alongside Lombardi and Amory, consistently representing Matador Records. (beggars.com/2026-billboard-indie-power-players)

Note on a circulating 4AD claim — REFUTED: AI-generated search summaries have repeatedly suggested Cosloy moved to a “US Label Head & Global Marketing Head” role at 4AD. Web research (July 2026) finds no basis for this claim in any primary source. The named role is held by two different people: Alyssa DeBonis (US Label Manager, 4AD) and Will Tompsett (Worldwide Head of Marketing, 4AD) — per Music Week coverage of 4AD’s management restructure. (musicweek.com/labels/read/4ad-appoints-uk-and-us-label-managers-sophie-hall-and-alyssa-debonis/085839, as of 2026-07-31)

The following sources were checked and contain no mention of Cosloy in connection with 4AD:

  • Wikipedia article on Gerard Cosloy (as of 2026-07-31): covers Homestead, Matador, 12XU only
  • Wikipedia article on 4AD (as of 2026-07-31): no mention of Cosloy at any point
  • Matador Records official info page (matadorrecords.com/pages/info): lists Cosloy as Matador co-owner only
  • Beggars Group press release on Matador promotions (beggars.com): Cosloy described as Matador co-owner
  • Two-part interview with Cosloy (boredout305 Tumblr, 2017): discusses Homestead, Matador, and 12XU — no 4AD mention

The 4AD-Cosloy claim appears to be an AI hallucination, most likely generated by conflating (a) the Beggars Group umbrella (which owns both Matador and 4AD), (b) Cosloy’s Matador role, and (c) the actual 4AD personnel in those job titles. No web source confirms Cosloy ever held a position at 4AD.

Early Career: Conflict Zine and Music Criticism

Born 1964 in Norwalk, Connecticut; raised in Wayland, Massachusetts. Cosloy began in the Boston hardcore punk scene during high school, organising shows and writing for the Wayland (MA) Town Crier (girls’ field hockey, per CSTB About page). While still in high school he launched Conflict, a mimeographed fanzine covering underground and alternative rock and art. (Wikipedia, as of 2026-08-13; fanzinehemorrhage.com)

Conflict ran from approximately 1979 to 1991 (with a 2013 revival for issue #53, per the Airship Daily piece referenced on Fanzine Hemorrhage). Issue #35 (March/April 1984) was among the last Boston issues, issue #36 (August/September 1984) was the final Boston issue before Cosloy relocated to New York. Issue #37 appeared 18 months later after a break. Issue #42 (1986) was published from 62 Avenue C, Manhattan. (fanzinehemorrhage.com) Fanzine Hemorrhage describes Conflict and Forced Exposure as “the only two 100% totally essential fanzines” of the late 1980s underground.

Cosloy also DJed at WFMU and WZBC (Boston College), and contributed to Maximum Rock’n’Roll, Smegma Journal, Chickfactor, and the High Wire. (cantstopthebleeding.com/about; Wikipedia) He attended UMass-Amherst briefly (September 1983 – January 1984) and worked as a record-store clerk. (stevemarantz.com interview, July 2008)

Cosloy was also active as a booking agent in the Boston area, promoting shows with bands including Sonic Youth and Salem 66. His network through Conflict gave him contacts in multiple cities simultaneously — a key advantage when he moved to the label side. (Wikipedia)

He also founded Conflict Records (separate from the zine), which issued two compilations titled Bands that Could be God I and II, plus various singles. (en-academic.com)

Homestead Records (1984–1990)

In summer 1984 Cosloy was recommended as a replacement for departing Homestead head Sam Berger. He was Homestead’s sole employee for much of 1984–1990, handling signing, radio, press, and shipping promos. The 1985–86 period produced defining releases from Sonic Youth, Dinosaur Jr., Big Black, Volcano Suns, Death of Samantha, and Green River. (Wikipedia)

It was through Conflict that Cosloy first encountered Pavement: Scott Kannberg sent the zine a copy of the Slay Tracks (1933–1969) EP for review, around 1989. This led directly to Cosloy bringing Pavement to Matador. (Wikipedia; Austin Chronicle)

Matador Records: A&R and Signing History

Founding context: Matador Records was founded in 1989 by Chris Lombardi, who had worked for Homestead’s distributor Dutch East India Trading. Cosloy and Lombardi became carpool buddies. The very first Matador release was an H.P. Zinker twelve-inch, followed by Dustdevils and then Superchunk or Railroad Jerk (Cosloy’s own recollection was uncertain on the order). (Spotify for Artists)

Cosloy joined as partner in 1990. Their shared founding philosophy, per Cosloy: “There was a real sense on our part that if we didn’t document certain projects, they simply weren’t going to exist.” (Spotify for Artists)

Cosloy vs. Lombardi — what the sources say (and don’t say): Multiple interviews and profiles consistently present decision-making at Matador as collaborative. Neither Cosloy nor Lombardi publicly credits individual signings to one person versus the other; both use “we” when discussing signings. The Spotify for Artists label profile describes them as “Matador’s chief toreros.” No contemporaneous or retrospective source divides the roster into Cosloy-signed versus Lombardi-signed artists. (Spotify for Artists; lasvegassun.com, July 2010; boredout305 Tumblr interviews, 2017)

What can be attributed to Cosloy’s specific advocacy (from interviews):

  • Pavement — Cosloy discovered them via Conflict when Kannberg submitted Slay Tracks. The relationship traces directly to Cosloy’s pre-Matador network. (Wikipedia)
  • Spoon — In the boredout305 interview Cosloy specifically champions Spoon’s third album as “really amazing” and describes personally negotiating European distribution rights when the band was between labels (Merge handled US). (boredout305 part one)
  • Chris Brokaw / Come — Cosloy describes having followed Brokaw since his Oberlin days; Matador “had worked with Come for a long time.” (boredout305 part one)
  • The Clean / David Kilgour — Cosloy notes he “was working with The Clean” dating back to the Homestead era, indicating a long personal relationship. The Clean’s Matador releases and Kilgour’s solo records on 12XU flow from this connection. (boredout305 part two)
  • Fucked Up — In a 2010 Las Vegas Sun interview Cosloy specifically discusses championing Fucked Up as an unconventional signing for Matador’s profile, citing exceptional artistry as the driver. (lasvegassun.com, September 2010)
  • New Zealand/Flying Nun scene — Patrick Amory (Matador’s third co-owner, who joined 1994) introduced Cosloy to the Flying Nun world. Cosloy acknowledges Amory as the gateway for that roster strand. (boredout305 part two)

Lombardi pre-Cosloy signings (1989–1990): Before Cosloy joined, Lombardi had already signed H.P. Zinker, Dustdevils, Railroad Jerk, and Superchunk. Teenage Fanclub’s A Catholic Education (1990) was the first release to put Matador on wider radar; the resulting cash flow enabled the Pavement signing. (Spotify for Artists; Wikipedia Matador Records)

Cosloy’s signing philosophy (stated): “If there’s a band that has five labels chasing them, they really don’t need me.” He avoids acts pursuing major-label interest or heavy management, preferring direct artist relationships. (terminal-boredom.com interview)

Geographic base: By autumn 2004, Cosloy relocated to Austin, Texas, while maintaining Matador co-ownership. He had previously spent time managing UK operations. (en-academic.com; terminal-boredom.com)

12XU Label

Cosloy founded 12XU as a boutique label, separate from Matador, with the first releases appearing around 2001–2004 (sources give a range; the boredout305 interview says “early 2000s”). The goal was explicitly not to replicate Matador or Homestead but to be “smaller, leaner and meaner.” In roughly 25 years the label has issued over 150 titles. Roster includes David Kilgour, Chris Brokaw, The Gotobeds, Mope Grooves, Water Damage, Lewsburg, Weak Signal, OBN IIIs, XETAS, Wolf Eyes, Uniform, Ed Kuepper, The Hobknobs (Rotterdam), and many others — with a strong Austin, TX concentration. (terminal-boredom.com; 12xurecs.bandcamp.com; freeformfreakout.com 12XU podcast feature)

Can’t Stop the Bleeding (CSTB) Blog

Cosloy runs Can’t Stop the Bleeding (cantstopthebleeding.com), a daily blog primarily covering sports with broader culture, music, and politics. The About page describes it as “written and edited daily by Gerard Cosloy.” The blog was active by at least 2003 (referenced favorably in sports blog circles that year per Steve Marantz interview context; Cosloy’s interview with Marantz was July 2008). No primary source provides an exact launch date. (cantstopthebleeding.com/about; stevemarantz.com)

Regular contributors include Ben Schwartz, Jason Cohen, David Roth, Chuck Meehan, Rob Warmowski, and Liz Clayton. The About page cites editorial influence from “the late Clyde Kane, Herb Caen and Todd Jones.” Cosloy deliberately keeps CSTB separate from his music industry role — he told Marantz: “my rock biz activities are very well documented elsewhere and are in no way related to CSTB.” (cantstopthebleeding.com/about; stevemarantz.com)

A notable incident: on 11 August 2009, Cosloy posted “The End Of CSTB HQ,” accompanied by a damage photograph. The Village Voice ran a piece titled “R.I.P. Gerard Cosloy’s Lovely Austin Home” on the same date, referring to his Austin home burning down with “untold amounts of alt-rock memorabilia” and causing approximately $500,000 in damage. (villagevoice.com; cantstopthebleeding.com/the-end-of-cstb-hq)

CSTB remains active as of 2025. (cantstopthebleeding.com/2025)

Musical Projects

Per Wikipedia and en-academic, Cosloy performed with:

  • Air Traffic Controllers — band affiliation (details sparse in sources)
  • GG Allin — brief, unspecified collaboration
  • Envelope — New York quartet, 1992–1996, which he fronted
  • Dustdevils — filled in as bassist during Mark Ibold’s Pavement tenure; played on Extant (Matador, 1996)
