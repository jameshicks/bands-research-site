Bruce Langhorne

Bruce Langhorne

Aug 16, 202610 min read

Bruce Langhorne

Born: May 11, 1938, Tallahassee, Florida, USA Died: April 14, 2017 Real name: Joseph Bruce Langhorne Discogs ID: 271932 Name variations: B. Langhorne, Bruce Langhorn, Bruce Langborne, Bruce Langthorn, J. Bruce Langhorne, W.B. Langhorne

American folk multi-instrumentalist and session guitarist, central to the Greenwich Village folk scene from the early 1960s onward. Died Venice, California, April 14, 2017, of kidney failure; survived by wife Janet Bachelor. Best known for his guitar work on Bob Dylan’s Bringing It All Back Home (1965) — Dylan is reported to have said Langhorne’s oversized Turkish tambourine inspired the imagery of “Mr. Tambourine Man.” Beyond Dylan, Langhorne played on a sweeping range of the era’s key folk and folk-rock recordings, appearing on sessions for Joan Baez, Richard & Mimi Fariña, Buffy Sainte-Marie, Tom Rush, Gordon Lightfoot, Odetta, Carolyn Hester, Pete Seeger, and the Clancy Brothers over more than a decade of continuous session work. In 1971 he composed and performed the score for Peter Fonda’s film The Hired Hand, one of the era’s more distinctive film soundscapes, reissued by Blast First Petite in 2007.

Biography

Early Life and Folk Scene Entry

Born in Tallahassee, raised in Harlem. At age 12 a cherry bomb accident cost him three fingers; he switched from violin to guitar. He entered the folk scene through Camp Woodland, where he encountered Pete Seeger, then became an accompanist to blues singer Brother John Sellers at Gerde’s Folk City in Greenwich Village — the connections made there flowed into his entire session career.

Early Session Career — Greenwich Village, 1961–1963

Langhorne’s session career began in 1961 with a co-billed recording alongside Pete Seeger and the Clancy Brothers (A Spontaneous Performance Recording!, Columbia), where he appeared as a primary credited guitarist. That same year he played on The Folk Singers Of Washington Square (Continental Records), as a member of the group of that name — a loose collective that also included Sandy Bull and Logan English. The Washington Square folk scene was the nexus through which most of his subsequent connections flowed.

His first high-profile session came when Carolyn Hester brought him into Columbia Studio A in 1962 for her self-titled album — a session also attended by the then-unknown Bob Dylan, who played harmonica on three tracks, and produced by John Hammond. This session effectively placed Langhorne inside the Columbia folk orbit.

Through 1962–1963 he also played on early Clancy Brothers sessions (Hearty And Hellish, The Moonshiner, The Boys Won’t Leave The Girls Alone — CBS) and appeared on recordings by the Chad Mitchell Trio (Blowin’ In The Wind, Kapp Records, 1963), and Odetta (Sings Folk Songs, RCA Victor, 1963).

The Dylan Sessions, 1962–1965

Langhorne appears on two Dylan albums that are among the most thoroughly documented recordings of the era. The Freewheelin’ Bob Dylan (Columbia, 1963, released 1963) includes him on guitar for two tracks (B3 and D1 of the vinyl sequence). More significantly, he is present across much of Bringing It All Back Home (Columbia, 1965) — the album on which Dylan’s electric turn was initiated. The Discogs credits for the original pressing are sparse on individual track breakdowns, but Langhorne is confirmed as a key participant, credited on guitar. The Dylan Discogs profile (ID 59792) references master 3823 (Bringing It All Back Home) explicitly in the artist bio as an album where Langhorne comes to the fore on tracks including “She Belongs To Me,” “Love Minus Zero/No Limit,” and “Mr. Tambourine Man.”

Producer on both Dylan albums: Tom Wilson (2) (on Bringing It All Back Home) and John Hammond (on Freewheelin’).

Dylan in the Biograph liner notes on Langhorne’s tambourine: “He had this gigantic tambourine… It was as big as a wagon wheel.” More broadly: “If you had Bruce playing with you, that’s all you would need to do just about anything.”

Vanguard Sessions — Richard & Mimi Fariña and Others, 1965–1967

The Vanguard label became his most consistent home as a session player through the mid-1960s. He appeared on both Richard & Mimi Fariña albums:

  • Celebrations For A Grey Day (Vanguard, 1965): Electric guitar on tracks A7, B2, B5. Fellow players included Russ Savakus (bass) and Charlie Smalls (piano).
  • Reflections In A Crystal Wind (Vanguard, 1965): Electric guitar and tambourine across most of the album. Players included Felix Pappalardi (electric bass), Russ Savakus (double bass), Al Rogers (drums), Charlie Smalls (piano and celesta), and John Paul Hammond (harmonica). Richard Fariña arranged and composed; Mimi Fariña played guitar.

He also appeared on Joan Baez’s Farewell, Angelina (1965) in the electric guitar role, as well as multiple Odetta albums on RCA Victor in this period (It’s A Mighty World 1964, Odetta Sings Of Many Things 1964, Odetta Sings Dylan 1965, Odetta In Japan 1966) and on Joan Baez’s Joan (Vanguard, 1968).

In 1967 he contributed tambourine and bells to Peter Walker’s Rainy Day Raga (Vanguard) — a raga-influenced acoustic guitar album with liner notes by Timothy Leary.

Elektra and Other Labels, 1966–1968

Langhorne played electric guitar and second guitar across both sides of Tom Rush’s Take A Little Walk With Me (Elektra, 1966), one of the earlier recordings to document the folk-rock transition at Elektra. Fellow players: Harvey Brooks (electric bass), Bobby Gregg (drums), and Al Kooper (lead guitar and celesta). Kooper also wrote the liner notes.

He also appears on Tom Rush’s The Circle Game (Elektra, 1968), one of Rush’s most celebrated recordings, and on Gordon Lightfoot’s Lightfoot (United Artists, 1966) in the second guitar role.

Other 1967–1968 sessions: Steve Gillette (Vanguard, 1967); Jessie Mae Hemphill’s The Bag I’m In (ATCO, 1962); multiple Buffy Sainte-Marie sessions on Vanguard (Celebrations For A Grey Day era through the Best Of compilation’s electric guitar credits on tracks C2, D2, D4).

Late 1960s Production and Broader Work

By 1968–1969 Langhorne had moved beyond pure session guitar into production. He produced two releases on Reprise Records: Young Brigham by Young Brigham (Reprise, 1968), where he also played tabla, and a single Foggy River / Connection (Reprise, 1969). He also appeared as producer on The Buzzard / Keep On Groovin’ (A&M Records, 1968).

In 1969 he contributed electric bass, guitar, and tabla/hand drums to American Avatar (Reprise, 1969) and appeared on The Great Electric Experiment Is Over (Reprise, 1969) on percussion. The Moonstone (Verve Forecast, 1969) credits him as musician.

He appeared on John Sebastian’s solo debut (Reprise, 1971) as a player.

The Hired Hand Score and Film Work, 1971–1980

Langhorne’s principal credit as composer and sole performer. Peter Fonda’s western The Hired Hand (1971) was recorded under distinctly informal conditions: Langhorne brought a home-movie camera copy of the film to his Laurel Canyon house and improvised the score while his girlfriend Natalie Mucyn recorded him on a Revox reel-to-reel, with no prior mixing experience. Instruments used: Farfisa organ, piano, banjo, fiddle, harmonica, recorder, Appalachian dulcimer, lap steel. The result is a score characterized by desolate space and minimal motion — The Wire called it “perhaps the loneliest film score ever”; Dusted compared it to John Fahey, Sandy Bull, and Ry Cooder.

Original release: Scissor Tail Records (ST07), 1971. Reissued 2007 by Blast First Petite (PTYT 002), mastered by George Madaraz; Langhorne wrote the liner notes. The Blast First Petite release is the version that introduced the score to a wider audience; a Soul Jazz edition followed.

Langhorne went on to score four more films, several in collaboration with director Jonathan Demme:

Later Session Work and Africa Connection

In 1986 Langhorne appeared on Dance To The Beat Of My Drum (Blue Heron Records) playing agogô, guitar, and vocals — an African percussion and vocal ensemble recording. He continued this thread with Babatunde Olatunji’s Drums Of Passion: The Invocation (Rykodisc, 1988), again on agogô and vocals, alongside an array of African percussionists.

His last known significant session credit in the DB is on Monte Dunn & Karen Cruz (Fallout, 2008) on bass.

Later Life

Around 1980 Langhorne left Los Angeles for Hawaii, where he farmed macadamia nuts. He returned to the mainland around 1985 and settled in Venice Beach. A 1992 diabetes diagnosis redirected his energy: he founded Brother Bru-Bru’s African Hot Sauce (8,400 Scoville, low sodium, health-oriented), which became his primary public identity in his later years. The brand was sold by his family in 2022.

In 2006 he suffered a stroke — documented in a contemporaneous tribute by Jonathan Demme — that left him unable to play guitar. A 2011 release titled Tambourine Man assembled from archival recordings (keyboards and Caribbean percussion) was his final release. He died April 14, 2017, Venice, California, of kidney failure, age 78, survived by wife Janet Bachelor. An “Invisible Legend” documentary about his career was in production at the time of his death.
