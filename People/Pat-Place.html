Biography

Background

Place grew up in the Chicago suburbs, where she absorbed soul radio — Curtis Mayfield, Sam and Dave — alongside rock. She studied art at Northern Illinois University and completed final credits at Skidmore College’s summer programme in Saratoga Springs, where she earned a scholarship to videotape visiting artists including Lynda Benglis, Larry Poons, and Jackie Winsor. She earned a BFA in painting and sculpture.

She moved to New York in 1975, initially subletting in the loft of curator and critic Lucy Lippard in SoHo. She then worked at Pearl Paint on Canal Street for approximately a year before securing a rent-stabilised apartment on Sixth Avenue between Spring and Prince Streets — which she still holds. She arrived as a visual artist, not a musician. She did not play an instrument when she arrived, and was embedded in the downtown scene by 1976, attending shows at CBGB and the Ocean Club.

The East Hampton Star — “Pat Place: A Life in Music and Art” (December 2025)

The Contortions (1977–1978)

James Chance approached her at CBGB and asked if she played bass. She said yes despite having no idea how. One bass lesson went poorly; Chance suggested guitar instead. She borrowed a friend’s guitar. By the time of the Contortions’ first gig, she had been playing guitar for approximately three weeks.

She became the band’s slide guitarist, alongside Jody Harris (lead/rhythm guitar). She learned slide technique from China Burg of Mars — a widely shared approach within the no wave scene. Her dizzying, destabilising slide work contrasted with Harris’s more rhythmically grounded lines.

Original Contortions lineup (No New York era):

  • James Chance — saxophone, vocals
  • Jody Harris — guitar
  • Pat Place — slide guitar
  • George Scott III — bass
  • Don Christensen — drums
  • Adele Bertei — organ

The band played Max’s Kansas City, CBGB, and toured Toronto, Chicago, and Boston. The Contortions lasted approximately one and a half years in total.

Anya Phillips and the Band’s Dissolution

Anya Phillips came in as the band’s manager — and as James Chance’s romantic partner. According to Place, Phillips “had visions of James being this big star” and pushed to separate Chance from the collective, wanting to pay band members only session fees rather than as equal partners. This created serious conflict within the group. Place found herself caught between the band and management — serving as an uncomfortable go-between.

The breaking point came in Spring 1978 after a Paris gig: Phillips and Chance allegedly took the gig money and spent it on drugs rather than distributing it to the band. Place chose to leave with the other band members rather than remain in Chance’s orbit. She described the Contortions period as fuelled by “alcohol and drugs in that world” and burned out. She achieved sobriety in 1986.

China Burg and Mars

The player who taught Place slide guitar was Connie Burg — known within the scene as China Burg, later as Don Burg and Lucy Hamilton. Burg co-founded Mars in late 1975 alongside Sumner Crane (guitar/bass/vocals), Mark Cunningham (bass/guitar/vocals), and Nancy Arlen (drums). Like Place, Burg had no prior music training before picking up a guitar. Cunningham and Burg had met at college in Florida, where future DNA vocalist Arto Lindsay was also a student — a connection that made Mars and DNA part of the same pre-scene social network before either band had played a show.

Mars initially performed as China (after Burg’s nickname), debuting at CBGB in February 1977. They renamed themselves Mars in June 1977, reportedly to avoid confusion with Elton John’s backing group. They played approximately 30 gigs around Manhattan before disbanding in December 1978. Their final show was at Max’s Kansas City on 10 December 1978.

The CBGB and Max’s Kansas City circuits brought Mars into regular contact with the Contortions: the bands shared billing frequently and operated within the same tight social sphere. The PSF Pat Place interview confirms she watched Connie Burg play and was directly influenced by what she saw — Burg’s untrained, non-standard slide approach became the technique Place absorbed and brought to the Contortions.

After Mars dissolved, Burg learned bass clarinet to participate in Sumner Crane’s opera John Gavanti. She then collaborated with Lydia Lunch on The Drowning of Lucy Hamilton (1985, under the name Lucy Hamilton), engineered by Roli Mosimann and J.G. Thirlwell, recorded August–October 1984. She also worked with Mark Cunningham and others in Don King, and later with Alan Licht in Gerry Miles, and Andy Salcius in The Love Dogs. In 1992 she appeared on Steroid Maximus’s Gondwanaland (J.G. Thirlwell’s project).

Nancy Arlen died 17 September 2006 following heart surgery. Sumner Crane died of lymphoma 15 April 2003.

No New York (1978)

Label: Antilles Records | Producer: Brian Eno | Studio: Big Apple Studios, NYC

Contortions tracks (Side A): “Dish It Out,” “Flip Your Face,” “Jaded,” “I Can’t Stand Myself” (James Brown cover). Recorded entirely live, no overdubs — Eno’s intention was pure documentation. Place’s slide guitar is the destabilising texture throughout.

Bush Tetras: Formation (1979)

After leaving the Contortions, Place went through a period of burnout. She and Laura Kennedy (bass, from Detroit) had become close and moved in together. Dee Pop ran into Place after she left the Contortions: “I said, ‘I have a friend, you wanna get together?’ And she said, ‘I have a friend, Laura, you wanna get together?‘” The three jammed for six months without a vocalist. Adele Bertei (also ex-Contortions) briefly fronted the band but left after poor press.

Cynthia Sley was already in their social circle — she was making their clothes and cutting their hair. Shy, she had to be talked into fronting the band. Once she joined, the lineup was set: Sley (vocals), Place (guitar), Kennedy (bass), Dee Pop (drums).

First gig: 1 February 1980, Tier 3, NYC.

The band maintained a storefront at 1st Avenue and 1st Street as a dedicated rehearsal and composition space — a base that kept them constantly working.

Band name: Place wanted “Neon Tetras” (the fish); Pop wanted “Bush Babies” (the primate). They merged them. The band occasionally played secret gigs as “the Neon Babies.”

No Wave Cinema

Place was a recurrent presence in Vivienne Dick’s Super-8 films of the late 1970s. Dick is considered by film critic J. Hoberman the “quintessential No Wave filmmaker.” Confirmed Place appearances:

  • Guerillère Talks (1978, Super-8, 24 min.) — Unedited single-reel portraits of women in the scene; Place is one of the subjects alongside Ikue Mori (drummer, DNA), Lydia Lunch, and others. Proto-riot grrrl ethnography format: Dick described the camera as co-conspirator rather than voyeur.
  • She Had Her Gun All Ready (1978, Super-8, 28 min.) — Narrative short following two women from a Lower East Side kitchen to Coney Island. Place plays a “complacent, almost catatonic” figure opposite Lydia Lunch’s “demonic, aggressive” character. A film about female anger and obsessional identification.
  • Staten Island (1978) — Place appears in this film also. An androgynous figure in a silver suit on a post-apocalyptic littered Staten Island beach; music uses Telstar mixed with domestic kitchen clatter.

Place also appeared in Beth B and Scott B’s no wave film The Offenders (1980), alongside Lydia Lunch, Evan Lurie, and Kristian Hoffman. The Offenders was an episodic feature about kidnapping and abuse, originally screened serially at Max’s Kansas City and the Mudd Club before premiering at Film Forum on 6 March 1980.

Dick’s later 1979 film Beauty Becomes the Beast is centred on a performance by Lydia Lunch; Place’s involvement in that film is not confirmed by reliable sources.

Guitar Style

  • Radical inexperience as aesthetic: “I’m not trained. I just go on intuition. I try not to learn too much. We don’t want to ruin the primitive aspect of it all.”
  • With the Contortions: almost exclusively slide, producing a dizzying, tonal-but-atonal texture
  • With Bush Tetras: moved toward constructed chord voicings. Taught herself by jamming — developed “weird seventh jazz chords” and a rhythmic single-note approach. Learned the ninth chord (funk staple) from Jody Harris but deliberately avoided standard bar chords. Made up progressions; oriented playing rhythmically rather than melodically
  • Key effect: phase shifter, plus heavy distortion
  • “Too Many Creeps”: the guitar line is a repeating, distorted, rhythmically syncopated figure — closer to percussion than melody. Written in approximately five minutes at Bleecker Street Cinema
  • Influences: James Brown, Hamilton Bohannon — deconstructed funk rather than British post-punk
  • Performed with back to the audience — stemming from shyness about being watched, not arrogance
  • Her guitar style later cited as an influence by Kid Congo and Matt Verta-Ray (Talkhouse)

Visual Art (Parallel Career)

Place has maintained a serious parallel practice as a visual artist throughout:

  • Studied with Cindy Sherman, who selected Place’s photographs for a group show at Artists Space (c.1980)
  • Exhibited at 112 Greene Street; 1978 punk art exhibition in Washington DC
  • “The End” series (1981–present): 1,000+ photographs of movie end titles captured from television; exhibited at Jane Kim Gallery, 2008
  • “Rock and Roll Kisses” series: photographs of famous couples kissing
  • Represented by Jane Kim Gallery

Later Career

Joey’s Oscar (late 1980s–early 1990s): project with Brian Kelly and Julian Schnabel. Produced “She Is Dancing,” which appeared on the Basquiat soundtrack.

Maggie Estep (early–mid 1990s): played in Estep’s backing band during MTV appearances and a tour opening for Hole on the Live Through This era.

1995 reunion with original Bush Tetras lineup. 1997 LP Beauty Lies (Mercury/PolyGram), produced by Nona Hendryx.

After Dee Pop’s death (9 October 2021), rhythm section has been in flux. Steve Shelley (Sonic Youth) joined as drummer; produced They Live in My Head (Wharf Cat, 2023). Place and Sley remain the consistent core.
