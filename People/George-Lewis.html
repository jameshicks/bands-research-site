George Lewis

American avant-garde trombonist, composer, computer music pioneer, and scholar. Born July 14, 1952 in Chicago, IL. Real name: George Emanuel Lewis. Discogs artist ID: 141326. Also credited as: G. Lewis, George E. Lewis.

Core member of the AACM (Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians). Pivotal figure in the intersection of free improvisation, experimental composition, and interactive computer music. Later became Edwin H. Case Professor of American Music at Columbia University. Author of the definitive AACM history A Power Stronger Than Itself (University of Chicago Press, 2008).

Early Life and AACM Formation (1952–1976)

Lewis grew up in Chicago and encountered the AACM in his teens — the organization had been founded in 1965 by Muhal Richard Abrams and others on the South Side. He studied trombone and was shaped by AACM’s self-determination ethos: musicians composing, producing, and presenting their own work outside the mainstream industry. By the mid-1970s he was an established member of the AACM community alongside Roscoe Mitchell, Anthony Braxton, Lester Bowie, and others.

His first documented Discogs credit as a primary artist is the Roscoe Mitchell Quartet album on Sackville Recordings (1976), which he co-led. That same year he appeared on Anthony Braxton’s landmark Creative Orchestra Music 1976 (Arista, 1976) — a large-ensemble date that also featured Muhal Richard Abrams, Roscoe Mitchell, Dave Holland, Barry Altschul, and Wadada Leo Smith.

Solo Leader Work: Black Saint, Sackville, and Free Improvisation (1976–1985)

Lewis established himself as a solo voice almost immediately. His first fully solo leader record was The George Lewis Solo Trombone Record (Sackville, 1977) — unaccompanied trombone, one of the earliest such records in jazz history.

His label home in the late 1970s and early 1980s was Black Saint (Italy), which released some of his most important early work:

During this period Lewis was deeply embedded in the European free improvisation scene. He was a member of Company (2) — Derek Bailey’s legendary series of ad hoc improvisation meetings in London — alongside Derek Bailey, Evan Parker, and many others. He appears on their Incus releases.

New York Scene: Material, Yankees, and Downtown (1981–1985)

Lewis moved into the downtown New York experimental scene in the early 1980s, most prominently appearing on Material’s Memory Serves (Celluloid, 1981) — the fusion-era landmark produced by Bill Laswell and Martin Bisi. He contributes trombone alongside Henry Threadgill, Sonny Sharrock, Fred Frith, Billy Bang, Olu Dara, Michael Beinhorn, Charles K. Noyes, and Fred Maher.

The same year he played the PISA 1980 Improvisors’ Symposium (Incus, 1981).

In 1982 he recorded Yankees (Celluloid, 1982) — a trio improvisation with Derek Bailey and John Zorn, recorded by Martin Bisi. This is one of the key early documents of the downtown/European improv crossover.

Computer Music and the Voyager System (1987 onward)

Beginning in the mid-1980s Lewis devoted increasing attention to computer music and interactive systems. He developed Voyager — an interactive software system capable of real-time musical response to live performance. Voyager improvises with human musicians: it listens, interprets, and generates its own musical output in response. It became a long-running research project that Lewis has continued developing across decades.

The first Voyager recording was released in 1993 on the Avant label (Japan, A&R: John Zorn):

ReleaseYearLabelPersonnel
Voyager1993AvantLewis (trombone, computer); Roscoe Mitchell (alto/soprano sax); produced by David Wessel

Voyager made Lewis one of the pioneering figures in interactive AI music systems — preceding many of the field’s later developments by decades. His work in this area connected to the broader tradition of Musica Elettronica Viva (MEV), the Italian-American live electronics collective of which he was a member, alongside Alvin Curran, Frederic Rzewski, and Richard Teitelbaum.

News For Lulu / More News For Lulu (hat ART, 1988/1992)

One of the most celebrated projects of Lewis’s career is the trio with John Zorn and Bill Frisell, which reinterpreted hardcore bebop and hard bop compositions by figures like Sonny Clark, Hank Mobley, and Kenny Dorham:

ReleaseYearLabelNotes
News For Lulu1988hat ARTLewis, Zorn, Frisell; arrangements jointly credited to all three
More News For Lulu1992hat ARTSequel; live recordings from Paris and Basel

Lewis also made The Storming Of The Winter Palace (Intakt, 1988) — a quintet with Irene Schweizer, Joëlle Léandre, Maggie Nicols, and Günter Sommer.

European Work and Dutch/ICP Scene

Lewis recorded extensively with European players. Dutch Masters (Soul Note, 1992) was a quintet recording with Misha Mengelberg, Steve Lacy, Ernst Reijseger, and Han Bennink. He was a member of the ICP Orchestra (Misha Mengelberg’s Instant Composers Pool) and connected to the broader Dutch improvised music world.

He also appears on Derek Bailey’s Donaueschingen recordings (hat ART, 1994 — archival recording of the 1976 duo performance), documenting a long collaboration.

Later Projects and Academy (1990s–2021)

Lewis joined Columbia University’s faculty, where he became the Edwin H. Case Professor of American Music. His scholarly and compositional work continued in parallel:

  • Intakt Records — Swiss label that became a primary home for his European-scene work. He appears on multiple Intakt releases.
  • Sour Mash (Innova Recordings, 2010) — duo with composer/sound artist Marina Rosenfeld
  • Metamorphic Rock (Iorram Records, 2009) — UK release
  • Rainbow Family (Carrier Records, 2020) — Lewis credited for computer programming and liner notes; multi-artist
  • The Recombinant Trilogy (New Focus Recordings, 2021) — composed work; software by Damon Holzborn

ElectroAcoustic Septet

Lewis was a founding member of the ElectroAcoustic Septet, an ensemble combining analog improvisation with electronic processing, with members including Evan Parker, Ikue Mori, Ned Rothenberg, Okkyung Lee, Peter Evans, and Sam Pluta.
