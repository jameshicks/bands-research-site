Overview

John McKay (born 1957) was the self-taught guitarist on Siouxsie & the Banshees’ first two albums, joining in July 1977 as replacement for Peter Fenton. His tenure ended abruptly in September 1979 when he and drummer Kenny Morris walked out hours before a show on the Join Hands tour after a dispute over autograph-signing at a record store in Aberdeen; contemporaries praised his innovative guitar style as a formative influence on post-punk and goth rock. In 1982 he formed the trio Zor Gabor with his girlfriend and a collaborator named Dids, playing the Batcave circuit and occasionally supporting The Fall, but the project released only a single (1986) before going quiet in the late 1980s. His catalogue is concentrated on Polydor and Interfusion from the Banshees era, with later reissue appearances on Strange Fruit and Universal, and fresh activity on Rotten Fruit Records and Tiny Global Productions in the 2020s.

Notes (web) — Join Hands walkout: underlying tensions

The autograph-signing incident (7 September 1979, Other Record Shop, Union Street, Aberdeen) was the immediate trigger of the walkout but multiple sources, including McKay’s own words, point to deeper accumulated tensions.

McKay’s own account (Guitar World interview, 2025): McKay describes a physical confrontation as the final straw: “Siouxsie tried out her newfound kung fu on me and barged me in the back. That was enough – I walked out and Kenny came with me.” He frames the departure not as impulsive but as the product of a build-up: “No one person was responsible; I can’t really regret it. It was a build up between different characters.” He specifically identifies management as a wedge: “Various wedges driven between us, partly because of management.” A logistical failure on the Belfast date — gear not arriving, forcing the band to borrow equipment from The Cure for what McKay called “the first gig of the biggest tour we’d ever done” — was the proximate accumulation point: “The last couple of weeks were bad, culminating in the gear not getting to Northern Ireland for the Belfast gig… it was just ready to blow.” He describes the manager (referred to as Neil, i.e. Nils Stevenson) as “absolutely despotic with Siouxsie.” (Guitar World, retrieved 2026-10-29)

Kenny Morris’s account: Morris confirmed a sustained breakdown on the band’s internal dynamics rather than a single incident: “We disagreed over so many things and each time we were outnumbered by three to two.” He added: “At the end there was almost no more communication with Siouxsie Sioux, bassist Steven Severin and their manager Nils Stevenson.” The three-to-two split (Siouxsie, Severin, and manager Stevenson on one side; McKay and Morris on the other) recurs across accounts as the structural fault line. (Wikipedia — Kenny Morris (drummer), as of 2026-10-29)

Join Hands album context: The Wikipedia article for Join Hands notes that McKay and Morris “withdrew and became uncommunicative with the rest of the band and their manager and co-producer of the album, Nils Stevenson” during this period, but does not specify the cause of that withdrawal. The second search result synopsis (from the Wikipedia Join Hands page) states that differences stretched from record covers to mix to song selection during the making of Join Hands. The Wikipedia article does not elaborate further on who held which position in those disputes. (Wikipedia — Join Hands, as of 2026-10-29)

Contractual dimension: The search result synopsis from Wikipedia’s Join Hands article notes that Morris and McKay “walked away from their contracts” and that the Banshees were “likely to be suing McKay and Morris for the losses their departure may cause.” This is from the Wikipedia Join Hands article snippet; the full article text as fetched does not elaborate on this. It was not confirmed in McKay’s own Guitar World interview nor in the Kenny Morris Wikipedia article. Treat as partially corroborated — the contractual fallout is plausible (they were mid-tour under a Polydor deal) but the specifics are not documented in the sources checked here.

Summary of what is established:

  • The split had been building for weeks, not just hours.
  • The three-to-two power dynamic (Siouxsie + Severin + manager vs. McKay + Morris) created persistent outvoting on band decisions.
  • Management (Nils Stevenson) is identified by McKay as a key driver of internal division.
  • Creative disputes over the Join Hands recording (covers, mix, song selection) are referenced in a search snippet but not detailed in the primary sources checked.
  • The Belfast gear failure was a breaking point in the final weeks; the physical altercation with Siouxsie at the Aberdeen record shop was the precipitating incident.
  • No source found gives specifics of any formal contractual dispute or legal action.

Join Hands: mix and song selection disputes (web research, 2026-10-30)

This section records what was found in a targeted web search for McKay’s and Morris’s specific positions on the mix and song selection of Join Hands, versus Siouxsie/Severin/Stevenson. The fan archive thebansheesandothercreatures.co.uk (both joinhandsalbum.htm and joinhandstrivia.htm) timed out on every fetch attempt and could not be read. What follows is drawn from Wikipedia, the Arts Desk reissue review, and search-result aggregator snippets.

Mixing sessions — what the sources establish:

Wikipedia’s Join Hands article states: “Unlike the sessions for The Scream, the music was recorded without Siouxsie, as she added her vocals later. Morris did not take part in the mixing sessions, while Siouxsie was heavily involved.” (Wikipedia — Join Hands, as of 2026-10-30)

So the documented positions are: Siouxsie actively present and involved in mixing; Morris absent. McKay’s attendance at mixing is not explicitly stated — the broader withdrawal narrative (McKay and Morris “became uncommunicative with the rest of the band”) suggests he too was absent or disengaged, but Wikipedia does not state this for McKay specifically. Severin’s role in mixing is not mentioned.

Song selection — what the sources establish:

Multiple secondary sources (Arts Desk reissue review; search-aggregator summaries drawing on the fan archive and other press) attribute the following positions:

  • McKay’s position: He believed more songs could have fitted on Join Hands (the album runs to eight tracks). He also objected to, or at minimum expressed dissatisfaction with, the inclusion of “The Lord’s Prayer” — the eleven-minute closing track. Secondary sources characterise his view as seeing it as a throwback to the band’s pre-McKay/Morris identity. (The Arts Desk — Music Reissues Weekly: John McKay — Sixes and Sevens; search-aggregator snippet from thebansheesandothercreatures.co.uk material)

  • Siouxsie’s counter-position: After the departure, Siouxsie publicly dismissed the pair’s commitment: “John and Kenny were doing it for the money and you can’t do a good ‘Lord’s Prayer’ with that attitude.” This is sourced from search-aggregator snippets attributed to the fan archive and/or contemporary press; a primary interview citation was not confirmed independently in this research pass. Treat as corroborated by multiple secondary sources but not yet tied to a single dated primary source.

  • Production-side alignment: The engineer/co-producer Mike Stavrou is described in secondary sources as having sided with Siouxsie, Severin, and Stevenson on production decisions, reinforcing the 3–2 outvoting dynamic. McKay questioned Stevenson’s competence as a co-producer and reportedly felt pressured by Stevenson to alter his guitar approach after Stevenson heard another guitarist he preferred. These are from secondary accounts (Arts Desk review; search-aggregator summary from fan archive material) — no McKay quote naming these specific grievances about the mix or track listing was found in a primary source.

What remains unconfirmed:

  • No primary-source interview (McKay or Morris in their own words) was found that specifies their objections to particular mixing decisions — EQ, levels, specific treatment of tracks, or anything below the level of “Siouxsie was heavily involved / Morris was absent.”
  • The claim that McKay felt more songs should have been included is from secondary characterisation, not a direct McKay quote found in this pass. His 2025 Guitar World interview (already in this file) does not address it.
  • Whether Morris attended any mixing sessions (beyond the Wikipedia statement that he was absent) and whether he had any articulated position on the mix beyond absence is not documented in the sources checked.
