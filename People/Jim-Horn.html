Jim Horn

Born James Ronald Horn, 20 November 1940, Los Angeles, California. Saxophonist, flautist, oboist, and woodwind specialist; one of the most extensively recorded session musicians in American popular music from the late 1950s through the 2000s. Discogs ID: 255404. Name variations include “Big” Jim Horn, Brother Jim Horn, James R. Horn, The Jim Horn Players. Core member of the Wrecking Crew orbit, later associated with the Shelter Records house band (The Shelter People).

His Discogs contributor credits span roughly 1,100 unique master releases across seven decades, with peak density in the 1970s (286 unique masters) and sustained high volume through the 1980s (233) and 1990s (212). Primary instruments listed across credits: tenor, alto, baritone, soprano, and bass saxophone; flute, alto flute, bass flute, piccolo; oboe; clarinet; recorder; occasionally arranger for horn sections.

Overview

Horn grew up in Los Angeles and was already active as a professional musician by the late 1950s. His earliest documented sessions are with Duane Eddy and The Rebels (1959), credited on saxophone and flute for the debut LP The “Twangs” The “Thang” on Jamie Records. He continued working with Eddy through the mid-1960s, appearing on Girls! Girls! Girls! (1961) and Duane A Go Go Go (1965).

Through the early 1960s he worked across the emerging Los Angeles pop and rock session world, picking up credits on material produced at Western Recorders, Gold Star, and similar facilities. His connections ran through the same pool of players who would later be dubbed the Wrecking Crew — keyboardist Larry Knechtel and guitarist Louie Shelton are among the collaborators Discogs records him sharing sessions with three or more times.

By the mid-1960s Horn was appearing on records for every major label in the LA market. Credits from 1965–1966 include The Righteous Brothers’ Back to Back (Philles Records), Ike & Tina Turner’s “River Deep – Mountain High” (London/Philles, 1966, saxophone uncredited), and The Mamas & The Papas’ Deliver (Dunhill, 1967). He also arranged horns on Mae West’s Way Out West (Tower, 1966), one of the earliest arranger credits in the Discogs record.

The late 1960s expanded the stylistic range considerably. He appears on Van Dyke Parks’ Song Cycle (Warner Bros., 1967), Randy Newman’s debut (Reprise, 1968), Richard Harris’s A Tramp Shining (Dunhill/RCA Victor, 1968), José Feliciano’s Feliciano! (RCA Victor, 1968), and Joni Mitchell’s Ladies of the Canyon (Reprise, 1970, baritone saxophone). The jazz crossover credits from this period include Milt Jackson’s Memphis Jackson (Impulse!, 1970) and Blue Mitchell’s Collision in Black (Blue Note, 1969).

By 1970–1971 Horn had become a fixture at Shelter Records, Leon Russell’s label. He released his own material there: the single “Jennifer Juniper” / “Guerilla Love In” (1971–72), and the LP Jim’s Horns (1973), which he produced himself. He appears on Leon Russell’s Will O’ the Wisp (Shelter, 1975) and in the Shelter People house band. The Shelter Records association brought him into the Concert for Bangladesh orbit: he appears as saxophone/musician on the 1971 Apple Records release The Concert for Bangla Desh, connecting him formally to the George Harrison circle.

His work with George Harrison is one of the most sustained pairings in his career: flute and saxophone on Living in the Material World (Apple, 1973), flute on Dark Horse (Apple, 1974), saxophone on Extra Texture (Read All About It) (Apple, 1975), baritone and tenor saxophone on Cloud Nine (Dark Horse, 1987), and across the Best of Dark Horse 1976–1989 compilation. He also worked on Harrison’s 1974 North American tour.

Concurrent major credits in the mid-1970s include Carole King’s Wrap Around Joy (Ode, 1974, saxophone solo), Harry Nilsson and John Lennon’s Pussy Cats (RCA, 1974), Boz Scaggs’ Silk Degrees (Columbia, 1976, horns/tenor saxophone), and Steely Dan’s The Royal Scam (ABC, 1976) and Aja (ABC, 1977). On Aja he is credited with flute and saxes; it became one of the best-reviewed albums of the decade and gave him one of his most prominent mainstream credits.

The 1977–1978 period also includes Warren Zevon’s Excitable Boy (Asylum, 1978, saxophone, recorder), Joe Cocker’s I Can Stand a Little Rain (A&M, 1974) and live recordings, and the debut Toto album (Columbia, 1978). He continued working with Toto through Toto IV (1982, saxophone, recorders) and The Seventh One (CBS, 1988).

From the late 1970s onward his work shifted substantially toward country. He became a recurring presence on John Denver’s records — credited on flute, reeds, saxophone, recorder, and in some cases as arranger — across John Denver (1979), Autograph (1980), Seasons of the Heart (1982), It’s About Time (1983), Higher Ground (1988), Stonehaven Sunrise (1989), The Flower That Shattered the Stone (1990), Different Directions (1991), and the Wildlife Concert (1995). He also served as a touring band member for Denver.

Other major country-era credits include Hank Williams Jr. (Whiskey Bent and Hell Bound, 1979; Man of Steel, 1983; Montana Cafe, 1986; Born to Boogie, 1987; Wild Streak, 1988; Hog Wild, 1995), Kenny Rogers (Something Inside So Strong, 1989; Christmas in America, 1989; With Love, 1996; Across My Heart, 1997), and Alabama (Just Us, 1987; Cheap Seats, 1993). He also contributed to Bobby Bland’s Dreamer (ABC, 1974) and Reflections in Blue (ABC, 1977), and appears on numerous Malaco Records blues releases.

In 1988 he played saxophone on the Traveling Wilburys’ debut Volume One (Wilbury/Warner Bros.) and returned for Vol. 3 (1990), recording alongside Harrison, Bob Dylan, Tom Petty, Roy Orbison, and Jeff Lynne.

His solo records outside Shelter include Neon Nights (Warner Bros., 1988) and Night Shift / Work It Out (Warner Bros., 1990), and a late-career Beatles tribute record Through the Years – Jim Horn Meets the Beatles (Shikata Records, 2002).
