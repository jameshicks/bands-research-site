Paul A. Rothchild

Aug 02, 202610 min read

Paul A. Rothchild

American record producer. Born April 18, 1935, Brooklyn, New York. Died March 30, 1995 (lung cancer). Discogs artist ID: 252435. Also credited as “Paul Rothchild,” “Paul Rothschild,” “P.A. Rothchild,” and several other variants.

Staff producer at Elektra Records under Jac Holzman, 1963–1972. Primary producer for The Doors across their entire Elektra run. One of the defining architect-producers of American psychedelic and blues-rock, active from the Boston folk scene in the late 1950s through the mid-1990s.

Biography

Early Career: Boston Folk Scene and Prestige (1962–1963)

Rothchild came up in the Boston-area folk revival, working as an A&R figure and session supervisor around the early 1960s. His first traceable production credits (Discogs) are a 1962 bluegrass LP on Mt. Auburn Records and a 1963 flamenco album on Prestige International. He also worked as a supervisor on several Prestige Folklore sessions — folk and blues albums by artists including Eric Von Schmidt, Koerner Ray & Glover, and Jesse Fuller — a role documented on Discogs as “Supervised By” rather than full producer credit.

In 1963, he came aboard Elektra Records as a house producer under Jac Holzman, who is credited on contemporaneous sessions as “Production Supervisor.” This arrangement — Rothchild as hands-on producer, Holzman as executive overseer — would characterize his Elektra work throughout the decade.

Elektra Folk Years (1963–1965)

Rothchild’s earliest Elektra sessions were in the Greenwich Village and Cambridge folk worlds. His first Elektra production credit is The Even Dozen Jug Band (1963), a jug-band project that also connected him to a downtown scene later visible in multiple Elektra rosters. He produced or recorded Tom Paxton’s Ramblin’ Boy (1964), Fred Neil’s Bleecker & MacDougal (1965), Tom Rush’s debut Tom Rush (1965), and Phil Ochs’s I Ain’t Marching Anymore (1965, as “Directed By [Recording]”) and Ain’t That News (1965). He also worked on early sessions for Dave Van Ronk (whose blues recordings appear under Prestige and were later reissued) and Koerner, Ray & Glover’s Return of Koerner, Ray & Glover (1965, Elektra).

During this period the line between “producer” and “recording director” was fluid on Elektra; Rothchild’s role varied in title — Recording Director, Supervised By, Directed By — but represented consistent creative responsibility in the studio.

The Paul Butterfield Blues Band (1965–1966)

Rothchild’s production of The Paul Butterfield Blues Band’s debut album (1965) was a career-making moment, and a landmark for American electric blues. The record introduced Mike Bloomfield and Paul Butterfield alongside Elvin Bishop, Mark Naftalin, Jerome Arnold, and Sam Lay — a Chicago-derived interracial electric blues lineup signed to a New York folk label. Jac Holzman is credited as “Production Supervisor”; Mark Abramson received an “Assistant Recording Director / Assistant Producer” credit.

For East-West (1966), Rothchild again shared co-production duties with Abramson on most tracks; only the extended title track “East-West” itself (side B, track 1) was produced by Barry Friedman. The album’s extended modal improvisation on the title track — a bridge between blues, jazz, and what would become psychedelic rock — is among the most influential recordings Rothchild oversaw.

The Doors: Elektra Studio Run (1967–1971)

Rothchild became the primary producer for The Doors and remained so for six of their seven studio albums. His engineer on every Doors session was Bruce Botnick.

He did not produce L.A. Woman (1971). By his own account (repeated in posthumous liner notes and interviews — not confirmed via Discogs) he withdrew from that project after finding the band’s rawer direction incompatible with his approach; Bruce Botnick stepped up as producer on that final studio album.

Across those six albums, the recurring personnel core was: Jim Morrison (vocals), Ray Manzarek (keyboards/bass), Robby Krieger (guitar), John Densmore (drums), and Bruce Botnick (engineer). Strange Days and Waiting For The Sun added Doug Lubahn on occasional bass. The Soft Parade was the most orchestrated Doors record, bringing in brass and strings arrangers. Morrison Hotel returned to a sparse rock core.

Rothchild also produced the 1970 live double LP Absolutely Live and later the posthumous An American Prayer (1978), the spoken-word Morrison recordings with new musical backing by the surviving Doors members.

Other Elektra Contemporaries (1966–1972)

Alongside the Doors work, Rothchild produced a range of other Elektra artists through the same period:

  • Tim Buckley — self-titled debut (1966, Elektra). Personnel included Van Dyke Parks (harpsichord, piano), Jack Nitzsche (string arrangements), and Bruce Botnick (engineer).
  • Love (Da Capo, 1966, Elektra) — Arthur Lee’s band; Rothchild produced the album.
  • Clear Light (1967, Elektra) — a Sunset Strip psychedelic group; self-titled debut.
  • Rhinoceros (1968, Elektra) — produced their self-titled debut.
  • Ars Nova (1968, Elektra) — experimental rock/orchestral group; self-titled debut.
  • Goodthunder (1972, Elektra) — later-era Elektra release.

He also produced Tom Rush’s Classic Rush compilation (1970) and various Elektra singles through this period.

Janis Joplin: Pearl (1971)

Rothchild’s final studio production of the era, and one of the most celebrated American rock albums: Janis Joplin’s Pearl (Columbia/CBS, 1971, Discogs master 72235). Joplin died in October 1970 before the album was completed; Rothchild finished it posthumously. The record features the Full Tilt Boogie Band with John Till (guitar), Brad Campbell (bass), Ken Pearson (organ), Richard Bell (piano), Clark Pierson (drums), and various session players including Bobby Womack. Engineer was Phil Macy. Pearl became the best-selling album of Joplin’s career.

He later compiled and supervised posthumous Joplin releases including Janis Joplin’s Greatest Hits (1973, Columbia) and the documentary compilation Janis (1975, CBS), and contributed production to Box Of Pearls (1999, Columbia/Legacy).

Post-Elektra Work (1970s)

After his core Elektra run, Rothchild worked across several labels:

  • John B. Sebastian — self-titled (1970, Reprise); Sebastian’s breakout solo debut after The Lovin’ Spoonful.
  • The Four Of Us (1971, Reprise)
  • Bonnie Raitt — Home Plate (1975, Warner Bros.); Sweet Forgiveness (1977, Warner Bros.)
  • The Outlaws — Outlaws (1975, Arista); Lady In Waiting (1976, Arista); Hurry Sundown (1977, Arista). He produced the Florida Southern rock group’s first three albums, including their signature Green Grass & High Tides.
  • Funky Kings (1976, Arista)
  • The Rose — original soundtrack recording (1979, Atlantic), produced and arranged for Bette Midler’s film vehicle.
  • Stories We Can Tell — John Sebastian / Everly Brothers project (1972, RCA Victor)

He also worked with David Crosby on If I Could Only Remember My Name (1971, Atlantic) as producer; that credit appears in later reissue editions (Discogs master 84859).

Later Career (1980s–1995)

Rothchild’s activity thinned through the 1980s. He continued to be involved in Doors archival projects — producing Alive, She Cried (1983, Elektra), overseeing the Oliver Stone film soundtrack The Doors (1991, Elektra), and contributing to the 1997 Doors Box Set where he received a “Liner Notes [The Doors Opened]” credit alongside his standard producer credit.

Other later productions include:

  • Fast Fontaine (1981, EMI America) — as arranger and producer
  • Modern Pilgrims (1988, RCA)
  • Rude Awakening original motion picture soundtrack (1989, Elektra)
  • Body Heat (1993, Discovery Records) — produced and mixed

He died of lung cancer on March 30, 1995. Several posthumous Doors and Joplin reissues carry his name into the 2000s and beyond.

Scene Connections

  • Greenwich Village / Cambridge folk revival — Rothchild’s early Elektra work (Tom Paxton, Phil Ochs, Fred Neil, Tom Rush, Dave Van Ronk) situates him inside the core 1963–65 folk scene.
  • Electric Chicago blues crossover — The Butterfield albums were pivotal in introducing Chicago-school electric blues to predominantly white rock audiences and to the Elektra catalog.
  • Sunset Strip / Los Angeles psychedelic rock — The Doors, Love (Da Capo), Clear Light, and Rhinoceros are all central Sunset Strip / Elektra psychedelic records. Rothchild spent most of the late 1960s based in Los Angeles recording these acts.
  • Elektra Records ecosystem — Rothchild worked within Jac Holzman’s Elektra at its most creatively ambitious moment, sharing the building with other Elektra producers (Mark Abramson, Barry Friedman) and engineers (primarily Bruce Botnick). See Elektra Records.
  • Southern rock (The Outlaws) — His mid-1970s run with The Outlaws on Arista situates him in the Florida/Southern rock scene.

Open Questions

  • The specific recording locations for most Elektra sessions are not confirmed through Discogs alone (likely Sunset Sound, Elektra’s own studio, and/or TTG Studios for Doors work — would require web sources).
  • Rothchild’s exact relationship with the Boston folk scene pre-1962 (specific venues, acts managed or booked) is undocumented in Discogs.
  • The reason he was not involved in L.A. Woman is widely cited in secondary sources but not confirmed in Discogs metadata; the album credits Bruce Botnick as sole producer.
  • His work on Joni Mitchell’s Clouds (1969, Reprise) appears in the discography — his role there (producer) could be cross-checked against the Mitchell vault research if that exists.
  • Whether his “Choir” credit on Cyrus (2006, Collectors’ Choice Music) reflects a late-career performance role or is a database error is unclear.
