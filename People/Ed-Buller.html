English musician and producer. Member of The Psychedelic Furs per Discogs; credited with additional keyboards on Midnight To Midnight (1987). Also a member of Suede as producer in the 1990s.

Production Evolution: Suede Trilogy (1993–1996)

Buller described the three albums as “chapters in a book” and his core stated philosophy across all of them was to “instil a sense of trust and to help a band achieve their dreams” — working at the band’s level rather than imposing a top-down aesthetic. Repeat Fanzine interview

Suede (1993)

Recorded at Protocol, Angel, and Master Rock studios, London. Buller recalled recognising the band as potentially “one of the biggest bands in the world” from the outset — he took the demo of “The Drowners” home and kept rewinding it, convinced it was a hit. His priority was to let Bernard Butler’s virtuoso guitar playing drive the sound: Butler’s “standard approach to Suede songs at the time, was to play these very flowery, lyrical guitar parts,” and Buller largely deferred to him. The band’s professionalism impressed him: while Buller worked on arrangements, they would rehearse in the live room, or simply play covers because “they just liked playing so much.” He positioned Simon Gilbert in unconventional spaces — for “My Insatiable One,” Gilbert was placed by the tape-machine room, achieving what Buller considered “the best drum sound we ever got.” Vocally, Brett Anderson worked in short full-flow takes (typically 3–4), then directed Buller to specific moments: “he’d come back into the control room and say, ‘Can I hear take 4?‘” — a level of vocal self-awareness Buller had not encountered before. Repeat Fanzine

Buller acknowledged retrospectively that on the debut he was “slightly out of my depth” attempting ambitious arrangements and wanted to bring “lush, Trevor Horn-style production” to enhance the compositions — a goal he felt he only partially achieved. headlinerhub.com

Dog Man Star (1994)

Recorded at Master Rock, Kilburn (22 March – 26 July 1994), co-produced with Flood. Buller called it “the most unpleasant record I’ve ever made, in terms of atmosphere”: Butler and the rest of the band “hardly spoke to each other” and were “not in the studio together once during making, perhaps only for a matter of hours.” Buller set up an informal room with couches and a TV to accommodate Butler’s constant creative flow; Butler worked a 9–5 schedule, while Anderson was more nocturnal. Mojo; The Quietus

The sonic ambition expanded sharply. Butler pursued a “brutal beefy thud” — a raw, live sound contrasting with the debut’s more layered textures. Buller acknowledged he was “not a skilful enough manipulator” to deliver everything Butler had in mind, and admitted “there were things that he wanted to get right on the record that I was not capable of doing.” A key example was “The Asphalt World”: the demo ran 18 minutes; Butler resisted edits and it took over three weeks to reach a workable length. Buller used a modular Moog synthesizer for vocal filtering effects. The Roland SH-1000 was processed through pedals rather than programmed; Butler had “two ideas going through his head, the song and the soundtrack.” Brian Gascoigne arranged a 40-piece orchestra for “Still Life,” which Buller likened to a “Spinal Tap moment” where the strings swamped Butler’s guitar work. Repeat Fanzine; The Quietus

The album’s central production failure, in Buller’s own assessment, was the mixing: he mixed it himself and believed this was a mistake. He described the “New Generation” mix as “the worst of my career” — “if someone puts it on, I have to leave the room” — and acknowledged the original masters sounded superior to the final mixes. “Anything that falls into the category of over-production was me,” he told Mojo. “I had to go to America to learn how to do bottom end.” A specific happy accident: Butler tuning a TV in the rehearsal room while Buller rolled tape produced the ambient noise on “The Asphalt World” — “one of the rare days where he smiled.” Despite everything, Buller called it his favourite of the three albums. Repeat Fanzine; Mojo

Coming Up (1996)

Recorded December 1995–June 1996 across The Townhouse (Shepherd’s Bush), Mayfair, Westside, and Master Rock studios. The nine-month process was a complete atmospheric reversal: Buller called it “one of the funnest records I’ve ever made” and “the most luxurious record I’d ever made.” Brett Anderson was “completely in charge on that record” and the band got along well throughout. Repeat Fanzine; guitar.com

The production blueprint was explicitly modelled on Tony Visconti’s approach to T.Rex’s Tanx: each track was laid down using a “Marc Bolan ’60s click-track” (bongos, tambourine, and acoustic guitar from new guitarist Richard Oakes rather than a metronome), then built up in layers. Drums were recorded over roughly a month to achieve a “fat and chunky and dancey” sound using computer editing — a more laboured, technology-assisted drum process than either previous album. Repeat Fanzine; guitar.com

To differentiate the guitar sound from the Bernard Butler era, Buller largely avoided traditional amplifiers, instead using “a device borrowed from Flood called an ADA Ampulator” paired with a Marshall JMP-1 preamp and Yamaha FX-900 effects unit. For “Beautiful Ones” — Buller’s pick as “Richard’s tour-de-force” — he orchestrated multiple guitar layers: Oakes’s Jaguar, a 355, Telecaster, and Dobro resonator. The “Trash” lead break used “Ampulator and DI’d fuzz” through a Focusrite desk, exploiting overloaded preamps — “a technique dating back to 1968.” guitar.com

The key structural change from the two previous albums was bringing in Dave Bascombe as mixer for tracks 1–9. Buller was emphatic that Bascombe “deserves more credit than he got — a huge part of the sound of that record is his input, he did a really, really good job.” The decision to hire a professional mixer was a direct lesson drawn from the mixing failures of Dog Man Star. Repeat Fanzine

One notable dispute: tempo on “Trash.” Bascombe sped up the recording during mixing; Anderson later felt it made him “sound like you’re on helium — Pinky and Perky.” Buller disagreed: “I think it’s brilliant!” Repeat Fanzine

The Two Suedes

Buller drew a clear line between the Bernard Butler era and the Richard Oakes era: “there are two Suedes — the Bernard Suede and the Richard Suede.” Both versions were “a great band, a fantastic band” but fundamentally different entities. Butler was “the only person I’ve ever worked with who I would call a virtuoso…the human-being and the instrument basically become one,” but he also “hated the idea of being a pop star — absolutely hated it!” Oakes brought a different, more pop-oriented approach. Buller estimated Butler’s departure ultimately stemmed from a combination of his father’s cancer diagnosis, the band’s relationship collapse, and a refusal to cede sole creative control that the band would not grant him. Repeat Fanzine

Nude Records Work

Primary producer/engineer for Nude Records across 232 releases, the most of any individual in the label’s catalog. Produced all major Suede releases through Coming Up (1996), including the self-titled debut album (1993) and Dog Man Star (1994). Co-engineered with Gary Stout on the bulk of early Nude output.
