Overview

Nick McCabe (Nicholas John McCabe) is the lead guitarist of The Verve, active since the band’s earliest recordings in 1992. His discographic footprint sits almost entirely within the Verve orbit: Richard Ashcroft, Simon Jones, and Peter Salisbury each share over 200 credits with him, and the catalogue is anchored to Virgin and Hut Recordings across the 1993–2016 period, with more recent work appearing on Universal imprints. Beyond guitar his credits extend to keyboards and production, and he holds membership in Black Submarine and The Black Ships alongside his Verve work. Stylistically his output spans Britpop, alternative rock, shoegaze and psychedelic rock — the full breadth of The Verve’s sonic range.

Notes (web)

Absence from early Urban Hymns sessions (1996) and return

The band’s August 1995 dissolution was publicly framed as a full breakup. The Face “Dark Star” article (September 1997) states: “The Verve now privately admit that their not-very-convincing split was just a way to get rid of their guitarist. Always vulnerable, Nick was worn down by the band’s sprawling regime, which mixed touring, writing, recording and hardcore partying with an awful lot of waiting around, all according to Richard’s whim.” (musicsaves.org reproduction of The Face, September 1997) Ashcroft fired McCabe and dissolved the band simultaneously; the band then reformed without him approximately two weeks later, replacing him with Simon Tong.

Conflict — “really just” vs “just”: A previous version of this note (and some secondary sources) quoted the split admission as “really just a way to get rid of their guitarist.” The musicsaves.org reproduction of the original article text reads “just a way to get rid of their guitarist” — no “really.” The word “really” does not appear in the passage as reproduced. The secondary wording “really just” should be treated as a paraphrase, not a verbatim quote.

Several factors fed into McCabe’s removal. During the A Northern Soul touring cycle McCabe grew increasingly exhausted by other members’ drug use and erratic behaviour — other members were frequently partying and taking ecstasy, and Richard Ashcroft and associates were reported to be shouting and destroying objects — and he responded by scheduling his studio and rehearsal hours early in the day to avoid them. McCabe told The Face in September 1997: “I was a total emotional wreck. It got to the point that all I had in common with the rest of them was the music.” (musicsaves.org reproduction of The Face, September 1997) The article also records McCabe’s immediate reaction on learning of the split: “I just thought: ‘You fucking bastards.‘” On his position after rejoining: “It’s good now, you know. They asked me back, so they can’t complain. I get to do what the fuck I want now.” (musicsaves.org reproduction of The Face, September 1997) Separate reporting describes McCabe reaching “dizzying lows of depression” as a result of touring pressures, and Ashcroft concluding that he had become “more of a nuisance than a benefit.” (albumism.com retrospective, search snippet)

In a 2017 interview McCabe declined to specify the causes beyond confirming the outcome: “I was essentially sacked from The Verve for various reasons I don’t have the time or reason to go into now.” He also noted Ashcroft felt constrained asserting his songwriting within the band’s collaborative structure, implying the creative shift toward a more lyric-led direction partly drove the dismissal. (xsnoize.com, 2017, interview with McCabe — page returned 403 on direct fetch; summary derived from fetched content cache)

Approximately 18 months elapsed between McCabe’s effective sacking and Ashcroft’s callback. Ashcroft called on New Year’s Day / early January 1997 — reportedly impulsively, without consulting Simon Jones or Peter Salisbury first — because the Rockfield sessions, which had cost around £2 million without producing a finished album, lacked the chemistry and guitar sound that defined The Verve. McCabe learned from Simon Jones that the call had been unilateral. McCabe subsequently described his role as that of “a problem solver,” and Ashcroft later said the turning point for Urban Hymns was McCabe’s return. (stubermania.substack.com, 1997 Ashcroft interview; xsnoize.com, 2017 McCabe interview)
