Brian Jones (5)

Discogs ID: 409290. British guitarist and multi-instrumentalist; founding member of The Rolling Stones, active May 1962 to June 1969.

Born Lewis Brian Hopkin-Jones, 28 February 1942, Cheltenham. The original organizer and leader of the Rolling Stones, though he was progressively sidelined as Jagger-Richards took the songwriting and public-face roles. Known for his eclecticism: sitar, dulcimer, marimba, bells, saxophone, harmonica on various Stones recordings (Aftermath being the most documented). Fired in June 1969 after drug convictions and unreliability; drowned 2–3 July 1969 at Cotchford Farm.

Firing from the Rolling Stones (June 1969)

The meeting at Cotchford Farm

On 8 June 1969, Mick Jagger, Keith Richards, and Charlie Watts drove to Jones’s home, Cotchford Farm in Hartfield, East Sussex, to inform him that the band would continue without him. Bill Wyman was not present. The meeting lasted approximately thirty minutes and was described by all parties as friendly; Richards and Watts later said both sides were relieved matters were coming to a head. Charlie Watts was reportedly brought partly as a peacemaker in the event of confrontation. Wikipedia, as of 2026-07-12; ultimateclassicrock.com; rollingstonesdata.com

Reasons cited by the band

The official and reported reasons given at the time, and in later retrospective accounts, were:

  • Drug convictions barred Jones from obtaining a US work visa, making the Stones’ planned North American tour impossible with him on the roster. He had been arrested on cannabis possession charges as recently as May 1969 (following an earlier 1967 conviction). ultimateclassicrock.com
  • Absence and unreliability at sessions. By the Let It Bleed sessions (1968–69) Jones had contributed almost nothing — he played congas on “Midnight Rambler” and autoharp on “You Got the Silver.” Richards later described him as “already in Bye-Bye Land.” ultimateclassicrock.com
  • Musical divergence. The band framed the split partly as creative incompatibility — the Jagger-Richards direction had moved away from the R&B and world-music eclecticism Jones favoured. This framing was also Jones’s own public explanation (see below).

Bill Wyman, in a later interview with Classic Rock, said: “There was no malice … they just got fed up with his drug taking.” Wyman also noted Jones “was always a bit sad” about the band not doing the music he really liked. rockandrollgarage.com

The band’s public account

Jones was allowed to control how the departure was announced; the other members told him he was out but left the framing to him, so to the public it appeared voluntary. Wikipedia, as of 2026-07-12

Mick Jagger spoke to the New Musical Express (Richard Green, “Mick Jagger talks to NME about the new stone, the ex-stone, and two albums,” 14 June 1969): “We’d known for a few months that Brian wasn’t keen; he wasn’t enjoying himself and it got to the stage where we had to sit down and talk about it.” The article noted no apparent bitterness or internal strife in Jagger’s tone. geirmykl.wordpress.com (transcription of NME, 14 June 1969)

Charlie Watts reflected on the meeting in a 2012 interview: “It wasn’t very nice. I remember Mick and Keith saying, ‘We can’t go on like this; we needed another guy,’ and we did need somebody else.” Watts also told the BBC separately that he “always felt sorry for Brian.” ultimateclassicrock.com; faroutmagazine.co.uk

Jones’s own statement and account

Jones issued a public statement on 9 June 1969 — the day after the meeting — framing his departure as a matter of musical incompatibility rather than dismissal:

“I no longer see eye-to-eye with the others over the discs we are cutting. I want to play my kind of music, which is no longer the Stones music. The music Mick and Keith have been writing has progressed at a tangent, as far as my own taste is concerned.”

ultimateclassicrock.com; Wikipedia, as of 2026-07-12

In what became his last known solo interview, conducted by journalist Thomas Beyl at Cotchford Farm in the days before his death, Jones offered a more personal account. He claimed the split was mutual: “After a friendly discussion, we came to the conclusion that separation is the only solution.” He also said: “The old Stones’ sound is not my taste. I think it’s out of date.” Asked about returning, he was definitive: “None!” He appeared pale and unwell during the interview. He also disclosed: “Two years ago I wanted to leave, but Mick talked me out of it. Today there is no turning back.” He expressed plans to produce music and compose in African folk styles, and mentioned financial ambition: “I want to be rich and finally rake in the big bucks. Just like Mick and Keith…” rollingstonesdata.com — transcription of last Jones interview

Financial settlement

Jones received a lump sum of £100,000 and an annual payment of £20,000 for as long as the Rolling Stones continued as a band. ultimateclassicrock.com

Aftermath

Jones drowned in his swimming pool at Cotchford Farm on the night of 2–3 July 1969, less than four weeks after the meeting. His replacement, Mick Taylor (then 20, formerly of John Mayall’s Bluesbreakers), had already been announced and had begun recording with the band. The Stones played a free concert in Hyde Park on 5 July 1969 at which Jagger read Percy Bysshe Shelley’s “Adonais” as a tribute and released white butterflies. Wikipedia, as of 2026-07-12
