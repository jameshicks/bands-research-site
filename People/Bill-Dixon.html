Bill Dixon

Full name: William Robert Dixon Born: October 5, 1925 — Nantucket, MA, USA Died: June 16, 2010 — North Bennington, VT, USA Instruments: Trumpet, flugelhorn, piano Discogs ID: 258128

Free jazz trumpeter, composer, visual artist, and educator. One of the central organizers and theorists of the American avant-garde music scene of the 1960s; founder of the Jazz Composers Guild. Professor of Music at Bennington College (Vermont) from 1968 to 1995, where he founded the college’s Black Music Division. Also a visual artist whose paintings appeared on many of his own album covers. A&R producer for Savoy Records during the 1960s.

Biography

Early Work and the New York Scene (1963–1966)

Dixon came to prominence in New York in the early 1960s as part of the same ferment that produced Ornette Coleman, Cecil Taylor, and the nascent free jazz movement. His earliest Discogs-documented work is as co-leader of The Archie Shepp-Bill Dixon Quartet with Archie Shepp, releasing the quartet’s self-titled LP on Savoy Records in 1963 (cat. MG-12178). The group included bassist Don Moore, drummer Paul Cohen, and drummer Howard McRae across different tracks; Dixon and Shepp split production and liner note duties, and Dixon also handled the cover design. The set mixed originals by both men with covers of Ornette Coleman’s “Peace” and Leonard Bernstein’s “Somewhere,” signaling their position at the crossroads of the jazz avant-garde and broader cultural engagement.

The 1964 split LP Bill Dixon 7-Tette / Archie Shepp And The New York Contemporary 5 (Savoy MG-12184) documents Dixon leading his own ensemble in parallel with Shepp’s group. Dixon’s side featured Ken McIntyre on alto saxophone and oboe, Howard Johnson (3) on baritone saxophone and tuba, George Barrow on tenor saxophone, David Izenzon on bass, Harold Dotson on bass, and Howard McRae on drums — a deliberately textured ensemble that foreshadowed his later orchestral thinking. Shepp’s side featured Don Cherry, John Tchicai, Ted Curson, Ronnie Boykins, and Sunny Murray.

In 1964 Dixon organized the October Revolution in Jazz, a series of concerts at the Cellar Café in New York that are widely credited as the first organized presentation of free jazz to the public. This led directly to the formation of the Jazz Composers Guild in late 1964, a collective of avant-garde musicians (including Shepp, Carla Bley, Paul Bley, Sun Ra, Cecil Taylor, and others) designed to negotiate collectively with venues and record companies and maintain artistic control. The Guild dissolved in 1965 amid internal disagreements, but its influence on later musician-run institutions was substantial.

Intents And Purposes and RCA (1967)

Dixon’s most prominent solo record of the decade was Intents And Purposes (1967, RCA Victor, LSP-3844), issued under the name The Bill Dixon Orchestra. An ambitious large-ensemble work, it featured Jimmy Garrison and Reggie Workman on bass, Byard Lancaster on alto saxophone and bass clarinet, Robin Kenyatta on alto saxophone, Jimmy Cheatham on bass trombone, George Marge on English horn and flute, Marc Levin on percussion, Robert Pozar on drums, and cellist Catherine Norris — an unusually broad palette for a free jazz date at a major label. Dixon is credited as trumpet, flugelhorn, written-by, and leader. The record was not commercially successful, and Dixon would not record again as a primary artist until 1977.

Cecil Taylor’s Conquistador! (1968)

Dixon appears on Cecil Taylor’s Conquistador! (1968, Blue Note, BST 84260) as trumpet — a high-profile sideman credit alongside Jimmy Lyons (2), Alan Silva, Henry Grimes, and Andrew Cyrille, recorded by Rudy Van Gelder. The association with Taylor was artistically important and is documented also in later collaborative recordings (see Cecil Taylor / Bill Dixon / Tony Oxley, and the Vade Mecum dedication “For Cecil Taylor: Almost Anacrusis…”).

Opium For Franz and the Austrian Connection (1977–1978)

After a decade largely away from recording under his own name, Dixon surfaced on Franz Koglmann’s Opium For Franz (1977, Pipe Records, Austria) as a featured soloist alongside Steve Lacy, Aldo Romano, Cesarius Alvim, and other European players. The collaboration with Koglmann continued on the later compilation Opium (2001, Between The Lines). This Austrian connection established Dixon’s profile in European avant-garde circles just as his Soul Note period was beginning.

Soul Note Period (1980–1996)

The bulk of Dixon’s mature recording output appeared on the Italian label Soul Note (a sibling imprint to Black Saint), produced throughout by Giovanni Bonandrini. This is the most substantial single-label relationship of his career, spanning over fifteen years and roughly eight primary-artist albums.

In Italy — Volume One (1980, SN 1008) and In Italy — Volume Two (1981, Soul Note): these were recorded live in Italy and document Dixon’s working band of the period — Alan Silva on double bass, Freddie Waits on drums, Steve Horenstein on tenor and baritone saxophone, Stephen Haynes on trumpet, and Arthur Brooks on trumpet. Dixon painted the front cover artwork himself.

November 1981 (1982, SN 1037/38): a double LP featuring a core group of Alan Silva and Mario Pavone on double bass and Laurence Cook on drums. The titles — “Penthesilea,” “The Second Son,” “Webern,” “Llaattiinnoo Suite” — reflect Dixon’s range of literary and musical reference. His own back cover painting is credited.

Thoughts (1987, SN 1111): a large ensemble record with William Parker and Mario Pavone on bass, Marco Eneidi on alto saxophone, John Buckingham on tuba, Peter Kowald on double bass, and Laurence Cook on drums. One track is titled “For Nelson And Winnie,” reflecting Dixon’s ongoing political engagement.

Son Of Sisyphus (1990, Soul Note 121 138-1): smaller unit with Mario Pavone, Laurence Cook, and John Buckingham, with Dixon painting both covers.

Vade Mecum (1994, Soul Note 121208-2): a significant late Soul Note record with Barry Guy and William Parker on double bass and Tony Oxley on percussion. Liner notes by Dixon and Ben Young. All compositions by Dixon.

Vade Mecum II (1996, Soul Note): followed the same trio format.

Late Career and the Exploding Star Orchestra Collaboration (2008)

Dixon’s final recording as a primary artist was Bill Dixon With Exploding Star Orchestra (2008, Thrill Jockey, THRILL 192), a collaboration with Rob Mazurek’s Chicago-based collective Exploding Star Orchestra. The record comprises three long pieces: two (“Entrances / One” and “Entrances / Two”) composed by Dixon, and one (“Constellations For Innerlight Projections”) composed by Mazurek as a video score. The ESO personnel on the date included Nicole Mitchell (flute, voice), Jeff Parker (guitar), Jason Ajemian (double bass), Matthew Lux (bass guitar), Josh Berman (cornet), Jim Baker (piano), Matt Bauder (tenor saxophone, bass clarinet), Jeb Bishop (trombone), Jason Adasiewicz (vibraphone, tubular bells), Damon Locks (voice), John Herndon and Mike Reed (2) (drums). Dixon played trumpet and contributed artwork; Mazurek played cornet and contributed artwork/video score. Recorded by Greg Norman.

Dixon died in June 2010, two years after this release.
