Coin Coin Chapter One: Gens de couleur libres

Artist: Matana Roberts Label: Constellation Records (CST079) Released: May 10, 2011 Format: 2xLP + CD (live in-studio recording) Runtime: 61:15

Inaugural chapter of Roberts’ planned twelve-part Coin Coin cycle. Named after Marie Thérèse Metoyer (c. 1742–1816), a freed slave in colonial Louisiana who became a prosperous landowner and worked to buy freedom for her family. “Coin Coin” was Metoyer’s nickname; it was also the nickname of Roberts’ grandfather. The narrative of the cycle launches in 1742. “Gens de couleur libres” (“free people of color”) refers to the free Black and mixed-race community in antebellum Louisiana.

Concept

Roberts describes the broader Coin Coin project as “panoramic sound quilting” — a method of quilting together free jazz, spirituals, blues, folk, musique concrète, minimalist drones, hymns, spoken word, and personal biography into a unified structure. On the project: “I’m most interested in impression and abstraction, as well as a certain cognitive dissonance.” And: “The entire project is a precarious love-hate-love letter to my birth country: a celebration of the human experience, American style.” [downbeat.com/news/detail/matana-roberts-coin-coin-sonic-patchwork-american-history]

What Reviewers Found Significant

Across reviews, the distinctive qualities identified:

  1. Scale and ambition of the cycle concept — a twelve-chapter arc through American history, compared by some to an “aural Finnegans Wake”
  2. Roberts’ vocal range — from primal, anguished screaming to soothing lullaby-like passages to stream-of-consciousness spoken narrative
  3. Genre synthesis — free jazz of the 1960s fire music lineage (Ayler, Shepp, Sanders) fused with spirituals, folk, and spoken word in a way that didn’t feel assembled but integrated
  4. Political-historical specificity — the engagement with the history of free Black people in Louisiana (and by extension African American history broadly) as primary content, not backdrop
  5. Accessibility of difficulty — multiple reviewers noted it crossed audiences (free jazz, experimental, avant-garde listeners) without pandering
