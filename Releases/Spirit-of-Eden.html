Spirit of Eden

Fourth studio album by Talk Talk. Released 1988 on Parlophone. Discogs master ID: 26436; primary release ID: 499352.

A radical departure from the band’s synth-pop origins, Spirit of Eden was assembled over extended studio sessions in near-total darkness with a rotating cast of jazz, classical, and folk musicians. The album’s six pieces were recorded improvisationally and then edited — many contributors recorded without hearing the other parts, some in isolation from the rest of the session. The result is a dense, atmospheric work drawing on free jazz, ambient, and chamber music that is widely regarded as a founding text of post-rock.

Recording

Recorded at Wessex Sound Studios (Wessex Studios), London. Sessions ran from 11 May 1987 to 11 March 1988 — approximately ten months. Engineer Phill Brown has described the sessions as taking place in near-total darkness, with candles, oil wheels, and strobes. Musicians were led into the studio blind and played to a backing track through headphones without knowing what other contributors had recorded. Hollis and Friese-Greene then edited the improvisations into the finished pieces. Many overdub sessions involved exhaustive experimentation (“hours of trying every possible overdub idea” — Phill Brown).

EMI Lawsuit

After delivery of the finished album, EMI deemed it commercially unacceptable and requested re-recording. Mark Hollis refused. The dispute went to litigation as EMI Records Limited v Hollis & Others.

The central legal argument concerned the contract’s option clause: EMI held that their three-month extension-notification window began only after they were “satisfied” the album was commercially satisfactory; Talk Talk argued it began upon completion of recording. Justice Andrew Morritt (High Court) initially ruled for EMI. The Court of Appeal overturned this decision on 23 May 1989, ruling in Talk Talk’s favour. The band were released from their EMI contract and subsequently signed to Polydor Records for Laughing Stock (1991).

Critical Reception

Contemporary

Initial critical response was divided. Rolling Stone’s Album Guide (1992) awarded it 1 star out of 5, with J.D. Considine calling it “pointless noodling.” NME gave it 7/10. Q gave it 4 stars.

Retrospective

Reassessment has been decisive. Pitchfork awarded it 10/10 in a January 2019 review by Jeremy D. Larson (describing it as “the great inhale of religious feeling”). AllMusic gives 5 stars. Mojo gives 5 stars. Uncut gives 10/10. The album is widely cited as a founding text of post-rock. The Rolling Stone 1-star verdict is now treated as a notable critical misjudgement of the era.

Production Notes

The album was produced by Mark Hollis and Tim Friese-Greene, who had collaborated since The Colour of Spring (1986). Recording took place over approximately a year in sessions that became increasingly experimental in method. Musicians were invited to play in the dark or with minimal direction, contributing improvisations that Hollis and Friese-Greene later edited into the finished pieces. In many cases, players recorded without knowing what the other contributors had played.

Phill Brown served as engineer — he had worked with Talk Talk on prior records.

Tim Friese-Greene played harmonium, piano, organ, and guitar in addition to his production role, and co-wrote all compositions with Hollis.

The album features a notably broad ensemble: jazz double bassist Danny Thompson (John Martyn, Pentangle), harmonica player Mark Feltham (Nine Below Zero), guitarist Robbie McIntosh (The Pretenders), percussionist Martin Ditcham, trumpeter Henry Lowther, violinist Nigel Kennedy, electronic instrument builder Hugh Davies (playing his invented “Shozygs”), and the Choir of Chelmsford Cathedral.

Cover artwork is an illustration by James Marsh, who created covers for the broader Talk Talk discography.
