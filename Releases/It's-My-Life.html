Background

It’s My Life is Talk Talk’s second album and the first to be produced by Tim Friese-Greene. Friese-Greene was initially brought in to assist with the remixing of the title single; the collaboration quickly deepened into a full production partnership and co-writing arrangement that would define the rest of Talk Talk’s existence. Simon Brenner, who had played keyboards on the debut The Party’s Over (1982), departed before these sessions; Friese-Greene effectively absorbed that role while also taking the producer’s chair.

The album sits at the transitional point of Talk Talk’s arc: more muscular and ambitious than the debut, still rooted in synth-pop form, but already showing the spatial instincts that would open up fully on The Colour of Spring (1986). Two of its singles — “It’s My Life” and “Such a Shame” — were the band’s biggest commercial hits.

Production Notes

Tim Friese-Greene’s first full production credit for Talk Talk. He had previously been brought in to remix the “It’s My Life” title single; EMI’s arrangement led to a full production role on the album. His dual credit here — Producer and Keyboards — sets the template for his remaining Talk Talk work, where he contributed as both a performer (piano, organ, harmonium) and a post-production architect.

“Dum Dum Girl” and the title track are the first Hollis / Friese-Greene co-writes to appear on record. The collaboration would deepen: by The Colour of Spring (1986) all songs carried both names.

“Call In The Night Boy” (B3) has a separate mixer credit — Walter Samuel (ID 341374) — distinct from the rest of the album. No engineer is credited on the Discogs release records; the name of the recording engineer for this album is not confirmed in the database.

Simon Brenner does not appear as a performer on the album but retains a co-writing credit on “Call In The Night Boy,” consistent with his departure from the lineup before recording began.

Recording

Recording took place in 1983. No studio name is specified in the Discogs records, the Wikipedia article, or any other accessible web source for this album. Secondary sources confirm the album was recorded in England. No tracking engineer is credited on any pressing or in any source consulted; the only engineer credit that appears is Walter Samuel, who mixed “Call In The Night Boy” (B3) only.

Critical Reception

Contemporary (1984)

UK press reception was sharply negative on release:

  • Melody Maker: described the album as “ugly”
  • NME: characterised it as “limp and whiney”
  • Record Mirror: “crushingly, excruciatingly average”

One NME reviewer (David Quantick) gave the album 4/10, calling it “horrible.” This domestic hostility contrasted with strong European commercial performance.

“Such a Shame” was a major continental European hit despite stalling at no. 49 in the UK. “It’s My Life” was the stronger UK performer (no. 46) and crossed to the US Hot 100 at no. 31. The album itself performed well in Europe — top 5 in Switzerland, Netherlands, Germany — while being a mid-chart entry in the UK (no. 35) and modest in the US (no. 42).

Retrospective

Contemporary dismissal gave way to later reappraisal, though It’s My Life is not typically the focus of Talk Talk retrospectives (which centre on Spirit of Eden and Laughing Stock). Classic Pop magazine ran a “Classic Album” feature on the record. The album was listed at no. 872 in Colin Larkin’s All Time Top 1000 Albums (2000 edition). Producer Tim Friese-Greene, speaking to Jim Irvin in 2006, offered a qualified self-assessment of the title single: “I don’t think It’s My Life is a crap song, but you certainly couldn’t say it’s one of the best… it’s nothing to get excited about.”

Steve Thompson Remix Note

The Steve Thompson credited on the “Dum Dum Girl” 12” single (tracks A1, A2, B2) is Steve Thompson the American remix engineer. He worked alongside Michael Barbiero as the Thompson & Barbiero team, who were active remixers for UK and US labels throughout the mid-1980s. This is not the same Steve Thompson as the UK producer Steve Thompson (of Thompson & Jay). The Barbiero pairing is confirmed by cross-referencing credits on other Thompson-remixed Talk Talk 12” pressings from the same period. The Discogs credit (ID 41642) does not name Barbiero explicitly on the “Dum Dum Girl” single, but the Thompson & Barbiero pairing is the standard configuration for this era.
