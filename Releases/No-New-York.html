No New York

Discogs Release ID: 399666 (master: 149570)
Label: Antilles (AN-7067), a division of Island Records
Year: 1978
Format: LP, Stereo
Producer: Brian Eno
Engineers: Kurt Munkacsi, Vishek Woszcyk; assistant Rod Hui
Recorded: Big Apple Studio, NYC, Spring 1978

The canonical document of New York’s No Wave movement. Four bands, four tracks each, across two sides. Brian Eno attended the “Bands at Artists Space” performances in SoHo (May 1978), selected four of the performing acts, and recorded them at Big Apple Studio that spring. Released on Antilles Records, an Island Records subsidiary. The record gave the movement international visibility and fixed the No Wave canon: to be on No New York is to be definitionally no wave; to have been at Artists Space and not on it is a footnote.

Historical Context: “Bands at Artists Space” (1978)

A five-night underground no wave festival organized by artists Michael Zwack and Robert Longo at Artists Space, SoHo. Ten bands performed in total:

Schedule: The final two nights featured DNA + The Contortions (Friday), then Mars + Teenage Jesus And The Jerks (Saturday).

How Eno got there: Eno was in New York to produce Talking Heads’ More Songs About Buildings and Food. Diego Cortez — curator, filmmaker, and co-founder (with Anya Phillips) of the Mudd Club — advised Eno to attend and document the scene.

The selection: One account attributes the final four to active maneuvering by the included bands to keep Chatham, Red Transistor, and Theoretical Girls off the LP (sourced from secondary accounts; not confirmed by Eno directly). What is documented is that Eno described the acts as “research bands” taking “deliberately extreme stances that define the edges of a piece of territory,” and told the New York Times: “I very much had the sense that this wasn’t going to last for long. It just seemed to me like one of those flames that burns very brightly for a short time and then it goes out.”

Recording approach: Minimal production. James Chance: “Done totally live in the studio, no separation between the instruments, no overdubs, just like a document.” Eno applied an echo to the guitar click to trigger compression, describing the result as sounding “like helicopter blades.”

Sleeve: Lyrics were printed inside the jacket in a way requiring buyers to physically tear apart the sleeve to read them.

The Contortions formed 1977 by James Chance (born James Siegfried, April 20, 1953, Milwaukee; died June 18, 2024, New York). Avant-funk colliding punk, free jazz, and James Brown; Chance physically provoked audiences during live shows. These four tracks predate their debut LP Buy (ZE/Island, 1979). Original lineup dissolved 1980: Pat Place → Bush Tetras; Harris, Christensen (and Scott) → The Raybeats. George Scott (2) (George Leonard Scott III) died August 5, 1980, heroin overdose; born Burlington, Iowa, October 16, 1953.

Teenage Jesus And The Jerks — NYC no wave group fronted by Lydia Lunch; formed 1976 as The Scabs. James Chance played saxophone with the group briefly before leaving to form The Contortions. First release: “Orphans” 7” (1978, Migraine). Other members at various times: Jim Sclavunos, Jay Korber, Paul Costuros, Thurston Moore. Gordon Stevenson — met Arto Lindsay at Eckerd College, St Petersburg, FL; bassist November 1977–June 1978; directed film Ecstatic Stigmatic (1980); died 1982, AIDS. Bradly Field — percussionist; died mid-1990s.

Mars (Discogs: Mars (4)) — founded December 1975 NYC as “China,” renamed Mars in mid-1977. The most tonally extreme of the four bands: dense, dissonant, unconventional time structures, minimal funk. Played approximately 30 gigs between January 1977 and December 1978, never leaving Manhattan; recorded roughly 32 minutes of music in total. Last show December 1978 (simultaneously recording their EP, released 1980 on Infidelity). Dissolved when Nancy Arlen chose to focus on visual art and clubs refused to book them. Post-Mars: Cunningham, Crane, and Burg continued as John Gavanti with Ikue Mori on drums. Sumner Crane (born 1946; real name Sumner Edward Schoenwetter) died April 15, 2003, cancer.

Sleeve credits Ikue Mori as “Ikue Ile.”

DNA (4) formed 1977. The name was taken from a Mars song. Arto Lindsay (born Arthur Morgan Lindsay, May 28, 1953, Richmond VA) — atonal, untrained guitar and yelped vocals; the signature DNA sound. Ikue Mori (born December 17, 1953, Tokyo) — arrived NYC 1977 for a visit, fell into the scene, stayed; no prior musical experience before DNA. After DNA disbanded, abandoned drums for drum machines, eventually laptop. Robin Lee Crutchfield (born September 8, 1952, Ohio) — after DNA, formed Dark Day (cold wave). Tim Wright (2) joined DNA on bass after Crutchfield’s departure. DNA releases: You & You 7” (1978, Medical Records), A Taste Of DNA (1981, Italian Records), DNA On DNA (2004, No More Records).

Brian Eno (born May 15, 1948, Woodbridge, Suffolk; full name Brian Peter George St John le Baptiste de la Salle Eno) — former Roxy Music member; by 1978 established as ambient pioneer (Discreet Music, Ambient 1) and art-rock producer (Bowie’s Berlin trilogy). No New York was a departure: documentarian rather than shaper, keeping sessions minimal and not imposing arrangements. Cover photograph is Eno’s own.

Steve Keister (Discogs ID 1868049) — New York sculptor, born 1949; known for geometric abstract “USOs” (unidentified suspended objects) in plywood, spandex, Plexiglas; Whitney Biennial 1981; solo shows at Gagosian and Blum Helman. Cover design co-credit alongside Eno connects him to the downtown scene’s visual art infrastructure.

Legacy

The album was mentioned on the back cover of The Fall’s Dragnet (1979). Thurston Moore and Byron Coley’s No Wave: Post-Punk. Underground. New York. 1976–1980 (Harry N. Abrams, 2008) uses the four No New York bands as the canonical boundary of the no wave scene — to be on the record is definitionally to be no wave. Artists citing the record as an influence include Sonic Youth (formed 1981; Mars specifically), Swans (formed 1982), Jon Spencer, the Rapture, and Yeah Yeah Yeahs.

Notes (web) — Communists research

Web search conducted June 2026 to identify the “Communists” band from the May 1978 Artists Space festival.

Confirmed: The name “Communists” appears consistently across secondary sources as one of the ten acts at the five-night festival (May 2–6, 1978), organized by Michael Zwack and Robert Longo. Sources include the Wikipedia articles for No New York, Artists Space, and No wave — all of which draw on the same underlying secondary literature.

Not found: Members/lineup, recordings (studio or live), contemporary press coverage (Village Voice, SoHo News, Trouser Press, New York Rocker), references in no-wave history books (Marc Masters 2007, Thurston Moore/Byron Coley 2008), oral history interviews, or fan wiki entries. The NYU Fales Artists Space Archive (mss_291) and the Diego Cortez No Wave Collection at Cornell (RMM08120) were checked via their online finding aids — neither mentions the Communists by name.

Assessment: The Communists appear to be one of the most thoroughly undocumented acts from the scene. Unlike Terminal (which similarly lacks its own pages but surfaces in some oral histories), the Communists left no recoverable web footprint beyond the festival billing that passed into Wikipedia. Physical archives — the Artists Space papers at NYU Fales, which include concert flyers and ephemera from this period — may contain a programme that names the members, but that requires on-site access.

Notes (web) — Band selection research

Web search conducted June 2026 to find primary sourcing on how the four bands were chosen for the album.

The core finding comes from Glenn Branca, interviewed by Marc Masters for The Wire (May 2018 — described as a previously unpublished conversation originally conducted for Masters’ 2007 book No Wave). Branca gives the clearest account of any participant:

“Yeah, that was what the gossip was. But the problem was it seems that Eno had personally met the people on the East Village side, and they convinced him that the idea of four bands was better.” “And very quickly the critics started to say that Theoretical Girls was the fifth band on No New York, because it was really strange that we weren’t included.” “What I always thought is that he actually might have seen us as more direct competition with him personally than any of the other bands.”

[source: The Wire, Glenn Branca interview with Marc Masters, thewire.co.uk/in-writing/interviews/glenn-branca-interview-with-marc-masters, as of 2026-06-26]

What this establishes:

  • The original plan circulating as gossip in the scene was ten bands (all festival performers). This matches a secondary claim in the weaselwalter.blogspot.com Contortions history, which states the album was “originally to feature two songs from ten bands.”
  • The East Village bands — the four who ended up on the record — had personal contact with Eno and lobbied for the four-band format. Branca frames this as “they convinced him,” not as pure Eno editorial judgment.
  • The SoHo contingent (Theoretical Girls, Tone Death / Gynecologists, Daily Life) did not have this access. Branca characterizes the divide as almost a “war between the two sides.”
  • Diego Cortez’s role: consistently described across all sources as the person who advised Eno to attend the festival and document the movement. No source found attributes the four-band selection specifically to Cortez’s curation. The selection geography (all four bands associated with the East Village side) is consistent with Cortez’s known curatorial orientation, but no direct statement from Cortez about the selection process has been found.

What this does not establish:

  • Which individuals among the East Village bands did the convincing (Arto Lindsay? Anya Phillips as Contortions manager? James Chance?). One AI-summarized search result claimed “Arto Lindsay and Anya convinced Brian Eno to change it to a showcase for just the East Village acts” — but the underlying source for this specific claim could not be identified or verified. It may be a paraphrase of the Branca quote (Arto Lindsay being the most prominent East Village figure) or a separate interview not located in this search.
  • Whether Eno’s own account of the selection (no primary Eno interview found discussing the four-band choice specifically) aligns with Branca’s version.
  • Whether “maneuvering” is a word used in any identified secondary source, or whether the secondary source flagging in the research queue is the Branca account itself (paraphrased) or something else.

Conflict note: The secondary source framing in the original research queue item (“one secondary source claims this”) is consistent with Branca’s Wire interview — but Branca’s account attributes the convincing to the East Village bands collectively, not to specific named individuals. No source using the word “maneuvering” was located.
